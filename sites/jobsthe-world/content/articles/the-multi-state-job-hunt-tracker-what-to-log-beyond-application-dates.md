A standard job-search spreadsheet has a company, a role, a date applied and a status. That works when every job is in your city. It breaks down when you're applying to remote-first employers in several states, provinces or countries.

The problem isn't the application. It's that "remote" hides a pile of conditions. Many of them only surface in the third interview or, worse, in the offer letter. By then you've spent weeks on a role you couldn't take.

This guide covers what to add to your tracker so you can rule roles in or out early. It's built for job seekers in the US, Canada, Australia and the UK.

## Why "remote" needs more columns

A remote job still has a legal and practical location. Your employer has to pay you, withhold tax, follow employment rules and, sometimes, check your professional credentials. All of that depends on where *you* sit, not where the company is headquartered.

Four things tend to decide whether a role works:

- **Time:** which hours you'll actually be online
- **Credentials:** whether your license or registration is valid where you live
- **Location:** whether "remote" really means "anywhere" or "near a hub"
- **Money:** how pay, tax and benefits change with your address

Each one deserves its own column, plus a "last verified" date, because postings and policies change.

## Start with the basics, then add four layers

Keep your core fields: company, role, link, date applied, contact, status and next step. Then add the layers below. You don't need all of them for every role. Fill them in progressively as a job moves from "interesting" to "interview" to "offer."

### Layer 1: Time zone and schedule

"Remote" often comes with "must overlap with Eastern hours." Log it the moment you see it.

Useful columns:

- **Team's home time zone** (where most of the team sits)
- **Required overlap window**, written in *your* local time (for example, "9:00–1:00 my time")
- **Meeting-heavy or async?** Note what the posting or interviewer says
- **On-call or shift expectations**
- **Your sleep and family cost:** a simple High/Medium/Low rating

Convert the overlap into your own time once, then record it. This sounds trivial. It prevents the classic mistake of agreeing to "9 to 5 Pacific" from Sydney or London without thinking through what that means.

Watch for daylight saving. In Australia, some states and territories change their clocks and others don't, so the gap between you and a US team can shift during the year. The US and Canada change on dates that differ from the UK's. Add a short note like "overlap shrinks in winter" so you aren't surprised twice a year.

Ask interviewers directly: "Which hours do people on this team typically overlap, and how flexible is that?" Log the answer word for word.

### Layer 2: Licensing, registration and credentials

If your field is regulated, location can make or break an application. Think nursing, teaching, counseling, social work, law, accounting, engineering, financial advice, real estate and some trades.

Rules vary by jurisdiction. In the US, many professions are licensed state by state. Some have multistate arrangements, such as the Nurse Licensure Compact for nurses, but participation depends on the state and the profession. Canadian regulated professions are generally overseen at the provincial or territorial level. Australia has a mix of national and state-based arrangements depending on the occupation. The UK has professional regulators, and some rules differ across its nations.

Columns to add:

- **Credential required for this role?** (Yes / No / Unsure)
- **Where must it be valid?** Your state, the employer's state, or the client's location
- **Do I already hold it?** Include expiry date
- **Path if not:** reciprocity, endorsement, a compact, or a new application
- **Estimated lead time and fees:** only after you've checked with the regulator
- **Who pays?** Employer, you, or split

For US occupations, CareerOneStop's licensed occupations tool is a reasonable starting point for seeing what's regulated in a given state. Treat it as a pointer, then confirm with the licensing board itself. Save the board's contact details and the date you checked.

A useful rule: if a role requires a license in a place where you don't hold one, ask the recruiter early whether the employer expects you to already have it or will support the process.

### Layer 3: Relocation and location expectations

Many "remote" listings carry fine print. Some are remote only within certain states. Some are hybrid with a few office days a quarter. Some are remote now but "expected to relocate to a hub within a year."

Log the exact wording, then categorize it:

| Category | What it means | What to log |
|---|---|---|
| Fully remote, any location | Work from anywhere the employer is set up to employ you | Confirm list of eligible regions |
| Remote, listed regions only | Only specific states, provinces or countries | Is your address on the list? |
| Remote with travel | Periodic on-site weeks or events | Frequency, who pays, notice period |
| Remote now, hub later | Future relocation expectation | Timeline, support offered, whether it's in writing |
| Hybrid | Regular office days | Commute realism, flexibility |

Also record:

- **Travel budget and policy:** flights, hotels, per diems, time-in-transit counted as work or not
- **Relocation support:** lump sum, repayment clauses, tax treatment
- **Your own flexibility:** are you willing to move, and by when?

If you might move during your search, add a "Valid from" column for your address. A job you can take from Denver may not work from Perth, and your tracker should show that.

### Layer 4: Tax and payroll flags

This is the layer people skip, and it's the one that can quietly change your take-home pay. Nothing here is tax or legal advice. The aim is to capture the questions so you can ask them of the employer and, where needed, a qualified tax professional.

**What to log for every role:**

- **Employment type:** employee or contractor (affects who handles tax, benefits and insurance)
- **Employer of record or payroll provider:** does the company hire directly where you live, or use a third party?
- **Your tax jurisdiction:** state, province, territory or country
- **Benefits that depend on location:** health coverage, retirement plans, leave entitlements
- **Currency and pay frequency**

**Region-specific reminders:**

- **US:** State income tax rules differ, and some states don't tax wages. Employers generally need to be set up for payroll in the state where you work. If you live in one state and the employer is in another, ask how withholding is handled. Some states apply special rules to remote workers employed by in-state companies, so don't assume your home address settles everything.
- **Canada:** Provincial tax generally follows where you live, and Quebec has its own filing system. Ask whether the employer is registered for payroll in your province. The Canada Revenue Agency is the authority to check for residency and filing questions.
- **Australia:** Income tax is federal, but employers may face state-based obligations such as payroll tax, which can influence where they're willing to hire. The Australian Taxation Office publishes guidance on tax residency if you're moving in or out of the country.
- **UK:** Income tax rules can differ for Scotland compared with the rest of the UK. GOV.UK has guidance on tax residence if you're working across borders.

For cross-border roles, such as a US company hiring a Canadian or a UK employer hiring an Australian, add a flag called **"International hire?"** and a follow-up task: ask whether they hire in your country, how, and who handles payroll and tax.

If a company says "we'll figure it out after the offer," treat that as a yellow flag. Note it.

## Add a "workability" score

With these layers filled in, you can rank roles by feasibility, not just appeal. A simple version:

1. Give each layer a status: **Clear**, **Needs checking** or **Blocker**
2. Add a final column: **Workable?** (Yes / Maybe / No)
3. Sort by that column before deciding where to put your interview-prep time

A role with a dream title and two blockers deserves less energy than a good role with four clear statuses.

## A suggested column list

Here's a compact set you can paste into a spreadsheet:

- Company / Role / Link
- Date applied / Status / Next step date
- Contact name and email
- Team home time zone / Required overlap (my time) / DST note
- Credential required / Where valid / Lead time
- Remote type / Eligible regions / Travel / Relocation expectation
- Employment type / Payroll setup / Tax questions open
- Salary range and currency / Benefits notes
- Workability (Yes / Maybe / No)
- Last verified date / Source of info

Use data validation (dropdowns) for the status fields and conditional formatting so blockers show in red. A tracker you can scan in ten seconds gets used. One you have to read doesn't.

## Questions to ask at each stage

Stage the questions so you don't overload an early call.

**Recruiter screen**
- Which locations can you hire in right now?
- What are the expected working hours and overlap?
- Is this role employee or contractor?

**Hiring-manager interview**
- How does the team handle meetings across time zones?
- Is any travel or relocation expected in the next 12–24 months?
- Does the role require a specific credential in a specific location?

**Offer stage**
- Who is the legal employer where I live?
- How is payroll and tax withholding set up for my location?
- Are benefits identical for my region, or different?
- Can the location and schedule expectations be written in the offer letter?

Record each answer, who gave it and the date. If something matters, ask for it in writing.

## Keep it current

Review the tracker weekly. Mark any role where the posting changed, a policy update arrived, or your own plans shifted. If you're moving, add a second "if I'm in..." column rather than rewriting rows.

The goal isn't a perfect spreadsheet. It's catching a mismatch in week one rather than week six. Dates tell you when you applied. These extra columns tell you whether the job can really be yours.
