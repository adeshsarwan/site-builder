You submitted a strong application, refreshed your inbox for two weeks, and got nothing. That silence feels personal, but it usually isn't. Remote roles tend to draw applicants from several countries at once, and a small hiring team can be buried in volume. A short, well-timed follow-up can pull your application back into view.

This guide covers when to follow up, who to contact, what to write, and when to stop.

## Why applications go quiet

Knowing the likely reasons helps you write a calmer, more useful message. Common causes include:

- **The role is still open and nobody has started reviewing.** Postings often stay live while the team is busy with other work.
- **The hiring manager is traveling, on leave, or in a different time zone** from the recruiter.
- **The budget or headcount is being reconsidered.** The job can be paused without the posting being removed.
- **Your application sits in an applicant tracking system** and nobody has opened that stage yet.
- **You were screened out.** Many employers never send a rejection, so this does happen, but it is only one of several possibilities.

Because you can't tell which applies, your follow-up should be easy to answer and should add something new. "Just checking in" adds nothing. A sentence of fresh, relevant value does.

## The follow-up timeline

Use the posting's own information first. If it says "we will contact shortlisted candidates within two weeks," treat that as your clock. If it gives no timeline, use the schedule below.

| When | Action | Channel | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day 0 (the day you apply) | Save the posting, note the contact details, and look for a named recruiter or hiring manager | Notes or spreadsheet | Prepare so you can follow up quickly |
| Days 1–3 | Optional: connect with the recruiter or a team member with a short note | LinkedIn | Put your name in front of a person |
| Days 7–10 | First follow-up if there is no stated timeline | Email to recruiter or hiring contact | Confirm receipt and restate fit |
| Days 14–17 | Second follow-up, adding something new | Email, or a different person on the team | Give a reason to reply |
| Days 21–28 | Final, graceful nudge | Email | Close the loop and keep the door open |
| After that | Stop chasing and move your energy to other applications | None | Protect your time and reputation |

Two adjustments matter:

- **If you have already interviewed,** shorten the gaps. Follow up a few business days after the date they said you would hear back.
- **If the employer is a government body, university, or large public organization,** expect slower hiring. Wait longer between messages and check their published process, since some run strict, formal timelines.

Count business days, not calendar days. A message sent on a weekend or national holiday will usually sit unread until Monday anyway.

## Whom to contact

Who you write to affects your reply rate more than how clever your message is. Try these in order:

1. **The named recruiter or talent coordinator.** If the posting lists one, start there. Their job is to answer exactly this question.
2. **The hiring manager.** Search the company's team page or professional profiles for the person who would lead the role. Write to them if there is no recruiter or if your first follow-up went unanswered.
3. **A person who referred you or who you know on the team.** A warm contact can nudge the process more gracefully than a cold email can.
4. **A general careers or jobs inbox.** Use this as a last resort when you can't find a named person. Put the exact job title and reference number in the subject line so it can be routed.

Avoid writing to several senior executives at once, and don't message the same person on three platforms in one day. One channel, one person, one clear ask is the rule.

### Finding an email address

Check the posting, the company's careers page, and the confirmation email you received after applying, since it often comes from a monitored address. If you can only find a profile on a professional networking site, send a brief message there instead of guessing at an address.

## Scripts you can copy and adapt

Keep every message short enough to read on a phone. Put the job title in the subject line, and replace the bracketed parts with your details.

### Script 1: First follow-up to a recruiter (days 7–10)

**Subject:** Application for [Job Title] – [Your Name]

Hi [Name],

I applied for the [Job Title] role on [date] and wanted to confirm that my application arrived. I'm especially interested because [one specific reason tied to the company or the work].

My background in [skill or result most relevant to the posting] seems closely matched to what you describe, and I'd be glad to share anything further that would help.

Thank you for your time.

[Name]
[Location and time zone] | [Link to portfolio or profile]

Including your time zone is a small remote-work courtesy that makes scheduling a call easier later.

### Script 2: Message to the hiring manager (when there is no recruiter)

**Subject:** [Job Title] – quick note from [Your Name]

Hi [Name],

I recently applied for the [Job Title] position on your team. I've followed [a project, product, or public work of the team], and your approach to [specific detail] stood out to me.

In my last role I [one concrete result, described without invented numbers], which relates directly to [a need in the posting]. If it would be useful, I can send a short example.

I understand you're likely reviewing many applications, so no rush. I just wanted to introduce myself.

Best,
[Name]

### Script 3: Second follow-up that adds something new (days 14–17)

**Subject:** Re: Application for [Job Title]

Hi [Name],

I wanted to follow up on my earlier note. Since then, I [completed a relevant course, published a piece of work, shipped a project, or read something relevant to the team's goals], and I've attached [a short sample or link].

I remain very interested in the role. Could you let me know whether the position is still open and what the next step might be?

Thank you,
[Name]

The "something new" is what separates a persuasive nudge from a repeat request. If you truly have nothing new, ask a thoughtful question about the role or the team instead.

### Script 4: Asking a contact for a nudge

Hi [Name],

I hope you're well. I applied for the [Job Title] role at [Company] on [date]. Given your experience there, would you be comfortable letting the hiring team know I've applied, or pointing me to the right person to contact? I completely understand if that isn't possible.

Happy to send a short summary you could forward.

Thanks so much,
[Name]

Giving the contact an easy out keeps the request low-pressure and protects the relationship.

### Script 5: Final graceful nudge (days 21–28)

**Subject:** Following up – [Job Title]

Hi [Name],

I'm checking in one last time on the [Job Title] role. If the position has been filled or the timeline has changed, I completely understand, and I'd appreciate knowing so I can plan accordingly.

I'd welcome the chance to stay in touch for future openings on your team.

Kind regards,
[Name]

This message often gets a reply because it makes answering easy. Even a short "we've moved on" gives you closure.

## Remote-first and regional details

**Time zones.** Send your email so it lands in the recipient's morning if you can. Most email tools let you schedule delivery. If the employer is in another country, check which holidays they observe before you decide a message has been ignored.

**Spelling and vocabulary.** Match the employer's conventions. In the UK, Australia, and Canada, "CV" and "resume" are both common depending on the employer, and spelling varies (organisation or organization, for example). Mirror the posting's wording.

**Work authorization.** If you are applying across borders, state your location and work eligibility plainly in the first follow-up, since this is often an early screening question for remote roles. Don't assume the team has noticed it on your application.

**Date formats.** Write dates out in words, such as "12 March" or "March 12," so that day-month and month-day conventions can't be confused.

**Contract and agency roles.** If a staffing agency posted the job, follow up with the agency recruiter, not the end client. They control the pipeline and may not have heard back themselves.

## Keep your tone confident, not pushy

A few habits keep you on the right side of the line:

- **Be brief.** Four to six sentences is enough.
- **Make one clear request,** such as confirming receipt or asking about timing.
- **Lead with value, not need.** Say what you can do for the team, not how long you've been waiting.
- **Avoid guilt and pressure,** such as "I haven't heard back and I'm confused."
- **Don't say you have other offers** unless it's true and you're ready to discuss it, and then only to a recruiter who is already engaged.
- **Say thank you** and mean it. Courtesy is remembered.

## When to stop

Three unanswered messages over about four weeks is a reasonable limit. After that, further messages risk hurting your standing. Log the application as "no response," and consider reapplying only if the company posts a new role that fits you well.

In the meantime, keep your search moving:

- Apply to other roles in parallel instead of waiting on one.
- Stay visible by commenting thoughtfully on the company's public updates.
- Ask for informal conversations with people in similar roles, which can open doors that applications don't.

## Track everything

A simple spreadsheet keeps follow-ups from slipping. Record the company, job title, date applied, contact name, each message you sent, and the next planned follow-up date. When a reply arrives, you'll know instantly what you said and when.

Following up is a normal part of professional communication. Send a clear, courteous message at the right time to the right person, then let it go and keep applying.
