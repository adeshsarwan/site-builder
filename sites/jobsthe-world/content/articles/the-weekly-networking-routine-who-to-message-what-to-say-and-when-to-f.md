Most job seekers treat networking like a rescue mission. They ignore it for weeks, panic after a rejection, send ten awkward messages in one evening, then stop when nobody replies.

A routine fixes that. It turns networking into a small, boring habit that sits next to your applications instead of competing with them. This guide gives you a weekly cadence, wording you can adapt, and follow-up timing you can stick to. It's built for remote-first searches, where you're often reaching people in other cities, countries and time zones.

## The idea: networking is a pipeline, not a favor

Applications go into a queue where a stranger decides your fate. Networking gives a human being a reason to look at your application, or tells you about a role before it's widely advertised.

You don't need to be charming or "work a room." You need a steady flow of short, specific, low-pressure conversations. Aim for **five to eight new outreach messages a week** and **two or three follow-ups**. That's roughly 90 minutes of total effort. If your week is already full, drop to three messages. A small cadence you keep beats a big one you abandon.

## Who to message: build a rotating list of five groups

Don't start with senior executives or people you've never heard of. Start with people who have a reason to answer. Rotate through these groups so you never run dry:

1. **Warm contacts.** Former colleagues, managers, classmates, clients, and people you've worked with in volunteer or community groups. These are your fastest replies.
2. **Second-degree connections at target companies.** Someone who knows someone, or who shares a past employer or school with you.
3. **People in the role you want.** Not hiring managers, but people doing the job now. They can tell you what the work is really like.
4. **People who recently joined a company you like.** Newer employees often remember the hiring process and tend to be open to a chat.
5. **Community members.** People active in professional associations, Slack or Discord groups, open-source projects, alumni networks, or industry meetups who post or answer questions in your field.

For each target employer, try to identify two or three names across these groups. Write them in a simple tracker (a spreadsheet is fine) with columns for name, company, how you know them, date contacted, next follow-up date and status.

### A note on remote-first searches

When the company is distributed, "local" matters less, but time zones and hiring regions matter a lot. Before you ask anyone about a role, check where the company can legally employ you. Some remote roles are limited to certain US states, to Canada only, to Australia and New Zealand time zones, or to the UK and EU. If you're not eligible, a referral won't fix that, so ask about it early and politely.

## The weekly cadence

Here's a schedule you can copy. Shift the days to suit your life; keep the order.

| Day | Task | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Pick 5–8 people for the week and add them to your tracker | 20 min |
| Tuesday | Send first messages to half of them | 20 min |
| Wednesday | Apply to roles; send any follow-ups that are due | 30 min plus applications |
| Thursday | Send first messages to the rest | 20 min |
| Friday | Review replies, book calls, thank people, update the tracker | 20 min |

Two rules keep this working. First, send first messages in the recipient's working hours, not yours. A message that lands mid-morning in their time zone is more likely to get read than one buried overnight. Second, never message and apply in the same session without connecting them. If you applied to a role, say so in the message to someone at that company.

## What to say: first-message wording

Good first messages share four traits: they're short, they say why *this* person, they make a small ask, and they make it easy to say no. Avoid asking for a job, a referral or "a quick coffee" from someone you've never spoken to. Ask for something they can answer in a few minutes.

### Warm contact (former colleague)

> Hi Sam, hope you're well. I'm looking for my next role in [function], ideally remote, and I'm focusing on [type of company or industry]. I noticed you're at [Company]. Would you be open to a 15-minute call, or even a few lines by message, on what the team is like? Happy to return the favor in any way I can.

### Cold message to someone in your target role

> Hi Priya, I'm a [your role] exploring a move into [target role] and your path from [previous area] to [current role] at [Company] caught my eye. If you have five minutes, I'd love to ask one question: what skill made the biggest difference in your first six months? No worries at all if you're busy.

### Someone who recently joined a company

> Hi Daniel, congratulations on the new role at [Company]. I'm interested in the [team or role] and I'm curious how the move to a distributed team has gone. Would you be open to sharing one thing you wish you'd known before joining? Thanks either way.

### When you've already applied

> Hi Maria, I applied this week for the [role title] at [Company] and wanted to introduce myself. My background is in [one-line relevant strength]. If you're open to it, could you tell me what the team values most in this role? I don't want to put you on the spot, so please ignore this if it isn't appropriate.

### Why these work

- They name the reason for contacting that specific person.
- The ask is small and has an obvious end point.
- They don't hide the fact that you're job hunting.
- They give permission to decline, which makes people more likely to help.

Keep any message to roughly three to five sentences. Put your key credential in one line and leave your full résumé out unless asked.

## Follow-up timing

Most people simply forget to reply, so a follow-up is normal, not pushy. Use this sequence and then stop:

- **Day 0:** First message.
- **Day 5–7:** One brief follow-up if there's been no reply.
- **Day 14–21 after that:** Optionally, one final light-touch note, perhaps with something useful (an article relevant to their work, or congratulations on a company announcement).
- **After that:** Close the loop in your tracker and move on. You can reconnect months later if you have news.

### A follow-up that doesn't nag

> Hi Priya, just floating this back up in case it got buried. No pressure at all. If a quick note is easier than a call, even one line would be really helpful. Thanks!

### After a conversation

Send a thank-you within 24 hours. Mention one specific thing you learned and what you plan to do with it:

> Thanks so much for your time today, Daniel. Your point about writing project updates asynchronously was really useful, and I'm going to add an example to my portfolio. I'll let you know how the application goes.

Then follow up again in two to three weeks with a brief update, such as "I applied for the role you mentioned" or "I took your advice about X." This is how a single chat turns into a relationship, and it's the step most job seekers skip.

### If someone offers to refer you

Make it easy. Send the job link, the exact title, a three-line summary of why you fit, and your résumé. Apply through the official process too, and tell them when you've done so. Update them after any interview, whatever the outcome.

## Fit it around applications

Networking and applying work best when they feed each other.

- **Before applying:** Check whether you have a first- or second-degree contact at the company. If so, message before or on the day you apply.
- **After applying:** Message someone relevant within a few days, referencing your application.
- **When you're rejected:** Don't delete the contact. Thank the person who helped and ask whether they know of similar teams.
- **When you're swamped:** Cut applications to the strongest-fit roles for the week, but keep at least three outreach messages going.

Quality matters more than volume on both sides. A tailored application with one real conversation behind it will usually serve you better than several rushed ones.

## Keep it sustainable

- **Give before you ask.** Congratulate people, share a useful resource, answer a question in a community, or make an introduction. A minute spent helping builds goodwill.
- **Be honest and specific.** Don't exaggerate your connection to someone, and don't imply a referral you haven't been offered.
- **Respect privacy and boundaries.** Use professional channels, keep messages work-related, and accept a non-reply gracefully.
- **Track what works.** After a month, look at which groups replied most and which wording got a response. Adjust one thing at a time.
- **Expect silence.** A cold message with no reply doesn't say anything about your worth. Plenty of people are busy, on leave or just not checking that platform.

## Your first week, step by step

1. Create a tracker with the columns listed above.
2. List 15 names across the five groups; choose five to send this week.
3. Adapt one script from above and personalize the first line for each person.
4. Schedule the follow-up date in your calendar when you send each message.
5. Put a 20-minute Friday review in your diary.

Repeat for four weeks before judging results. By then you'll have a working list, a few conversations, and a habit that will keep supporting your search long after you land the role.
