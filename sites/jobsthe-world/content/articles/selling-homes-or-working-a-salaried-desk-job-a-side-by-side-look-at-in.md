Real estate looks like a shortcut. The licensing course is short compared with a degree, there's no pay ceiling, and every listing in your feed suggests someone else is doing well. A salaried desk job looks slower but safer. Both pictures are partly true and partly misleading.

This guide compares the two paths on three things that matter when you're deciding: how your income behaves, what it costs to start, and where each path leads in five to ten years. It also covers a point that matters if you're remote-first: selling homes is a local, in-person business.

## The core difference: who carries the risk

In a salaried job, your employer carries most of the risk. They find clients, pay for software and equipment, and pay you on a schedule whether this month was good or bad.

In residential sales, you carry that risk. Most agents work as independent contractors under a licensed brokerage. They are paid commission only when a deal closes, and they cover many of their own business costs. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Outlook Handbook describes real estate brokers and sales agents this way, with pay usually based on commission and many workers self-employed. It also publishes current median pay, which is worth checking yourself. A median is a midpoint, so it hides the gap between the busiest agents and the many who earn little.

Keep that gap in mind. "Uncapped" describes the best case. It says nothing about the typical case.

## Income risk, step by step

### The gap before the first paycheck

Commission arrives at closing, not when you meet a client. A realistic first sale involves finding a buyer or seller, working with them for weeks or months, then waiting through the contract-to-close period. Many new agents go a long stretch with no income at all, and they spend money throughout.

### Seasonality and market shifts

Housing activity moves with interest rates, local supply and the seasons. A salaried analyst or project coordinator might see a hiring freeze. An agent sees buyers disappear and deals fall through. You can't predict either, but only one of them hits your next paycheck directly.

### A hypothetical to make it concrete

These numbers are illustrative, not typical. Say you help a buyer purchase a $400,000 home and your side of the deal earns 2.5%. That's $10,000. If your brokerage keeps 30%, you receive $7,000. From that you cover marketing, association and listing-service dues, insurance, transport and your own taxes. Whether the result is good depends on how many deals you close in a year and what your costs are.

Run this on paper with your local prices, your brokerage's actual split and your honest estimate of deals in year one. Most people find the first-year number smaller than expected.

## Startup costs: what you'll actually pay for

Costs vary by state, province and country, so use these as a checklist and get real quotes.

- **Pre-licensing education:** required in most US states and in Canadian provinces and Australian states, with rules set locally.
- **Exam and application fees:** plus fingerprinting or background checks in many places.
- **Licensing or registration renewals:** ongoing, often with continuing education.
- **Brokerage costs:** some charge monthly desk or technology fees, transaction fees, or take a larger share of early commissions in exchange for training.
- **Association and listing-service dues:** often needed to access listings and show properties.
- **Errors and omissions insurance:** sometimes required by the brokerage or the regulator.
- **Marketing and tools:** a website, CRM, photography, signs, business cards, lead sources.
- **Transport:** you'll drive to showings, so factor in fuel and wear.
- **Living-expense runway:** the biggest cost, and the one most people forget.

A salaried path has costs too, such as a certificate, a degree, or unpaid time spent job hunting. But once you're hired, the employer normally covers tools, software and often training.

## Rules differ by country

If you're comparing across the US, Canada, Australia and the UK, don't assume the licensing process is the same.

- **United States:** licensing is set state by state. Requirements for hours, exams and reciprocity vary, so check your state's real estate commission.
- **Canada:** regulated by province. Ontario, British Columbia and others each have their own regulator, courses and rules for working under a brokerage.
- **Australia:** regulated by state or territory, with different entry qualifications and licence categories (for example, sales representative versus agent).
- **United Kingdom:** there is no general licensing requirement to be an estate agent. But agencies generally must register for anti-money-laundering supervision and join a government-approved redress scheme. Employers often set their own training expectations. Check current government guidance before assuming that "no licence" means "no requirements."

In every country, self-employed income brings tax duties that an employer would otherwise handle: self-employment tax in the US, sole-trader or incorporated-business obligations in Canada, ABN and superannuation choices in Australia, and Self Assessment and National Insurance in the UK. A local accountant is worth paying for before your first commission.

## Long-term growth

### Real estate

Growth in real estate comes from reputation and repeat business. Past clients refer friends, and a good local name becomes an asset. Some agents later build teams, become brokers, specialise in commercial or property management, or move into investing. None of that happens automatically. It takes consistent lead generation, which means you're always selling yourself.

The risk is plateauing. Without a pipeline, income can stall, and there's no employer ladder to climb.

### Salaried desk jobs

Salaried growth is more structured: titles, pay bands, mentoring and the chance to move laterally. Remote-first roles in operations, customer success, finance, marketing, analytics or project management can build skills that transfer between employers. The risk is a slower ceiling and dependence on one company's decisions. Your growth can also stall if you stay too long without building new skills.

## The remote-first problem

This is the part that often gets overlooked. Real estate is a local, relationship-driven job. You'll need to know neighborhoods, attend showings, meet clients and work within your regional listing system. Marketing and paperwork can be done online, but the core work isn't location-independent. If you plan to travel or relocate often, this path is harder.

If what you want is real estate exposure without the commission risk, look at salaried or hourly roles that support agents and brokerages:

- Transaction coordinator
- Inside sales or lead-qualifying roles
- Listing or marketing coordinator
- Property-management administration
- Mortgage or title-company support roles

Some can be done remotely, though availability varies. They pay less than top agents but more predictably, and you'll find out whether you like the industry before taking on licensing and dues.

## A practical test before you decide

1. **Calculate your monthly baseline.** Rent or mortgage, food, insurance, debt, transport, savings. This is the minimum you must cover.
2. **Build a runway.** Many advisers suggest setting aside several months of baseline expenses. If you can't cover roughly six to twelve months of both living costs and business costs, consider starting part-time while employed.
3. **Interview three working agents.** Pick ones who've been licensed for different lengths of time, and ask about their first-year income, costs and how they get clients. Ask what the brokerage actually provides.
4. **Compare brokerages.** Ask for the full fee schedule in writing: split, caps, desk fees, technology fees, training.
5. **Check your sales temperament.** Prospecting daily, handling rejection and working evenings and weekends are part of the job.
6. **Set a decision point.** For example: "If I haven't closed X deals by month Y, I return to salaried work." Deciding that in advance keeps sunk costs from deciding for you.

## Which path fits whom

Real estate tends to suit people who already have a local network, enjoy outgoing sales work, tolerate irregular income and have savings or a partner's steady income. It's less suited to someone who needs predictable cash flow next quarter or wants to work from anywhere.

A salaried desk job suits people who value predictable income, benefits and structured development, or who need location flexibility. It's weaker if you want income tied directly to your effort, though some salaried roles include commission or bonuses.

Many people don't have to choose all at once. You can hold a salaried job, complete the licensing course and test the market on weekends, provided your employer allows it. That costs you evenings but protects your income while you learn what the work is really like.

## Bottom line

Don't compare real estate's best-case commission with a salaried job's base pay. Compare your realistic first-year net income after costs and taxes against a salary, then weigh the stability and flexibility each gives you. If the numbers still favor real estate and you've got the runway, the risk is a calculated one rather than a gamble.
