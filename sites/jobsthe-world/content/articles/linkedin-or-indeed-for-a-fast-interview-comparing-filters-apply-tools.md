Most job seekers don't have a platform problem. They have an attention problem. You have perhaps an hour a day, and every site wants to be the place you spend it.

This guide compares LinkedIn and Indeed on what you can actually use: filters, apply tools, costs and the claims each makes about getting you seen. It's written for remote-first searchers in the US, Canada, Australia and the UK. Both platforms change their features often, so treat the details below as things to confirm on your own screen, not as permanent facts.

## The short answer

If you want the fastest route to a first conversation, don't pick one platform for everything. Use each for what it does best:

- **Indeed** is usually the better *volume and screening* tool: a large pool of listings, straightforward free access, and pay and job-type filters.
- **LinkedIn** is usually the better *human contact* tool: you can often see who posted a role, who works at the company and who might know the hiring manager.

Interviews tend to come from a person noticing you, not from the platform itself. So the practical split is Indeed to find and screen roles, LinkedIn to find a person attached to the roles you care about.

## What each platform costs you

Both platforms let you search and apply for free. The difference is what they try to sell you next.

**LinkedIn** offers paid Premium tiers for job seekers. The features have included things like extra messaging credits to contact people you aren't connected to, and extra insight into a listing, such as how you compare with other applicants. Prices vary by country and plan, so check the current figure in your own currency before you decide. Free trials are sometimes offered. If you try one, set a reminder to cancel before it renews.

**Indeed** is free for job seekers to search, apply and upload a resume. Employers pay to promote jobs and to use recruiting tools. That means some listings at the top of your results are paid placements, and they're labeled as sponsored. A sponsored listing isn't a worse job, but it is there because someone paid, not because it suits you.

**A practical rule:** don't pay for anything until you've used the free version seriously for two weeks. If your problem is that nobody replies, a subscription won't fix a weak resume or a mismatched search.

## Filters: where the real time savings are

Filters decide how much junk you wade through. Here's a side-by-side of the ones that matter most for a remote-first search.

| What you need | LinkedIn | Indeed |
|---|---|---|
| Remote-only results | Work-type filter (remote, hybrid, on-site) | Remote option in the location and filter menus |
| Freshness | Date-posted filter | Date-posted filter |
| Pay | Salary filter on some plans and regions; depends on listings showing pay | Pay filter; depends on listings showing pay |
| Seniority | Experience-level filter | Experience-level filter |
| Job type (contract, part-time) | Yes | Yes |
| Skip the long application | "Easy Apply" filter | Filter or label for quick apply options |
| Company insight | Employee profiles and connections | Company pages and employee reviews |

Filter names and locations shift between versions, so use this as a checklist of what to look for, not a map of buttons.

### Three filter habits that save time

1. **Sort by date, and filter to the past day or week.** Fresh listings are more likely to still be open and to still be reviewing applications.
2. **Save the search and turn on alerts.** Set up a handful of specific alerts, such as your exact title plus a close variant, and let the platform bring listings to you.
3. **Don't trust the word "remote" on its own.** A remote listing may require you to live in a specific country, state, province or time zone, or to be near an office a few days a month. Read the location line and the first paragraph of the description before you do anything else.

That third point matters a lot across four countries. A US-based "remote" role may not be open to candidates in Canada, Australia or the UK for payroll or legal reasons. The reverse is also true. Filter to your own country first, then check the fine print.

## Apply tools: quick isn't the same as effective

Both platforms offer one-click or short-form applications.

**LinkedIn's Easy Apply** sends your profile details and resume to the employer through LinkedIn. It's fast, which is exactly why it's popular and why those listings can attract a lot of applicants. Many LinkedIn listings also show an applicant count or similar signal. Use it as a rough guide to competition, not as a verdict.

**Indeed's quick apply** works much the same way using your Indeed profile or uploaded resume. Some listings send you off to the employer's own site instead.

Neither tool is a trick. Here is how to use them well:

- **Use quick apply for roles that are a close match** and where a second pass at tailoring wouldn't change much.
- **Go to the employer's own application page for roles you really want.** It usually lets you add a cover note or answer role-specific questions.
- **Tailor the top third of your resume.** Mirror the job's own terms for your title, tools and results, but only where they are true.
- **Answer screening questions honestly and carefully.** A wrong answer to a work-authorization or location question can screen you out automatically.

If an application takes more than 20 minutes for a role that isn't a strong fit, skip it.

## Visibility claims: what to take with a pinch of salt

Both platforms say, in various ways, that you'll be easier for employers to find. Some of that is real. Here's how to read the claims.

**LinkedIn's "Open to Work" setting.** You can signal that you're looking, and you can choose whether the signal is visible to everyone or only to recruiters. Choose based on your situation. If your current employer might see a public banner, recruiter-only is the safer option. Either way, the setting helps only if your profile contains the words a recruiter would search for, such as your job title, skills and location.

**Indeed's resume visibility.** If you upload a resume, you can usually choose whether employers can find it. Making it searchable can lead to inbound messages. It also means your contact details are more widely available, so use a dedicated email address and leave off your home street address.

**"Actively recruiting" and similar labels.** Treat these as hints, not guarantees. A label doesn't tell you the employer will read your application this week.

**Premium or boosted placement.** Be skeptical of any claim that paying will move you to the front of the line. Ask what exactly is being offered and whether you can verify it. If you can't, don't pay for it.

The honest position is this: no platform can promise you an interview, and nothing in a marketing page proves that a feature raises your chances. Run your own test instead.

## A 14-day test to decide where your time goes

Spend two weeks tracking real results. You only need a simple spreadsheet.

**Setup (one session)**
- Make your LinkedIn headline and Indeed resume say the same thing: target title, key skills, and "remote" with your country.
- Create three saved searches on each platform.
- Create a free email address used only for the job search.

**Daily routine (about 45 minutes)**
- 10 minutes: review new alerts on both platforms and discard anything with the wrong location or pay.
- 20 minutes: apply to two or three strong matches, with a tailored resume top section.
- 10 minutes: on LinkedIn, find a recruiter, hiring manager or team member for one of those roles and send a short, specific message.
- 5 minutes: log what you did.

**What to log**
- Platform and listing source
- Whether you used quick apply or the employer's site
- Date applied
- Any reply, viewed signal or interview invitation

After 14 days, compare. If most of your replies came from Indeed applications, shift more time there. If your replies came from LinkedIn messages to people, put more effort into outreach. With fewer than a dozen applications on each side, treat the pattern as a hint and keep testing.

### A short, usable outreach message

Keep it to three or four sentences:

> Hi [Name], I applied for the [Role] position today. I've spent the last [X years] doing [relevant work], most recently [one specific result]. If it's useful, I'm happy to send a short example. Thanks for your time.

Don't ask for a job in the first message. Show that you're a specific, relevant applicant.

## Add one free local source

Neither platform is the full market. A government-run job board is free and often lists roles you won't see elsewhere, and it carries less risk of paid placements. Add one to your routine:

- **US:** CareerOneStop, run by the US Department of Labor, has a job finder and tools for tailoring a search.
- **Canada:** Job Bank, run by the Government of Canada.
- **Australia:** the Workforce Australia job search.
- **UK:** the government's Find a job service.

Check these once or twice a week rather than daily.

## Protect yourself from fake remote jobs

Remote listings attract scams on every platform. Warning signs include being asked to pay for equipment or training upfront, a recruiter who will only chat through a messaging app, vague duties with unusually high pay, or any request for bank details before a formal offer. The US Federal Trade Commission publishes guidance on job scams, and Australia's Scamwatch offers similar advice. Verify the company through its own website, and contact it using details you found yourself.

## Bottom line

Use Indeed as your main search engine and filter-driven screening tool, use LinkedIn as your tool for reaching real people, and pay for nothing until your free routine has run for two weeks. The platform that deserves your daily effort is the one that produces replies for *you*, and a short log will tell you which that is.
