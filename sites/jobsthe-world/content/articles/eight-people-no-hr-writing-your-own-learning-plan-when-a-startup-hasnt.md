You accepted the offer, your laptop arrived (or didn't), and the welcome message was a Slack emoji. At a company of eight, that is normal. Nobody is neglecting you on purpose. Nobody owns onboarding because nobody has had time to build it.

That gap is also an opening. At a larger company, onboarding is a conveyor belt. At a startup, the person who builds a clear picture of how things work, and writes it down, becomes useful quickly. This guide gives you a plan to run on your own, from day one to day 90.

## First, reframe the job: you are your own onboarding manager

A formal program tells you what to learn and when. Without one, three things tend to go wrong:

- You absorb information at random, so gaps show up in week eight.
- You wait to be told what to do, and the founders assume you're fine.
- You learn a lot but leave no trace, so the next hire starts from zero too.

Your plan needs to answer three questions: **who do I need to know, what do I need to understand, and what should I write down?** Everything below is built around those.

## Days 1–5: Get unblocked and set the ground rules

Keep the first week practical. You aren't trying to be impressive yet. You're trying to stop being blocked.

### Sort out access and admin

Make a short checklist and work through it with whoever handles operations, which is often a founder or an office-manager-type generalist:

- Accounts and tools: email, chat, project tracker, shared drive, code repository or CRM, password manager.
- Payroll and tax paperwork for your country. The forms differ (for example, W-4 and I-9 in the US, a TD1 in Canada, a tax file number declaration in Australia, starter details or a P45 in the UK), so ask who processes them and confirm nothing is outstanding.
- Equipment, expenses and how to submit them.
- Time off: how to request it, and whether anyone tracks it.

If something is missing, say so in writing and in one place, not scattered across DMs.

### Ask your manager five questions

Book 45 minutes with your manager, or the founder you report to, and ask:

1. What does a good first 30 days look like to you?
2. What is the one outcome you most want from my role by day 90?
3. How do you prefer updates: written, async video, a weekly call?
4. What are working hours and response expectations across our time zones?
5. Who should I talk to first, and who will be annoyed if I don't?

Write down the answers and send them back as a short summary: "Here's what I understood. Correct me if I'm off." That email is your first draft of expectations, and it costs nothing.

## Build your people map

In a team this small, relationships are your onboarding program. Aim to have a one-to-one conversation with every colleague in your first three weeks. With eight people, that is realistic.

### Who to meet

| Person or group | What to learn from them |
|---|---|
| Founder or CEO | Why the company exists, what the next six months depend on, what keeps them up at night |
| Your manager | Priorities, how they judge good work, how feedback happens |
| Closest teammate | Daily workflow, unwritten rules, which tools are really used |
| Someone from the revenue or customer side | Who the customers are, what they complain about, what they praise |
| Whoever builds the product | What is stable, what is fragile, what's planned |
| Anyone who handles money or operations | Budget limits, approvals, contracts, payroll |
| An advisor or contractor, if one exists | History and context the full-time team may have forgotten |

### Questions that work in any one-to-one

Use the same core set so you can compare answers later:

- What does your week actually look like?
- What do you wish a new person understood sooner?
- What's one thing we do that isn't written down anywhere but really matters?
- Where do you see us getting stuck?
- Who else should I talk to?

Notice where answers differ. If the founder says the priority is growth and the engineer says it's stability, you've just found a tension that will shape your work. Don't pick a side. Note it and raise it with your manager as a question.

### Remote-specific habits

Because nobody will bump into you at a coffee machine, you need to create the contact:

- Send a short intro message in the main channel with your background, what you'll be working on, and what you'd like help with.
- Schedule 25-minute calls rather than "quick chats" that never happen.
- Say thanks publicly when someone helps. It builds goodwill fast in a small group.
- Learn each person's time zone and working pattern and put them in your notes.

## What to learn: four layers

Without a curriculum, make your own. Work through these layers in order.

### 1. The business

Understand how the company makes money, who pays, and what it costs to serve them. Ask for the pitch deck, any investor update that can be shared, pricing pages and a few real customer conversations or support tickets. You don't need financial detail, but you should be able to explain the business to a friend in three sentences.

### 2. The product and customers

Use the product as a customer would. Keep a running list of confusing moments. These fresh-eyes observations are valuable in your first month and fade quickly, so write them down before you stop noticing.

### 3. The way work happens

Find out how decisions are made, where work is tracked, and what "done" means. Look for the real process, not the one in a stale document. Watch which channels get fast replies and which are ignored.

### 4. Your own craft, in this context

Whatever your role, learn how it applies here. A marketer should learn current channels and past experiments. A developer should read the codebase and recent pull requests. A customer success hire should read past tickets. Ask for the last three examples of work that someone was proud of, then ask why.

## What to document: build the onboarding you didn't get

This is the part most people skip and the part that pays off most. Create one shared document, ideally in the company's wiki or drive, titled something like "New Starter Notes." Keep it plain.

Start with these sections:

- **Access and tools:** what each tool is for and who administers it.
- **People:** who does what, time zones, how they like to be contacted.
- **Glossary:** internal terms, product names, acronyms.
- **How we work:** meeting rhythms, approval paths, where decisions get recorded.
- **Open questions:** things you haven't been able to answer yet, with an owner next to each.
- **Fixes I made:** anything you corrected or clarified, with the date.

Two rules keep this useful. First, write it as you learn, not in a batch at the end. Second, share it early and ask colleagues to correct mistakes. Corrections are a cheap way to learn what people care about, and they make the document trusted instead of "something the new person made."

Keep a second, private log too: a brief weekly note of what you did, what you learned, and what confused you. It will help you at your 90-day review, and in any future conversation about your pay or role.

## The 30-60-90 plan

Adapt the details to your role, but keep the shape.

| Window | Focus | Evidence you're on track |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–30 | Learn: access, people, business basics, first small deliverable | Every colleague met, starter doc shared, one small piece of work shipped |
| Days 31–60 | Contribute: own a defined piece of work, spot patterns | A project with a clear owner (you), regular written updates, one process improvement proposed |
| Days 61–90 | Own and propose: take on a recurring responsibility and suggest next priorities | A review conversation held, documented results, a draft plan for the next quarter |

### Days 1–30: learn and ship something small

Aim for one small, finished piece of work in the first month, even if it's minor. Finishing teaches you the real approval process faster than any explanation. Send a short weekly update, by email or in a channel, covering what you did, what you're doing next, and where you need help.

### Days 31–60: take ownership of something

Ask your manager to assign you a clearly bounded area. Then define what success looks like in writing. Small teams often leave goals vague because everyone is busy, so a concrete suggestion from you is usually welcomed. Revisit your early observations list and pick one or two issues worth fixing.

### Days 61–90: review and propose

Book a formal conversation with your manager around day 60 to 75. Bring your original expectations summary, your log of work, and what you think the next 90 days should hold. Ask directly: "What should I do more of, less of, or differently?" Then update your documentation and plan accordingly.

## Common traps

- **Waiting for permission to start.** In a team of eight, nobody is checking whether you're allowed to ask questions.
- **Over-documenting trivia.** Write what the next person would need on day one, not a diary.
- **Becoming the unofficial HR department.** Documenting is great. If you end up running everyone's admin, negotiate that explicitly as part of your role.
- **Staying invisible.** Remote work hides effort. Short, regular written updates make your progress visible without self-promotion.
- **Ignoring legal and pay basics.** If anything about your contract, hours or pay seems unclear, ask in writing early. If it's serious, check your country's official employment guidance or an employment adviser rather than relying on colleagues' guesses.

## A final check at day 90

Ask yourself:

- Could a new hire become productive faster because of what I wrote?
- Do I know who to go to for each type of problem?
- Does my manager know what I've delivered and what I want next?
- Do I understand how the company makes money and what could break it?

If the answers are mostly yes, you've done more than finish onboarding. You've built the first version of it, and that's a strong thing to be able to say in any future review or interview.
