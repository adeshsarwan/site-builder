You got the promotion. The title is new, the job description is thin, and the person who hired you said something like "just keep the team moving." That's a common starting point, and it's fixable.

This guide covers three things: what to settle before you start, how to spend your first week, and how to turn loose goals into results someone else can verify. It's written for managers on remote-first teams, where nobody sees you work and good habits have to be deliberate.

## Why the first 90 days feel so vague

Most people are promoted because they were strong at the work. Management is a different job: you're now judged on what your team produces, how clearly priorities are set, and whether people can do their best work without you hovering.

Nobody may have written that down. So your first task isn't to prove you're brilliant. It's to replace ambiguity with agreements, in writing, with the people who evaluate you.

## Pre-start checklist: what to do before day one

Work through these in the days before (or, if you've already started, in your first few days).

### 1. Get clear on the scope of your role

Ask your manager, and write down the answers:

- Which team, projects and processes are now yours?
- What decisions can I make alone? What needs your approval? (Budget, hiring, schedules, tooling, vendor choices.)
- What will you be looking at when you decide whether I'm doing well in six months?
- What's the biggest problem on this team right now?
- How often will we meet, and what do you want me to bring to those meetings?

If the answers are fuzzy, say so politely: "Here's what I'm hearing. Tell me where I've got it wrong." Then send a short summary email. That email becomes your first piece of evidence.

### 2. Learn what changes about your own position

Check with HR or your manager on the practical details:

- Has your pay, classification or contract changed? Rules on overtime, notice periods and probation differ between the US, Canada (by province), Australia and the UK, so don't assume your old terms carry over.
- Do you now have access to compensation, performance or personnel files? What's confidential?
- What training does the company offer for new managers?

### 3. Understand your people before you meet them

Collect what already exists:

- An org chart and each person's role, location and time zone.
- Recent performance reviews, goals and any development plans.
- Open requests: promotions, raises, leave, flexible-schedule arrangements.
- Current projects, deadlines and who owns what.

Be careful with how you use this. Read it to prepare questions, not to form fixed opinions.

### 4. Plan the peer-to-boss shift

If you're managing former teammates, address it directly rather than hoping it goes unnoticed. Decide what you'll say about the change, and think ahead about friendships, shared complaints and inside jokes that might now put you in an awkward spot. You can stay friendly without staying a confidant on every topic.

### 5. Set up your systems

On a remote team, your calendar and documents are your office. Before day one:

- Block recurring time for 1:1s, focus work and team meetings.
- Create a private running doc for each direct report (topics, commitments, feedback given).
- Find where decisions, goals and processes are documented, and note what's missing.
- Check your tools' permissions: approvals, timesheets, expense systems, shared drives.

### 6. Line up your own support

Ask for a mentor or peer manager you can check in with. Ask who handles questions you shouldn't solve alone: HR for employee issues, legal or compliance for anything involving discipline, termination, harassment or discrimination. Know that path before you need it.

## Your week-one agenda

The aim of week one is to listen, set a rhythm and avoid big changes. Here's a template you can adapt to your time zones and team size.

| Day | Focus | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Announce and align | Send a short, warm note to the team: who you are, how you'll work, when 1:1s will happen. Meet your manager to confirm priorities and success measures. |
| Tuesday | Start 1:1s | Hold your first 1:1 with each direct report (30–45 minutes). Use the questions below. Take notes and send a short follow-up. |
| Wednesday | Map the work | Review projects, deadlines, risks and dependencies. Identify what's late, unowned or unclear. |
| Thursday | Meet your peers and partners | Talk to managers or stakeholders your team relies on. Ask what's working and what frustrates them. |
| Friday | Reflect and share | Write a one-page summary of what you heard. Share the themes (not private comments) with the team and your manager, with next steps. |

If your team spans many time zones, spread 1:1s over the first two weeks rather than squeezing them in. A rushed meeting at someone's 8 p.m. sends the wrong message.

### First 1:1 questions that work

Keep it conversational. Pick four or five:

- What's going well that I should protect?
- What's getting in your way right now?
- How do you prefer to get feedback, and how often?
- What's the best way to reach you: chat, email, video? When are you offline?
- What do you want to be doing in a year? What would help you get there?
- What should I know about how this team has worked in the past?
- What's one thing you'd change if you were me?

Then share how you work: your communication style, your response times and your expectations. Ask them to correct you if something doesn't fit.

### What to avoid in week one

- Announcing big process changes before you understand why things are the way they are.
- Taking over tasks from your old role because it feels comfortable.
- Complaining about your predecessor or the previous structure.
- Promising things you can't control, like raises or promotions.

## Turning vague goals into results others can verify

"Improve team morale" and "make things run smoothly" can't be checked, which means they can't protect you in a review. A good goal answers: what will be different, how will we know, and by when?

### A simple formula

**Outcome + evidence + deadline + owner of the check.**

Weak: "Improve communication on the team."

Better: "By the end of month two, every project has a written owner, a next-step date and a status note in the shared tracker, reviewed weekly. My manager can open the tracker to check."

Weak: "Develop my team."

Better: "By day 60, each direct report has a written development goal agreed in a 1:1. By day 90, I've held a check-in on each one and recorded progress in our shared notes."

Notice the evidence in these examples is something a person can look at: a document, a calendar record, a tracker, a delivery date. You don't need statistics. You need proof that exists outside your own opinion.

### Choose a mix of goal types

Aim for three to five goals, not ten. Cover:

1. **Delivery:** Something the team ships or fixes. Use an agreed measure such as on-time delivery of a named project, a backlog cleared, or an error type reduced from a baseline you record in week one.
2. **People:** Regular 1:1s held, development goals documented, feedback given.
3. **Process:** One bottleneck removed, with a before-and-after you can show.
4. **Relationships:** Stakeholders you've met and a regular update to them.
5. **Your own learning:** A training course completed or a skill practiced, with feedback from a peer.

### A first-quarter outline

**Days 1–30: Learn and listen**
- Hold a first 1:1 with every direct report, then on a regular schedule.
- Record a baseline for whatever you'll try to improve. If there's no data, note that and start collecting.
- Agree your priorities and success measures with your manager in writing.
- Write down the team's top three problems, in the team's own words.

**Days 31–60: Fix one thing and set expectations**
- Pick one process problem and make one visible change. Explain why.
- Write down team working agreements: response times, meeting norms, handoffs, when to escalate.
- Agree a development goal with each direct report.
- Give timely, specific feedback, including praise. Don't save it for reviews.

**Days 61–90: Show results and reset**
- Compare your baseline with where things stand now. Share what changed and what didn't.
- Ask your team for feedback on your management, using two or three questions in an anonymous form or a 1:1.
- Hold a review with your manager against the written measures.
- Set goals for the next quarter based on what you learned.

## Habits that matter more on remote teams

Distance makes small gaps bigger. A few habits help:

- **Write things down.** Decisions, priorities and expectations should live in a shared place, not just in a call.
- **Keep 1:1s sacred.** Moving them often tells people they're low priority. The agenda belongs mostly to the report.
- **Check in on workload, not activity.** Judge outcomes and clarity of ownership, not how quickly someone replies in chat.
- **Respect working hours and time zones.** Use scheduled sends and avoid expecting immediate responses outside someone's day.
- **Make time for informal contact.** A short chat at the start of a call or an occasional non-work channel helps, but keep participation optional.
- **Be explicit about how you'll give feedback.** In text, tone gets lost. For anything sensitive, use video or a call.

## When things get hard

You'll face a performance issue, a conflict or a request you can't grant. Some basics:

- Address problems early and privately, with specific examples and a clear expectation of what should change.
- Document conversations factually.
- Involve HR before formal action. Employment rules differ by country and region, and mistakes are costly for you and for the employee.
- Don't discuss one person's situation with others on the team.

## Common first-quarter mistakes

- **Doing instead of managing.** If you're still the team's top individual contributor, ask what only you can do and delegate the rest.
- **Changing too much too fast.** One visible improvement beats five half-finished ones.
- **Going quiet with your own manager.** Send a short update on a regular rhythm: progress, risks, what you need.
- **Treating everyone the same.** Fair doesn't mean identical. Adapt how you coach and communicate.
- **Skipping your own development.** Ask for feedback from your manager and your team, and act on it.

## Your next step

Before you do anything else, send your manager a short email summarizing your understanding of the role, the top priorities and how success will be measured. Ask for corrections. That single message does more for your first quarter than any template, because it replaces guesswork with something both of you can point to.
