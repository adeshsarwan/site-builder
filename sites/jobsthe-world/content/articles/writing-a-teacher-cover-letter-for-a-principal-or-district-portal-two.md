A teacher cover letter has two jobs. For a principal, it shows you understand their building and would be a good colleague. For a district portal, it has to get you through an automated screen and a human who may read dozens of applications in one sitting. These are different readers, so one generic letter will underperform in both places.

This guide gives you two templates, matching resume bullets, and a short list of the lines worth rewriting each time. It's written for applicants in the US, Canada, Australia and the UK, including those applying from a distance or for online and hybrid roles.

## First, know who is actually reading

Before you write, work out which of these you're dealing with.

**A principal or head of school (direct email or school-run application).** They care about fit: your subject and grade, your classroom approach, how you'd work with their team, and whether you've paid attention to their school. A warm, specific, one-page letter works well.

**A district, school board or central portal.** Your application is often reviewed against posted requirements first: certification or registration, subject endorsements, grade band, background checks. The letter should make those easy to find. Personality still matters, but clarity comes first.

**A hybrid.** Many portals forward shortlisted candidates to the principal. Write the portal letter so it still sounds like a person wrote it.

## What every teacher cover letter needs

Whatever the audience, keep to one page and four moves:

1. **Name the role and your credential status in the first paragraph.** For example, "certified in [state/province] to teach grades 6–8 mathematics."
2. **Give one or two short proof points.** Use outcomes you can honestly describe. If you have real numbers, use them. If you don't, describe what changed and how you know.
3. **Show you've researched them.** One concrete detail about the school or district, drawn from their website, improvement plan or job posting.
4. **Close with a clear next step.** Say you'd welcome a conversation and note your availability.

Skip the childhood-dream-of-teaching opening, a list of every duty you've held, and a repeat of your resume in paragraph form.

## Template 1: The letter to a principal

Use this when you have a name, an email address or a school-specific application.

> Dear [Principal's Name],
>
> I'm writing to apply for the [grade/subject] teaching position at [School Name]. I hold [credential, e.g., a state teaching license in secondary English / Ontario Certificate of Qualification / QTS / state teacher registration] and have [X] years of experience teaching [grade/subject] in [setting].
>
> What drew me to [School Name] is [specific detail: a literacy initiative, a project-based program, a bilingual pathway, a community partnership you saw on the school's site]. In my current role, I [one concrete example that parallels it]. For instance, [short story: what you did, how students responded, what you changed afterward].
>
> In the classroom, I focus on [two or three practices, e.g., clear learning targets, frequent low-stakes checks for understanding, restorative conversations]. I also work closely with colleagues: [example, such as co-planning with a special education teacher, leading a grade-level data meeting, mentoring a student teacher].
>
> I'd value the chance to talk about how I could contribute to your team. I'm available [days/times or "flexibly"] and can be reached at [phone/email]. Thank you for your time and consideration.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Name]

### Lines to customize in Template 1

- **The "what drew me" sentence.** This is the highest-value line. Make it impossible to paste into another school's letter.
- **The parallel example.** Pick the story from your experience that is closest to the school's stated priorities.
- **The colleague example.** Small schools often weigh collaboration heavily; larger ones may care more about your grade-level or department contribution.
- **The salutation.** Find the principal's name. If the posting is anonymous, "Dear Hiring Committee" is fine.

## Template 2: The letter for a district portal

Use this when you're uploading into an applicant tracking system, applying to a pool, or applying to several schools at once.

> Dear Hiring Team,
>
> I am applying for the [exact posting title] position (Requisition/Job ID: [number]) with [District/Board Name]. I am [certified/licensed/registered] in [jurisdiction] to teach [subject/grade band], with [endorsements such as ESL, special education, gifted, French immersion] and a current [background check/clearance status as applicable].
>
> My experience includes:
> - [Core duty from the posting, matched to your experience]
> - [Second requirement from the posting, matched to your experience]
> - [Third requirement, such as differentiated instruction, data-informed planning, or technology integration]
>
> I am familiar with [District Name]'s [strategic plan, equity commitment, curriculum framework, or student population served] and would bring [one relevant strength] to [type of school or program]. I am open to [specific schools, regions, or program types] and can start by [date].
>
> Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my background fits your needs.
>
> Respectfully,
> [Name]

### Lines to customize in Template 2

- **Posting title and ID.** Copy these exactly. Portals and HR teams file by them.
- **The credential sentence.** Put the most screenable facts first. If your registration is pending, say so plainly and give the expected date.
- **The three bullets.** Mirror the posting's language where it's true. If the posting says "differentiated instruction" and you do that, use that phrase rather than a synonym.
- **Your flexibility line.** Districts often hire into a pool. Saying which schools or grade levels you'd consider can move you forward.

## Matching resume bullets

Your letter and resume should tell the same story with different detail. Here are bullet patterns to adapt. Replace the brackets with your own facts, and leave out any number you can't support.

**For the principal version, emphasize classroom and community:**

- Designed and taught [course/grade] units built around [approach], adjusting instruction using [exit tickets/quizzes/conferences].
- Co-planned with [special education/ESL/grade-level] colleagues to support [type of learners].
- Started or ran [club, family night, reading program], bringing in [families/community partners].

**For the district version, emphasize requirements and evidence:**

- Delivered [subject] instruction aligned to [state standards/provincial curriculum/Australian Curriculum/National Curriculum] for [grade band].
- Used [assessment data type] to group students and plan interventions, resulting in [observed change].
- Supported [IEPs/504 plans/IEP-equivalent supports, EAL/D or EAL, behavior plans] in an inclusive classroom.
- Taught in [online/hybrid/in-person] settings using [LMS name] and [video or collaboration tools].

Use strong, plain verbs: taught, planned, assessed, coached, redesigned, led. Avoid "responsible for."

## Quick comparison: what to change for each audience

| Element | Principal letter | District portal letter |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Role plus a reason you want this school | Exact title, job ID, credential status |
| Tone | Warm, conversational, professional | Clear, structured, professional |
| Evidence | One or two stories | Three matched requirements |
| Research detail | School program or community | District plan, priorities, or student population |
| Format | Short paragraphs | Short paragraphs plus a brief bullet list |
| Closing | Offer to talk, with availability | Availability, start date, flexibility |

## Regional details to get right

Credential language differs, and an error here can cost you a screen.

- **United States:** State licensure is the key item. Name your state, subject and grade band, and any endorsements. If you're moving states, say you're applying for reciprocity or already hold the license. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Outlook Handbook describes typical entry requirements for teachers, but each state sets its own, so check the state education agency.
- **Canada:** Teacher certification is provincial or territorial. Name your certifying body and any additional qualifications. If the posting is for a French immersion or Indigenous education role, say so only if it's accurate.
- **Australia:** Teachers register with the state or territory regulator. Mention your registration status and, where relevant, that you have the required working-with-children clearance.
- **United Kingdom:** Mention Qualified Teacher Status (QTS), or your route to it, and the phase and subject you teach. Many UK schools ask for a supporting statement rather than a traditional letter, so follow the posting's instructions on length and content.

Always use local terms: "school board" in much of Canada, "head teacher" in the UK, "year level" in Australia. Matching their vocabulary shows you've done your homework.

## If you're applying remotely or for online roles

When you apply from a distance, address logistics briefly, because reviewers will wonder.

- State your location, time zone and relocation plans in one sentence. For example, "I am relocating to [area] in [month] and available for interviews by video in the meantime."
- For virtual teaching, name the platforms you've used and what you did with them: live lessons, asynchronous feedback, parent communication.
- Make clear that you meet the licensing requirements of the jurisdiction hiring you. Remote status doesn't remove them.

## Final checks before you submit

- Read it aloud. If a sentence could appear in any teacher's letter, rewrite it.
- Check the school's or district's name, the principal's name and the job title three times. Pasted-over names are the most common mistake.
- Save as PDF unless the portal asks for another format, and name the file clearly: "Lastname_Firstname_CoverLetter."
- Confirm the letter stays within one page.
- Ask a colleague to confirm the claims in it are ones you could discuss in an interview.

Build a master version of each template, then spend your time on the three or four lines that change. That's how you can apply to many schools without any of the letters reading like a form.
