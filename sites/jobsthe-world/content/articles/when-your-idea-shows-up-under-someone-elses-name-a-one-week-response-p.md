You open the channel, the meeting recording or the shared doc, and there it is: your analysis, your phrasing or your proposal, presented under a colleague's name. Your stomach drops. Then the second worry arrives: if you say something, will you look petty?

On remote and hybrid teams this is both easier to fix and easier to miss than in an office. Everything is written down and timestamped, but fewer people see the full picture. Nobody overhears who did the work. Credit is decided by whatever is visible in the places where decisions get made.

This plan gives you seven days to respond. The pace is deliberate. It is fast enough that the record gets corrected while it still matters, and slow enough that you don't send something you regret.

## First, decide what actually happened

Before you reply to anything, work out which of these situations you're in. The right response differs for each.

- **Careless shorthand.** The person said "we landed on this approach" or "here's the plan," and people assumed it was theirs. Nobody was misled on purpose.
- **Sloppy attribution.** They used your work and forgot to name you, often because they were rushing to present.
- **Genuine misrepresentation.** They described your work as something they did, and you can point to evidence that they didn't.
- **A pattern.** This is the second or third time, or it happens mostly when senior people are watching.

Most first incidents fall into the first two categories. That matters because your goal is to correct the record. Winning an argument about motive is a different goal and usually a worse one. You can fix attribution without ever deciding what was in the other person's head.

## The one-week plan at a glance

| Day | Action | Purpose |

| Day 1 | Pause, then gather evidence | Replace the feeling with facts |
| Day 2 | Add a neutral public clarification (if the situation calls for it) | Fix the record quickly and lightly |

## Day 1: Pause, then gather evidence

Don't respond in the thread. Don't send a "just to clarify" message while your pulse is up. Close the tab, take a walk and come back.

Then collect what you need, quietly:

- Document version history showing who wrote what and when
- Your original messages, drafts, files or pull requests with timestamps
- Calendar invites or notes showing when you shared the work with the team
- The exact wording the coworker used (copy it; don't paraphrase)

Look at the specific words. "Here's the analysis" is different from "I built this analysis." Knowing exactly what was claimed keeps your response accurate, and accuracy is what keeps you from looking petty.

Also ask who saw it. A comment in a ten-person channel calls for a different response than a presentation to leadership.

## Day 2: Fix the record publicly, lightly

If many people saw the claim, and especially if decisions depend on it, a short public correction is reasonable. The best ones add information rather than subtract from the other person. You're giving credit, not taking it back.

Some phrasings that work:

- "Glad this is useful. I put the original model together last month, so happy to walk anyone through the assumptions."
- "Building on this, since I drafted the first version, here's the part I'd flag for next steps."
- "Thanks for sharing this. For context, the data pull and the first draft of the recommendations were mine, so ask me if you want the details."

Notice what's missing: no "actually," no "as I said," no accusations, no reference to the other person's behavior. You state your role as a fact and offer help, which makes you sound like the person who knows the material.

In a live meeting, the same move works out loud: "Happy to add context here, since I did the analysis." Say it calmly, then keep the conversation on the work.

Skip the public correction when the audience is tiny, the issue is minor and you'd rather start privately. Not every slip needs an audience. If you're unsure, go to the private message first and see what happens.

## Day 2–3: Message the coworker privately

This message is the most important step for protecting the relationship. Use a private channel or a short call, not email with a long list of cc's. Keep it brief and assume the best, while being clear about what you want.

A template you can adapt:

> "Hi [name], quick one. In [meeting/doc/channel], the [analysis/proposal] was described as yours. I actually built the first version, and I'd like the team to know that, since it affects how people bring me questions and how my work gets seen. Could you add a note crediting me, or would you rather I post a quick clarification? Happy to do either."

Why this works:

- **It names the specific thing**, so there's nothing vague to argue with.
- **It states the impact** in terms of your work and visibility, not their character.
- **It offers a clear fix with a choice**, which lets them correct course without feeling cornered.

If you'd rather not write it, ask for a ten-minute video call and use the same structure. Tone is easier to manage out loud for some people, and harder for others. Choose the format you handle best.

### If they get defensive

Stay on facts. "I'm not saying it was intentional, I just want the record to be accurate." Then repeat the ask. You don't need an apology. You need a correction.

## Day 4: Check whether it was fixed

Look at the channel or doc again. Did they add a credit? Did your public note land without pushback? If yes, thank them briefly and move on. A kind "thanks for updating that" costs nothing and makes a repeat less likely.

If nothing changed, you now have a clean sequence: the work, the claim, your private request, no resolution. That is a strong, calm basis for the next step.

## Day 5: Involve your manager when it's warranted

Go to your manager if any of the following apply:

- The correction didn't happen after you asked
- The work feeds into performance reviews, promotions or client commitments
- Senior stakeholders saw the claim
- It has happened before

Keep the conversation about outcomes. Something like:

> "I want to flag something about [project]. I built the first version of [work], but it was presented as [coworker]'s in [place]. I asked them to clarify and it hasn't changed. Can I get your help making sure the ownership is clear to [stakeholders]? I have version history if useful."

Bring your evidence, but don't lead with it. Don't ask for the coworker to be disciplined. Ask for clarity. Managers tend to respond better to a request they can act on, and you come across as someone solving a problem.

If the behavior is repeated or involves something more serious, check your employer's handbook for the right internal channel. Rules and protections differ by country, state, province and employer, so for anything beyond ordinary misattribution, read your company policy and consider speaking with a qualified advisor in your region.

## Day 6: Make attribution automatic

Once you've handled this incident, reduce the odds of a repeat. On distributed teams, visibility is something you build into how you work.

- **Put your name on the work.** Add author and date to docs, decks and dashboards.
- **Share progress in the open.** Post short updates in the team channel while you work, such as "draft of the pricing comparison is up, feedback welcome." The thread becomes a public record.
- **Send a brief summary after meetings** that lists who owns which pieces.
- **Present your own work** when you can, or ask to be on the agenda for it.
- **Use shared workspaces** with version history instead of attaching files to private messages.
- **Credit others generously.** It sets a norm that makes the right behavior easy to copy.

None of these requires you to be loud. They simply make ownership obvious before anyone has to ask.

## Day 7: Review and decide what's next

Take fifteen minutes to write down what happened, what you did, and how it resolved. Keep it factual and private. If it was a one-off, you're done. If a second incident shows up, you'll have dates and details instead of a vague sense that something is off.

Ask yourself two questions:

1. Did this person respond reasonably once I raised it?
2. Is the team's culture making it easy to blur credit, for example through unclear ownership or rushed presentations?

If the answers are "no" and "yes," that tells you something about whether to invest in this team long term, which is useful to know if you're also weighing other roles.

## What not to do

- **Don't reply-all with a correction in the heat of the moment.** The tone will outlast the facts.
- **Don't recruit allies to pile on.** It converts a small problem into a conflict.
- **Don't rewrite history.** Claim only what you did and acknowledge what others contributed.
- **Don't wait weeks.** The longer a claim stands, the stranger a correction feels.
- **Don't screenshot-dump.** Keep evidence for your manager if it's needed, not as a threat.

## A quick note on tone across regions

Norms about directness vary between workplaces, and between countries, industries and companies. A blunt message that's normal on one team can land badly on another. If you're unsure, soften the wording and keep the facts. The structure of this plan holds either way: check the facts, correct the record lightly, speak privately, escalate only if needed.

## The takeaway

Being credited fairly is part of doing the job, and asking for it doesn't make you difficult. The approach that tends to work is steady: collect specifics, add context in public without blame, give the person a chance to fix it, and bring in your manager only when the issue persists. Done well, you end the week with the record corrected and your working relationships intact.
