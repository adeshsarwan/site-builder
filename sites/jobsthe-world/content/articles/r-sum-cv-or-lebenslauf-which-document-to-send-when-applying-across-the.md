If you work remotely, your next employer could be in Toronto, Manchester, Melbourne or Austin, and each hiring team may mean something different when it asks for a "CV." Send the wrong document and you can look careless. Send a 12-page academic record to a UK recruiter, or a one-page summary to a university, and the mismatch shows straight away.

This guide explains what each term usually means, how the four main markets differ, and how to keep one master file that you adapt in minutes instead of rewriting from scratch.

## The short answer

- **United States:** Send a **résumé** for almost all private-sector, nonprofit and government-adjacent jobs. Use a **CV** only for academic, research, clinical or scientific roles, or when the posting explicitly asks for one.
- **Canada:** Send a **résumé** for most jobs. Use a **CV** for academic and research roles. In French-speaking Canada, "CV" often just means the standard job-search document.
- **United Kingdom:** Send a **CV**. It is the normal job-search document, and it is short and targeted. "Résumé" is understood but rarely used.
- **Australia:** **Résumé** and **CV** are used interchangeably for the same job-search document. Follow the wording in the posting.
- **German-speaking employers:** A **Lebenslauf** is the German equivalent. It is a structured, usually reverse-chronological document, often submitted with a cover letter and supporting certificates.

## Why the words cause confusion

Both "résumé" and "curriculum vitae" describe a record of your working life. The split is about convention, not definition.

In the US, the résumé became the short, tailored sales document. The CV stayed with academia, where the expectation is a complete record: publications, grants, teaching, conference talks, committee work. In the UK and Australia, the term "CV" took over the short document, and the long academic version is usually called an academic CV or a full publication list.

So a US recruiter who asks for a CV in a corporate posting probably means a résumé, while a UK university hiring a lecturer wants the long version. Context settles it: look at the industry, the seniority and the wording of the instructions.

## What each market tends to expect

### United States: the tailored one-pager (or two)

US hiring teams usually skim. They want recent results near the top, with measurable outcomes where you honestly have them. Keep it to one page if you have under roughly a decade of experience, and two if you have more to say that is relevant.

Leave off your photo, birth date, marital status and similar details. US employers generally avoid receiving them because of anti-discrimination rules, and many applicant tracking systems or screeners are set up without them.

Use the CV only when the field calls for it. If you are applying for a postdoc, faculty role, research scientist job or similar, build a full CV with sections for publications, presentations, grants and teaching. The US Department of Labor's CareerOneStop site has general guidance on both documents if you want an official reference.

### Canada: very close to the US

Canadian employers expect a résumé that looks much like the US version: reverse-chronological, achievement-focused, no photo and no personal data. Spelling follows Canadian conventions (for example "centre" and "organization" are both in everyday use, so mirror the posting).

Two Canada-specific points:

- **Quebec and French-language roles.** If the posting is in French, you will often see "CV" used for the job document. Submit a French-language version if the employer works in French, rather than relying on an English file.
- **Work authorization.** If you live elsewhere and the employer is Canadian, say clearly whether you can work for them legally and whether you would be a contractor or an employee. The government's Job Bank has resources aimed at job seekers in Canada.

### United Kingdom: the CV is the résumé

In the UK, "CV" is the everyday word. The usual structure is a short profile, work history in reverse order, education, and skills. Most candidates keep it concise, and recruiters often prefer two pages. No photo, and you do not need to include your date of birth or marital status.

Use British spelling ("organised", "programme") and a day-month-year date format. If the posting asks for a "supporting statement", that is a separate piece of writing and not part of the CV; the public sector and universities use that term often. The National Careers Service has free guidance on writing a CV for UK applications.

### Australia: flexible term, slightly more detail

In Australia the two terms are interchangeable, so a "résumé" request and a "CV" request usually produce the same document. Length tends to be a bit more forgiving than in the US, and employers often like to see a clear career summary, key achievements per role, and sometimes referees.

Use Australian spelling and A4 page size. Where you list referees, ask them first and include a name, role and contact method. If you are applying from abroad, state your work rights and your location, because employers will want to know whether you can legally be employed and whether your time zone overlaps with theirs. Workforce Australia, the government's employment service, has job-seeker guidance on both documents.

### Germany and the Lebenslauf

If a remote role is with a German or other German-speaking employer, expect a more formal package: a **Lebenslauf** (a tabular, usually reverse-chronological record), a cover letter (Anschreiben), and sometimes copies of references or certificates. A photo has been traditional in German applications, though many candidates and employers now leave it out. Because anti-discrimination expectations differ from employer to employer, follow the posting, and if you are not sure, ask the recruiter or leave the photo off.

Gaps in work history are usually expected to be explained briefly. A concise line is enough.

## Build one master document, then adapt it

Rewriting from scratch for every country wastes time. Use this approach instead.

1. **Keep a master file.** List every job, project, tool, result, publication and certification, with dates. This file is for you and can run long.
2. **Create a short version.** Pull the most relevant material for the job into one or two pages. This becomes your US résumé, Canadian résumé, UK CV or Australian résumé.
3. **Keep a long academic CV only if you need it.** If you have publications or teaching, maintain this as a separate document and never send it to a corporate employer unprompted.
4. **Localize the details.** Change these items, not the substance:
   - Spelling (organization vs organisation, program vs programme)
   - Date format (month-day-year in the US; day-month-year in the UK and Australia)
   - Paper size (US Letter vs A4)
   - Phone number format with country code
   - Language, if the employer works in French or German
5. **Name the file clearly.** Use something like `Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf` or `Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf`, matching the term the employer used.

## Remote-specific details to add

Remote hiring adds questions that a local application would never raise. Answer them on the document or in your cover note.

- **Location and time zone.** A header line such as "Based in Lisbon, available for US Eastern overlap" saves the recruiter a question.
- **Work authorization.** Say whether you can be employed directly, or whether you work as an independent contractor. Do not guess at the rules; check the employer's policy and the relevant government guidance.
- **Remote tools and habits.** Mention async collaboration, documentation, project tools and any distributed team experience, but only if you have actually done the work.

## Check the instructions before you send

Before you submit, scan the posting for these signals:

- The exact term: "résumé", "CV", "curriculum vitae", "Lebenslauf"
- A requested format (PDF, Word, upload to a portal)
- A page limit
- A cover letter, supporting statement or selection criteria
- Anything that says "do not include a photo" or "include references"

If the instructions conflict with general convention, follow the instructions. If there are none and the field is not academic, send the short, targeted document that fits the country.

## A quick pre-send checklist

- [ ] The document uses the same term the employer used
- [ ] Length fits local convention and any stated limit
- [ ] No photo or personal data unless the posting asks for it
- [ ] Spelling, date format and page size match the employer's country
- [ ] Location, time zone and work authorization are clear
- [ ] The file is a clean PDF (unless another type is requested) with a sensible name
- [ ] Every claim is accurate and can be backed up in an interview

Getting the label right is a small detail, but it signals that you understand how the employer works. That matters when you are asking someone to trust you across a time zone.
