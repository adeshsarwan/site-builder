If you have applied to dozens of remote roles and heard nothing, the problem may not be your résumé. Some of the listings you applied to may not have been real, open, or open to you.

Postings stay online after a role is filled, get reposted to look active, or exist only to collect résumés for later. Remote roles make this worse because they attract applicants from everywhere. Many are also restricted to certain states or countries, and the listing doesn't always say so.

This guide gives you a process in three parts: find openings at the source, check whether each one is live, and give a person a reason to read your application. It works whether you live in the US or are applying from Canada, Australia or the UK.

## Step 1: Treat job boards as a map, not a destination

Large boards are good at showing you which companies are hiring for a role type. They are poor at telling you whether a specific posting is current. Use them for discovery, then move to the employer's own site.

A workable routine:

1. Search the board with a tight title and a "remote" filter, and sort by newest.
2. Skip anything without a named company or a clear description of the work.
3. For each promising listing, open the company's career page and find the same role there.
4. Apply on the company's page, not through a third-party "easy apply" button, when you can.

If a role appears on a board but you can't find it on the company's own site, treat that as a warning. It may have been filled or pulled, or the board may simply be lagging. Either way, your application is less likely to reach a person.

### Use filters that match remote reality

"Remote" on a board can mean fully remote, hybrid with a different label, or remote within one state. Set your filters, then read the listing for the following:

- **Location language:** "Remote – US" is different from "Remote – anywhere." Look for state lists, "must reside in," or time-zone requirements.
- **Employment type:** Employee, contractor, or through a staffing or employer-of-record arrangement. This affects pay, taxes and benefits.
- **Work authorization:** Whether they sponsor visas or will only hire people who can already work in the US, or in your country of residence.
- **Hours:** Overlap with US time zones, especially if you're in Australia or the UK.

## Step 2: Go to company career pages and the systems behind them

Most mid-size and larger employers run their openings through an applicant tracking system (ATS), a hiring database that holds the postings and the applications. Their career pages usually pull from it, so the career page is typically the most current record of what is open.

### Search the ATS directly

Many ATS platforms host public job pages at predictable web addresses. You can use a search engine to search those pages for your target title. Examples of address patterns you will see include greenhouse.io, lever.co, ashbyhq.com and myworkdayjobs.com. A search might look like this:

`site:jobs.lever.co "customer support" remote`

Swap in the domain and your own title. Then use the search engine's time filter to show results from the past week or month. Address formats change from time to time, so if one pattern returns nothing, try another or search for the company name plus "careers."

### Build a target company list

Searching by company beats searching by keyword. Spend an hour building a list of 25 to 40 employers that:

- hire remote workers in your function,
- have a history of hiring in your country, or are clearly open to it, and
- are the right size for your background.

Then check those career pages on a set schedule, such as twice a week. New postings are often most useful in their first few days, before the applicant pool grows. CareerOneStop, the US Department of Labor–sponsored site, has employer and occupation tools that help you identify which industries and job titles match your skills.

### Check the page, not just the title

Before you invest in an application, look for these signs.

| Signal | Likely live | Possibly stale or ghost |
|---|---|---|
| Posting date | Recent date, or a clear "posted X days ago" | No date, or the date keeps resetting |
| Where it appears | On the company's career page and the board | Only on a board or only on a re-posting site |
| Job details | Specific tools, team, reporting line, pay range where provided | Generic duties that could fit any company |
| Application flow | Opens a working form with role-specific questions | Redirects in circles, or asks for payment or sensitive data |
| Repetition | Posted once | Same role posted for months, or many near-identical versions |
| Company activity | Recent news, team posts, other roles opening | Hiring freeze mentioned, or a quiet company |

No single signal proves anything. A role open for a long time can be real, such as a hard-to-fill specialist position. Weigh several signals together and don't spend time over-analyzing a borderline listing.

### Watch for scams

Remote listings attract fake employers. Be wary of anyone who asks you to pay for equipment or training, send a check, or provide bank or ID details before an offer. The US Federal Trade Commission's consumer site has guidance on job scams. If an interview happens only by text or chat app, and the email domain doesn't match the company's website, stop and verify.

## Step 3: Make sure you are eligible before you apply

For cross-border searches, a surprising amount of wasted effort comes from applying where you can't be hired.

- **Check the location line.** If it says "US only," a Canadian, Australian or UK applicant without US work authorization is likely to be filtered out, whatever your skills.
- **Look for international hiring language.** Terms like "we hire in these countries," "employer of record," or "contractor" suggest the company can employ people abroad.
- **Be honest on the authorization question.** Application forms often include a yes/no question about legal right to work. Answer accurately. If you need sponsorship, the official source on US work eligibility and visa categories is USCIS (uscis.gov). Don't assume a company sponsors because it is large.
- **Clarify pay and tax implications early.** If you would be a contractor, you may handle your own taxes and benefits. Ask in a screening call and check with a qualified tax adviser in your country.

## Step 4: Get past automated screening

Many people picture a robot rejecting résumés. In practice, ATS tools mostly store and sort applications, and recruiters search or filter them. Some employers add knockout questions, such as location, authorization or required experience, which can screen people out automatically. You can't beat those with formatting. You can only answer them accurately and apply to roles you qualify for.

What you can control:

- **Mirror the posting's language truthfully.** If the listing says "customer onboarding" and you call it "client implementation," add the phrase the employer uses where it's accurate. Recruiters often search by these terms.
- **Keep the file simple.** Use a standard single-column layout, normal headings such as "Experience" and "Education," and a common file format. Skip text boxes, icons for contact details, and graphics that could scramble when the system reads the file.
- **Put the basics at the top.** Include your city and country, your time zone, and your work authorization status in a line the reviewer can see.
- **Match your title and dates to your profile.** Inconsistencies between your résumé and your online profile slow reviewers down.
- **Tailor the top third.** The summary and your first few bullets should reflect the role. You don't need to rewrite the whole résumé for every application.
- **Answer the screening questions in full.** Short, specific answers to open-ended questions carry weight because a person often reads them.

## Step 5: Reach a human reviewer

An application in the ATS is the start, not the end. Adding a human contact to the same application can help.

### Find the right person

Look at the posting for the recruiter's or hiring manager's name. If it isn't listed, check the company's team page or professional profiles for people with titles such as recruiter, talent partner, or the manager of the team you'd join. If you can't tell who is right, it is better to contact a recruiter than to guess a senior leader.

### Send a short, specific message

Apply first, then write within a day or two. Keep it to a few sentences:

1. Name the role and say you applied.
2. Give one or two specific reasons you fit, such as relevant results or tools.
3. Mention your location and work eligibility, so they don't have to hunt for it.
4. Offer to answer questions, and thank them without pressure.

Don't attach a stack of documents or ask for a referral from a stranger in the first message. Send one follow-up after about a week if there's no reply, and then move on.

### Use warm routes when you have them

A referral from a current employee is often the most direct route to a reviewer, because many companies route referrals to the recruiter. Look through your former colleagues, alumni networks and communities in your field for people at your target companies. Ask for advice about the team first, and ask for a referral only if the conversation goes well.

## A weekly routine that keeps you focused

- **Monday:** Check your target company career pages and save new roles. Verify location and eligibility.
- **Tuesday and Wednesday:** Submit tailored applications to the best three to five matches, and message a human contact for each.
- **Thursday:** Search ATS-hosted pages with date filters for roles you may have missed.
- **Friday:** Follow up on week-old applications, update your tracking sheet, and cut sources that produce only stale listings.

Track each application with the date, source, link, contact and status. After a few weeks you will see which sources produce replies and which waste your time.

## Final checklist before you hit submit

- Is the role on the company's own career page?
- Is the posting recent and specific?
- Does the location line include my country or state?
- Can I truthfully answer the authorization question?
- Does my résumé reflect the posting's key terms?
- Do I have a named person to message?

Fewer, better-targeted applications to real openings usually beat a large number sent to listings that may never have been live.
