You have an offer, and the role is on-site or hybrid. You would rather work remotely, or at least more than the offer allows. Two worries tend to follow. One is that asking now is too late. The other is that even if the answer is yes, the employer can quietly take it back.

Both worries are manageable. The first is mostly about timing. The second is about wording. This guide covers both, with scripts you can adapt.

## When to ask: after the written offer, before you accept

The best moment to negotiate is after you have a written offer and before you say yes. The employer has chosen you, so your leverage is at its peak. They have also stopped comparing you with other candidates.

If you can, raise location earlier as a question, not a demand. Asking in an interview, "How does the team work day to day, and where does the work get done?" costs you nothing. It also tells you whether a request is realistic before you invest more time.

Asking after you have accepted is a different situation. By then you have agreed to the terms on paper, and any change becomes a favour rather than a negotiation. If you are already in that position, see the notes on requesting flexibility after you start, below.

### A simple timeline

1. **Interviews:** Ask neutral questions about where and how the team works.
2. **Verbal offer:** Thank them, ask for the offer in writing, and say you would like to review the full terms, including work location.
3. **Written offer:** Read the location wording and any referenced policies, then send your request in one clear message.
4. **Agreement:** Get the agreed arrangement into the signed contract or an attached letter.
5. **Signature:** Sign only after the document matches what was discussed.

Most employers expect some back-and-forth on an offer. A polite, specific request about location is rarely a reason to withdraw one, though outcomes depend on the company and role.

## Decide what you are actually asking for

"More remote" is vague, and vague requests are easy to refuse. Before you write anything, choose a specific ask and a fallback.

- **Fully remote**, with occasional travel for planned events.
- **Hybrid with fewer office days**, such as two instead of four.
- **Flexible days**, where you choose which days you attend.
- **Remote start date after onboarding**, for example in-office for the first month and then remote.
- **A trial period**, with a review date and defined criteria.

Be ready to say why it works for the employer, not only for you. Good reasons are concrete: your team already spreads across locations, your tasks are mostly independent work and video meetings, or you can be in the office for specific moments such as quarterly planning. Avoid reasons that sound like a complaint about the commute alone.

## Scripts you can adapt

Keep requests short, warm and written. A written request creates a record and gives the hiring manager something to forward.

### Script 1: The first ask (email to the recruiter or hiring manager)

> Thank you again for the offer. I'm excited about the role and the team. Before I accept, I'd like to discuss work location. The offer describes four days a week in the office. I do my best work with a mostly remote setup, and I'm confident I can deliver the same results with two office days a week, or with in-person time focused on key meetings. Would that be possible? I'm happy to talk through how I'd keep collaboration strong.

### Script 2: When they say "we need to check"

> That makes sense. Is there anything I can provide to help with that decision, such as how I'd handle communication or the days I'd plan to be on site? I'd like to finalize my decision by [date], if that works for you.

### Script 3: When the answer is "not now"

> Understood. Would it be reasonable to agree on a review after [three or six months], with the goal of moving to [your preferred arrangement] if things are going well? I'd like that written into the offer so we're both clear on the plan.

### Script 4: When they agree verbally

> That's great news, thank you. Could you please update the offer letter so it reflects the agreed arrangement? I'd like the contract to state [X days in the office / remote work from my home location] and what notice would apply if either side wants to change it.

Notice the last script. A verbal yes is not an agreement you can rely on later. The goal is always to convert it into text.

## The wording that lets an employer reverse a "yes"

Employers often agree to flexibility in conversation and then include language that preserves their right to change it. This is not necessarily bad faith. Many companies write policies to keep options open. But you should know what you are accepting.

| If the document says | What it may mean | What to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| "Your place of work is the London/Chicago/Sydney office" | Remote work is a permission, not a term of your job | A clause naming your home as a regular place of work, or a stated hybrid pattern |
| "Remote work is at the company's discretion" | They can withdraw it at any time | Notice period before changes, and a reason for any change |
| "Subject to company policy as updated from time to time" | A later policy change can override what was agreed | The agreed arrangement stated as an individual term that prevails over general policy |
| "Hybrid, as business needs require" | The number of office days can rise without discussion | A minimum-flexibility statement, such as "no more than X days" |
| "You may be required to relocate or attend any company site" | Mobility clause that could undo your arrangement | Limits on location changes, or a carve-out for your agreed arrangement |
| "Remote work is available during the probation period only on approval" | Flexibility may disappear while you are most vulnerable | Approval confirmed now, not later |

### Other clauses to read carefully

- **Work location and eligibility:** Some policies limit remote work to certain states, provinces or countries, often for tax, payroll or legal-entity reasons. Confirm that your home location qualifies and that moving later would not break the arrangement.
- **Policies incorporated by reference:** If the offer says you must follow the remote work policy or handbook, ask to read it before you sign. If you can't see it, you can't know what you have accepted.
- **Equipment and expenses:** Check whether the employer provides a laptop and monitor, and whether any home-office stipend or internet reimbursement exists. If you've negotiated this, get it in writing.
- **Travel expectations:** "Occasional travel" can mean very different things. Ask for an estimate, who pays, and how far ahead you get notice.
- **Core hours and time zones:** For remote roles across regions, check whether you must work specific hours.
- **Termination and notice:** Read how either side can end the arrangement and what notice applies. This matters most if you are relocating or have given up another option to take the job.
- **Probation or trial wording:** If remote status is tied to a probation period, ask what "success" means and who decides.

### The strongest protection: a specific, individual term

A line in a general policy can change for everyone. A line in your offer letter or contract about your personal arrangement is harder to change. A useful clause might read:

> "The Employee's primary place of work is their home address as notified to the Company. The Employee will attend the [city] office up to [two] days per month for planned team events. Any change to this arrangement requires [30] days' written notice and prior discussion."

Treat this as an illustration, not legal text. Employment contracts differ by country and region, so have an employment lawyer or a relevant advisory service review anything significant, especially if you are relocating or turning down another offer.

## Your legal position differs by country

In most cases you are negotiating, not asserting a right. Rights to request flexibility vary, and a right to ask is not a right to receive.

- **United States:** There is no general federal right to work remotely. Employment is commonly at-will unless your contract says otherwise, so a verbal promise is weak protection. If a disability or medical condition is involved, you may be able to ask for a reasonable accommodation. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission explains how that works.
- **Canada:** Rules are set mainly by province or territory for most employees, and by federal rules for federally regulated employers. Check the standards where you work. Written terms still matter. If a core term like location is changed unilaterally later, that can raise legal questions, so get advice before assuming how it would play out.
- **Australia:** Under the Fair Work Act, eligible employees can request flexible working arrangements, and employers must respond in writing and can refuse only on reasonable business grounds. Eligibility rules, such as length of service, apply, so check the Fair Work Ombudsman's guidance for your situation. A new hire may not yet be eligible, which makes a written agreement in the offer more important.
- **United Kingdom:** Employees can make a statutory flexible working request from their first day of employment. Employers must handle it reasonably and can refuse only for specified business reasons. The right is to make a request, and the number of requests you can make in a year is limited, so don't use one carelessly. Negotiating in the offer first is often cleaner.

Always check current rules on the official government pages, since employment law changes.

## If you have already accepted

If you accepted before raising it, you can still ask. Wait until you have started or are close to your start date, and frame it as a specific proposal with a review date. Offer evidence: your output, your reachability, and your plan for in-person moments. In the UK and Australia, check whether a formal flexible working request is available to you and what the timeline is.

Expect a lower chance of success than if you had asked before signing. That is why the pre-signature window matters.

## A pre-signature checklist

- The offer or contract states your location arrangement in specific terms.
- Any "discretion" or "as updated" wording is limited by notice or consultation.
- You've read every policy the contract refers to.
- Your home location is eligible, now and if you move.
- Equipment, expenses and travel are stated, not implied.
- Probation or trial conditions are clear.
- The verbal agreement matches the written one.

If the employer won't put anything in writing, treat that as information. You can still accept, but do so knowing the arrangement could change, and weigh the offer on its written terms alone.
