If you have spent years inside a close cycle, you already know how a business really reports its numbers. That is a head start. It is not the whole journey. Finance roles ask a different question: not "is this right?" but "what should we do next, and what is it worth?"

This guide separates what carries over from what you must learn from scratch. It then lays out a month-by-month plan. The timeline assumes roughly 8–10 focused hours a week alongside a full-time job. The month ranges are my planning estimates, not measured averages. Your pace will vary with your starting Excel level and how much your current role exposes you to forecasting.

## What carries over, and what doesn't

| Area | Carries over? | Rough time to close the gap |
|---|---|---|
| Three-statement fluency, accruals, revenue recognition | Yes, a major advantage | Little to none |
| Reconciliations, controls, attention to detail | Yes | None |
| Month-end variance explanations | Partly (backward-looking) | 1–2 months to make them forward-looking |
| Excel | Partly (you know ledgers, not models) | 2–3 months |
| Budgeting and forecasting | Partly | 2–3 months |
| Three-statement projection models | No, new build skill | 2–3 months |
| Valuation (DCF, comparables, precedent deals) | No, learn from scratch | 3–4 months |
| Capital structure, cost of capital, returns analysis | No | 2–3 months, overlapping with valuation |
| Market and industry judgment | No | Ongoing, start in month 1 |

Several of these stages overlap, so the full path is closer to 9–12 months than the sum of the ranges.

## Months 1–2: Audit yourself and pick a target

Start by deciding which door you're walking through, because the skill mix differs.

- **Corporate finance / FP&A:** budgeting, forecasting, business partnering. This is the most natural first move for accountants and the one most likely to be offered remotely.
- **Corporate development and treasury:** M&A support, financing, cash management. It needs more valuation and capital-structure knowledge.
- **Investment roles (equity research, credit, buy-side, investment banking):** the heaviest on valuation and the most competitive. Many are in-office, especially at junior levels.

Write down your top three target titles. Pull ten current postings for each and highlight the repeated requirements. That list becomes your syllabus.

Also check your credentials in the context of your region. A CPA (US), CPA Canada designation, CA ANZ or CPA Australia membership, or ACA, ACCA or CIMA in the UK all signal rigor. None of them replaces finance-specific proof, but they open doors in controllership-adjacent finance roles. For occupational context, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook has separate profiles for accountants and for financial analysts, with typical duties and education.

**Deliverable by end of month 2:** a one-page gap list and a target-role shortlist.

## Months 2–4: Rebuild your Excel and forecasting habits

Accounting spreadsheets are built to tie out. Finance models are built to flex. Practice the shift:

1. Separate inputs, calculations and outputs on different, clearly labeled areas.
2. Replace hardcoded numbers with assumptions you can change in one cell.
3. Learn lookup functions (XLOOKUP or INDEX/MATCH), scenario switches, data tables and basic charting.
4. Build a simple driver-based budget: units × price, headcount × cost, and so on.

Then change how you talk about variances. At month-end you probably explain what happened. Start adding two sentences: why it happened, and what it implies for the next quarter. Do this in your current job if you can. Offer to help with the forecast, the board deck or a budget refresh. This is the cheapest and most credible bridge you have.

**Deliverable:** a driver-based forecast for a real or public company, plus one variance memo that looks forward.

## Months 4–6: Learn to build a three-statement model

This is where your accounting background pays off most. A projected income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement that balance and link properly is exactly what you've spent years checking.

Build one from a public company's filings:

- Pull three to five years of history from annual reports (in the US, SEC filings; in Canada, SEDAR+; in Australia, ASX announcements; in the UK, Companies House or the company's investor page).
- Project revenue from drivers, not growth percentages alone.
- Forecast working capital, capex, depreciation, debt and interest.
- Make sure the balance sheet balances and cash flows tie to the balance sheet.

Expect the first model to be slow and flawed. The second is much faster. Budget for building at least two, in different industries, such as a software company and a retailer.

**Deliverable:** two clean models you can walk through in ten minutes.

## Months 6–9: Valuation from scratch

Be honest with yourself: this is the new skill, and accounting rarely teaches it. Valuation is about estimating what future cash flows are worth today and cross-checking against what the market pays for similar businesses.

Learn these in order:

1. **Time value of money and cost of capital.** Discounting, WACC, cost of equity, and why the inputs are judgments rather than facts.
2. **Discounted cash flow (DCF).** Free cash flow, terminal value, and the sensitivity of the answer to your assumptions. Build sensitivity tables so you can show a range, not a single point.
3. **Trading comparables.** Choosing a peer set, calculating multiples such as EV/EBITDA and P/E, and normalizing for accounting differences. Here your accounting knowledge helps again: you'll spot lease treatment, one-offs and capitalized costs that distort multiples.
4. **Precedent transactions and, for private-equity-style roles, a simple LBO.** Only if your targets need them.

Courses, textbooks and structured programs can all work. A professional program such as the CFA Program is a respected route to investment knowledge, but it is a significant, multi-year commitment. Consider it if you're aiming at portfolio management or research. For FP&A and corporate development, a strong portfolio often matters more than another credential.

**Deliverable:** a valuation of one public company, written up as a short memo with a recommendation, key assumptions and what would change your mind.

## Months 9–10: Add commercial and market judgment

Valuation without context is arithmetic. Begin a weekly habit of reading earnings releases and transcripts, then asking: What drives this business? What would break the forecast? What did management emphasize, and what did they avoid?

Pick two or three industries and learn their metrics: subscription businesses care about retention and unit economics; manufacturers about margins and inventory; banks about capital and credit quality. Your accounting training lets you read footnotes that others skip, which is a genuine edge in credit and equity research.

**Deliverable:** three short company write-ups you'd be comfortable sending to a hiring manager.

## Months 10–12: Position, network and interview

Now convert the work into a story.

### Rewrite your résumé

Lead with outcomes in finance language. "Reduced close by two days" is fine, but add "built a rolling forecast used by the CFO" or "prepared the variance analysis behind the quarterly outlook." If you haven't done these, say what you built in your portfolio, labeled clearly as independent work.

### Build a small portfolio

Share two models and one memo, with sensitive data removed. Use public companies only. Put them in a simple, tidy folder or document you can link to.

### Network deliberately

Ask people one or two steps ahead of you, ideally accountants who moved into finance, for 20 minutes. Ask what their first finance role actually involved and what they wish they'd known. Don't ask for a job in the first message.

### Prepare for interviews

Expect three types of questions:

- **Technical:** walk me through a DCF; how does a change in depreciation flow through the three statements; why might two companies with the same EBITDA have different values?
- **Behavioral:** why are you leaving accounting, and what have you done to prepare?
- **Case or modeling test:** often a timed Excel exercise. Practice under a clock.

## A note on remote-first searches

Remote opportunities are not evenly spread. FP&A, financial planning, revenue operations finance, and corporate finance roles at distributed companies are the likeliest remote-friendly targets. Junior investment banking and many buy-side seats tend to expect in-person work, so check each posting for location requirements and time-zone overlap.

If you're applying across borders, confirm work authorization and whether the employer can legally hire in your country. Also check how your accounting designation is recognized there. Don't assume a US-only role will pay or hire the same way in Canada, Australia or the UK.

## Common detours that cost months

- **Collecting certificates without building models.** Proof of work beats proof of attendance.
- **Skipping the forecast stage and jumping straight to valuation.** Your DCF is only as good as the projections underneath it.
- **Applying only to investment roles.** A stepping-stone role in FP&A or corporate development is a legitimate path and can lead to investing later.
- **Hiding your accounting background.** Present it as the reason your models are clean and your assumptions are grounded in reported numbers.

## The short version

Your accounting skills cover the reporting layer. Over 9–12 months, add forward-looking modeling, valuation and market judgment, and prove each with a deliverable. Start with the closest role, FP&A or corporate finance, and let the investment path open from there.
