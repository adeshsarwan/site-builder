You apply to a remote role on Monday. On Thursday a recruiter emails to say, "Thanks for your interest in the role." You can't remember which role, which resume you sent, or whether you already followed up. Or you find the same posting on a second job board and apply again, which can make you look disorganized.

Remote-first job seekers are especially prone to this. Your pool of employers isn't limited by city, so the volume climbs fast. Postings get re-listed, recruiters at agencies post the same job under different names, and replies arrive across email, LinkedIn and applicant-system portals.

The fix doesn't need an app subscription. A well-built application log takes about 20 minutes to set up and a few minutes per application to maintain. This guide walks through what to put in it.

## Why a log beats "I'll remember"

A log does three jobs:

1. **Prevents duplicates.** Before you apply, you search the sheet. If the company and role are already there, you stop.
2. **Records what you sent.** When an interview invitation arrives, you can open the exact resume and cover letter the employer saw.
3. **Tells you when to act.** Follow-ups happen on a schedule instead of when you feel guilty.

It also gives you something useful when a search drags on: evidence of what's working. If you've sent 40 applications with one resume version and heard nothing, and 15 with another and got several replies, you have a reason to change course. Keep that analysis to your own data; a small sample won't prove anything, but it will point you somewhere.

## Choose a tool you'll actually open

Use whatever you already have: Google Sheets, Excel, Notion, Airtable or a plain table in a notes app. The best tool is the one you'll update the same day you apply. A spreadsheet has two advantages: it's easy to filter and sort, and it's easy to export if you change tools later.

Create one row per application, not per company. If you apply to two different roles at the same employer, that's two rows, linked by the company name.

## The columns to include

Start with the core columns. Add the optional ones only if you'll really use them.

| Column | What to enter | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Date applied | Use YYYY-MM-DD, for example 2025-03-14 | Sorts correctly and avoids day/month confusion across countries |
| Company | Employer's name | Duplicate check |
| Role title | Exact title from the posting | Titles are often re-posted with small changes |
| Posting link | URL, plus the job ID if there is one | The listing may disappear; the ID helps you find it again |
| Source | Company site, job board, referral, recruiter | Shows which channels produce replies |
| Status | One value from a fixed list (see below) | Lets you filter to what needs action |
| Resume version | Your version code (see below) | Tells you which file they received |
| Contact | Recruiter or hiring manager name and email | Saves digging through your inbox |
| Next action | A short phrase, such as "Follow up" | Turns the log into a to-do list |
| Next action date | Date | Drives your reminders |
| Notes | Anything specific | Pay range, interview details, red flags |

### Optional columns for remote searches

- **Location eligibility:** Many "remote" roles are limited to certain states, provinces, countries or time zones. Note what the posting says, such as "US only" or "within 3 hours of Eastern time." This stops you from chasing roles you can't take.
- **Work authorization or hiring entity:** If a role requires you to be employed through a contractor arrangement or a local entity, note it.
- **Pay range and currency:** Useful when postings list ranges in different currencies or in hourly versus annual terms.
- **Time zone for interviews:** Write the recruiter's time zone next to any scheduled call.
- **Application method:** Applicant-system form, email, referral or recruiter submission.

## Set up status columns that mean something

A fixed list of statuses lets you filter and count. Use a dropdown (data validation in Sheets or Excel) so you can't type free text and end up with five spellings of "waiting."

A workable list:

- **Saved:** You found the role and haven't applied yet.
- **Applied:** Submitted; no response.
- **Acknowledged:** Automated or human confirmation received.
- **Screening:** Recruiter or phone screen scheduled or completed.
- **Interviewing:** One or more interview rounds in progress.
- **Offer:** Verbal or written offer received.
- **Rejected:** The employer said no.
- **Withdrawn:** You pulled out.
- **Closed/No response:** You've followed up and given up on it.

Keep the list short. Ten or more statuses become something you maintain instead of something you use.

### Use conditional formatting

Color-code the status column. For instance, gray for Saved, blue for Applied, yellow for Screening and Interviewing, green for Offer, and light red for Rejected. When you open the sheet, you should see where the live opportunities are within seconds.

## Track resume and cover letter versions

Losing track of which resume you sent is one of the most common problems in a high-volume search, and it matters most when an interview is scheduled and you need to review what you claimed.

### Name files consistently

Pick a naming pattern and stick with it. For example:

`Lastname_Resume_ProjectManager_v3_2025-03.pdf`

Include your target role family, a version number and a month. In the UK and Australia, you might call it a CV; the principle is the same.

### Add a version notes tab

Create a second tab in your spreadsheet called "Versions." Give each version one row with:

- **Version code:** For example, PM-v3.
- **Date created:**
- **What changed:** "Moved certifications to top; added a remote-collaboration bullet; removed older role."
- **Target roles:** "Project manager, operations."
- **File location:** Folder path or a share link.

Then, in the main log, the Resume version column holds just the code ("PM-v3"). When you tailor a resume for a specific posting, save it as a new minor version (PM-v3a) and note what you altered, such as keywords you mirrored from the job description. Don't claim anything in a tailored version that isn't true; the point is to emphasize relevant experience, not invent it.

### Save what you submitted

Many applicant systems don't let you view your submission afterward. Save a PDF of the final resume and cover letter in a folder named for the company, and copy the job description into your notes or save it as a PDF. Postings often vanish after they close, and you'll want the original wording to prepare for interviews.

## Build in reminders

The log only works if it tells you what to do next. There are two simple ways to do this.

### Option 1: A "Next action date" filter

Each time you update a row, set the next action and its date. Once a day (or at the start of each work session), filter for dates that are today or earlier. That list is your to-do list.

In Google Sheets, a formula such as `=IF(AND(K2<>"",K2<=TODAY()),"DUE","")` in a helper column can flag due rows. Adjust the column letter to match your sheet.

### Option 2: Calendar events

For interviews and deadlines, add a calendar event with the link to the row. Use calendar notifications for anything with a fixed time. Put the time zone in the event so a remote interview doesn't catch you off guard.

### A sensible follow-up rhythm

There's no universal rule, but a steady pattern keeps you consistent:

- **After applying:** If the posting gives a timeline, wait until it passes. If not, a gap of roughly one to two weeks is a reasonable time to send a short, polite check-in, especially if you have a named contact.
- **After an interview:** Send a thank-you note within a day or so. If the employer gave a decision date, follow up a day or two after it passes. If they didn't, ask at the end of the interview what the timeline is, and record the answer in the log.
- **After a follow-up with no reply:** Set the next action to one final nudge, then mark the application as Closed/No response and move on.

Write the follow-up date when you apply, not later. Deciding in advance removes the temptation to check your inbox every hour.

## Add a quick pre-application check

Before submitting, spend 30 seconds on this routine:

1. Search the log for the company name.
2. Search for the role title and the job ID.
3. If the role is already there, read the notes. Don't reapply unless you have a good reason, such as a materially different role or an invitation to reapply.
4. If it's new, add the row first with status "Saved," then apply, then change it to "Applied."

Adding the row before you apply is the habit that keeps the log complete. If you try to catch up at the end of the week, you'll forget things.

## Keep a weekly review

Set aside 15 minutes once a week, perhaps Friday afternoon. Go through the sheet and:

- Update any statuses that changed.
- Check for rows with overdue next actions.
- Close out applications that have gone quiet after your follow-up.
- Note which source or resume version is producing replies.
- Decide how many new applications you'll send next week and what kind.

This review is also a good moment to look at quality. If you're applying to dozens of roles but few match your skills or location eligibility, tighten your filters rather than adding more volume.

## Protect your data

Your log contains names, emails and compensation notes. Keep it in an account protected by a strong password and two-step verification. Don't store government ID numbers, bank details or passwords in it. If a posting asks you for sensitive information early in the process, treat that as a warning sign and verify the employer before sharing anything. Government consumer-protection agencies in each country publish guidance on job scams if you need a refresher.

## A minimal starter setup

If you want to begin in the next ten minutes, do this:

1. Create a new sheet with these headings: Date applied, Company, Role, Link, Status, Resume version, Contact, Next action, Next action date, Notes.
2. Add a dropdown to Status with the list above.
3. Add a second tab called Versions with a row for your current resume.
4. Enter every application you can remember from the past two weeks, even if the details are incomplete. Dig through your sent folder to fill gaps.
5. Set next action dates for anything older than a week with no reply.

You can add columns later. A simple log you update beats an elaborate one you abandon.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Logging in batches.** Update the sheet at the moment you apply.
- **Free-text statuses.** Use a dropdown.
- **Vague notes.** "Talked to recruiter" is less useful than "Recruiter Sam said team decides by the 20th; asked about on-call."
- **Not recording the source.** Without it, you can't tell which channels are worth your time.
- **Overwriting versions.** Make a new version code every time you materially change the resume.
- **Skipping the closed-out step.** Rows left on "Applied" forever bury the ones that matter.

A good application log is dull, and that's its strength. It answers three questions in seconds: Have I already applied here? Which resume did they see? What should I do today? Once those answers are reliable, you can spend your energy on the part that actually gets you hired: writing sharper applications and preparing well for the conversations that follow.
