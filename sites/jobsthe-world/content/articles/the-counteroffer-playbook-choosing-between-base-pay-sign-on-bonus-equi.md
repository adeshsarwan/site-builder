An offer lands in your inbox. It's close, but not quite right. You know you can negotiate. What you don't know is *what* to negotiate, and how to ask without sounding greedy or stalling the process.

This playbook gives you a way to choose the lever, a way to phrase the ask, and a few remote-specific wrinkles that matter if your employer sits in a different country or state from you.

## Step 1: Work out what the offer is actually missing

Before you counter, spend ten minutes on three questions.

1. **What is the gap?** Compare the offer with your current total pay, your target, and what similar roles advertise. If you can't find ranges, many US states and some other jurisdictions require pay ranges in postings, so check the original listing.
2. **What does this company have room to move on?** A funded startup may have equity to spare but a rigid salary band. A large employer may have fixed bands but a discretionary sign-on budget.
3. **What do you need in the next 12 months versus the next five years?** A mortgage deposit and a lifelong salary trajectory call for different asks.

Write down your answers. The rest of this article tells you how to turn them into a counter.

## Step 2: Choose your lever

Each part of a package does a different job. Here is a quick comparison.

| Lever | Best for closing | Why it's easier or harder to win | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base pay | A long-term gap in earnings | Often tied to a band, so it can be the hardest to move far | Little room if you're already at the top of the band |
| Sign-on bonus | A one-off gap (lost bonus, unvested equity, relocation of a home office) | Usually a one-time cost, so approvals are easier | Often has a clawback if you leave early; doesn't compound |
| Equity | Upside if the company grows | Flexible at startups; more rigid at public companies | Value is uncertain and vesting ties you in |
| Extra leave | Time, flexibility, burnout protection | Costs the company little in cash | Can be informal or hard to take if the culture is overworked |

### When to push on base pay

Base pay is the default lever for a reason. It feeds into future raises, bonus percentages, pension or retirement contributions, and your next employer's offer. If the number is below what you'd accept as a fair long-term salary, start here.

Push on base when:
- The offer is below the range you were told or the range posted.
- You have a competing offer or clear evidence of market rates.
- The employer uses a percentage of base for bonus or retirement matching, which magnifies every dollar.

If the recruiter says the band is capped, that isn't the end of the conversation. It's your cue to move to a different lever.

### When to push on a sign-on bonus

A sign-on bonus is the cleanest way to bridge a specific, explainable gap. You are leaving an unpaid annual bonus, unvested shares, or a commission payout on the table. The employer can reasonably say yes without disturbing the salary of colleagues at the same level.

Ask for it when:
- You can name the amount you're giving up.
- The recruiter says base pay is firm.
- You want cash sooner rather than later.

Read the repayment terms. Many sign-on bonuses must be repaid if you leave within a set period. Ask how long and whether repayment is pro-rated.

### When to push on equity

Equity makes sense when you believe in the company and the offer's cash is already acceptable. At a startup, ask about the number of shares or options, the percentage of the company they represent, the strike price (for options), the vesting schedule, and what happens at an acquisition. A grant in "shares" means little without the total number outstanding.

At a public company, equity is usually granted by level, so you may be able to move it only modestly, often by asking for a larger initial grant rather than a different structure.

Tax treatment of equity differs a lot between the US, Canada, Australia and the UK, and it can change again if you're an employee in one country working for a company in another. Get advice from a qualified tax professional before you assume a grant is worth what the headline says.

### When to push on extra leave

Leave is the lever most people forget, and it's often the cheapest for the employer to give. It suits you if your pay is acceptable but your life needs more room: caring responsibilities, study, long-haul trips to see family, or recovery time from a demanding job.

Know your baseline first:
- **UK:** Statutory paid holiday is 5.6 weeks, which can include bank holidays, according to [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/holiday-entitlement-rights).
- **Australia:** The National Employment Standards give most full-time employees four weeks of paid annual leave per year, according to the [Fair Work Ombudsman](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/leave/annual-leave).
- **US:** There is no federal requirement for paid vacation, per the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/workhours/vacation_leave), so it's wholly negotiable.
- **Canada:** Minimums vary by province or territory, and federally regulated employers follow different rules. Check your local standard.

Ask for a specific number of days, written into the offer, not a vague "flexible time off" promise. Unlimited-leave policies can be hard to use, so ask what the average team member actually takes.

## Step 3: Remote-first factors that change the math

If your job is remote, add these to your checklist.

- **Location-based pay.** Some employers set pay by where you live. Ask how your band is determined and whether it changes if you move.
- **Currency and payroll.** Confirm the currency of your salary, how bonuses and equity are paid, and whether you're an employee or contractor. Contractors typically handle their own tax, leave and benefits, so a higher headline rate may not be a better deal.
- **Equipment and workspace.** A home-office stipend, equipment budget or coworking allowance can be quick wins.
- **Time zones and hours.** If the role needs overlap with a distant time zone, that's a legitimate reason to ask for extra pay, flexible hours or additional rest days.
- **Benefits that don't travel.** Health coverage, pension or retirement contributions and parental leave differ by country. Ask exactly what applies to you.

## Step 4: Build a counter that keeps things moving

Recruiters want to close. They also have their own constraints: a hiring manager waiting, a budget approval, a deadline. A good counter makes their job easier.

### The structure

1. **Express enthusiasm in one sentence.** Make it specific and true.
2. **State your ask with a number.** Vague asks invite vague answers.
3. **Give one reason.** Tie it to the market, your experience, or a specific gap.
4. **Ask a forward-looking question.** This keeps the process in motion.

### Script: asking for higher base pay

> "Thanks for the offer. I'm excited about the role, particularly the chance to lead the onboarding redesign. Based on the scope and my experience running similar projects, I was hoping for a base of $X. Is there flexibility to get closer to that number? If we can agree on it, I'm ready to move forward."

### Script: the base is capped, so pivot to a sign-on bonus

> "I understand the band is firm. I'm leaving a bonus of about $Y that I'd forfeit if I resign before it pays out. Would a one-time sign-on payment to cover part of that be possible?"

### Script: asking for equity

> "I'm very interested in the long-term opportunity here. Could we look at increasing the initial grant to N shares? It would help me feel aligned with where the company is heading. Could you also share the current number of fully diluted shares and the vesting schedule?"

### Script: asking for extra leave

> "The salary works for me. One thing that would make this an easy yes is an additional five days of paid leave per year, bringing me to X total. Is that something we can add?"

### Script: offering a package of options

Giving the recruiter a choice can speed up approval.

> "If the base can't move, I'd be happy with either a sign-on bonus of $Z or an extra week of leave. Which is easier on your side?"

## Step 5: Keep the process moving

A few habits protect your relationship and your timeline.

- **Respond quickly.** Counter within a day or two, and suggest a call if the recruiter prefers talking.
- **Negotiate once, thoroughly.** Bundle your asks in one message so you don't come back with a second round.
- **Ask for the deadline.** "When do you need my decision by?" gives you a clear window.
- **Get it in writing.** Verbal promises vanish when the hiring manager changes. Ask for a revised offer letter.
- **Don't bluff.** Only mention a competing offer if it exists, and don't give a number you'd turn down.
- **Be ready for a no.** Decide your walk-away point ahead of time. If they say no to everything, you can still accept, ask for a salary review at six months, or decline politely.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Asking for everything at once.** A long wish list can read as a refusal to accept any offer. Choose your top one or two.
- **Naming a number before you've done the research.** Anchor to evidence, not a hunch.
- **Treating equity as cash.** Until you can sell shares, they are not money in the bank.
- **Ignoring the fine print.** Clawbacks, vesting cliffs and notice terms matter as much as the headline numbers.
- **Going silent.** A pause of several days can make the employer assume you've lost interest.

## A quick decision guide

- If the **salary is below market**, push on base.
- If the **base is capped** and you're losing something specific, push on a sign-on bonus.
- If the **cash is fine** and you believe in the company, push on equity.
- If **pay is fine** and your life needs more breathing room, push on leave.
- If you're **fully remote**, add a stipend or equipment budget to any of the above.

You don't need to win every point. A good negotiation leaves you with an offer you can accept without resentment and a recruiter who still looks forward to working with you.
