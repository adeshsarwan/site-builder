Resume length causes more second-guessing than almost any other formatting decision. The honest answer is that neither page count is automatically right. A one-page resume that leaves out your strongest evidence loses to a tight two-pager, and a padded two-pager loses to a sharp single page.

This guide gives you practical rules based on how much relevant experience you have and what kind of role you're targeting, then shows exactly what to cut when you're over and what to keep when you're tempted to trim too far.

## The Core Rule: Earn the Second Page

A second page is justified when it contains **recent, relevant, specific evidence** that the first page can't hold. It is not justified because you've worked for a long time, or because you want to include everything.

A useful test: cover the second page and read only the first. Does the reader still understand who you are, what you do and why you're a fit? If yes, the second page is a bonus. If the first page is a teaser that only makes sense with page two, restructure it.

Also remember who reads your resume. A recruiter on a remote-first hiring team may be looking at dozens of applications in a sitting, often on a laptop or phone, and applicant tracking systems will parse your file before a person ever sees it. Both audiences reward clarity, not volume.

## Quick Reference: Length by Experience Level

| Situation | Recommended length | Keep | Cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student, new graduate, career changer with little related experience | 1 page | Projects, internships, relevant coursework, skills, volunteer work | High school details, irrelevant part-time jobs, long objective statements |
| Roughly 1–5 years of relevant experience | 1 page | Quantified achievements from the last 2–3 roles, core tools | Duties anyone in the role would have, older unrelated jobs |
| Roughly 5–10 years, steady progression | 1–2 pages | Last 2–4 roles in depth, promotions, scope of work, results | Early-career detail, outdated tools, generic skill lists |
| 10+ years, senior individual contributor or manager | 2 pages | Leadership scope, measurable outcomes, strategic projects | Roles beyond roughly 10–15 years back (reduce to a single line), minor certifications |
| Executive or director level | 2 pages, occasionally slightly more | Business impact, team size, budget or portfolio scope | Task-level detail, early roles |
| Academic, research or some scientific roles | CV format, longer is normal | Publications, grants, teaching | Anything not tied to the role |

Treat the years in the table as guides, not thresholds. Ten years of repetitive work might fit on one page, while four years across varied, high-impact roles might need more room.

## When One Page Is the Right Call

Stick to one page if any of these apply:

- **You're early in your career.** Entry-level hiring managers expect a single page and know you haven't had time to accumulate much.
- **You're changing fields.** Much of your history may not transfer. A focused page that highlights transferable skills beats two pages of loosely related duties.
- **Your career is short and simple.** If you've held one or two roles, filling a second page invites filler.
- **The role is highly specific.** If you're applying for a narrowly defined position, a targeted single page shows you've read the posting.

On one page, use the space deliberately. Lead with a short summary of two to three lines, followed by experience, then skills and education. Skip the objective statement unless you're making a career change and need to explain it in one sentence.

## When Two Pages Make Sense

A second page is reasonable when:

- **You have five-plus years of directly relevant experience** and the strongest bullets from your last few roles genuinely don't fit on one page.
- **You've managed people, budgets, or complex projects** and need space to show scope as well as results.
- **Your field expects detail.** Engineering, data, healthcare, finance, project management and similar roles often benefit from listing specific tools, methods, or certifications.
- **You've held consistent, progressively senior roles** and the progression itself is part of your story.

If you go to two pages, make the second page substantial. A few dangling lines on page two look like a formatting accident. Either fill at least half the page with valuable content or tighten back to one.

## Role Type Matters as Much as Years

### Technical and Engineering Roles

Hiring teams want to see technologies, scale and outcomes. Two pages is common for experienced candidates, but resist cataloging every tool you've ever touched. Group skills by category and prioritize the stack named in the job posting. Link to a portfolio, repository or case studies instead of describing every project in full.

### Creative and Design Roles

Your portfolio does the heavy lifting. Keep the resume to one page for most of your career, using it as a clean summary that points to your work. Senior creative leads may use a second page for team and client scope.

### Sales, Marketing and Customer Success

Numbers carry weight here: quotas, pipeline, retention, campaign results. If your strongest metrics span several roles, two pages can work. Otherwise, one page with sharp quantified bullets is usually enough.

### Operations, Project and Program Management

These roles often involve scale, so two pages is reasonable at mid-career and beyond. Show the size of what you ran, such as teams, budgets, timelines, and the results you delivered.

### Government and Federal Applications

Federal resumes in the US often require more detail than private-sector ones, and some postings specify what to include. Follow the instructions in the announcement rather than general length rules. Applications for other public-sector roles can have their own formats too, so always check the posting.

## Regional Notes for US, Canada, Australia and the UK

Conventions overlap, but there are differences worth knowing:

- **United States:** One page for early career, one to two pages after that. Leave off photos, date of birth and marital status.
- **Canada:** Similar to the US. Many employers use the word "resume," and one to two pages is typical. Bilingual candidates can note language skills clearly.
- **United Kingdom:** The document is usually called a CV, and two pages is the common expectation for most professionals. Early-career candidates can still do well with one.
- **Australia:** Resumes are often a little longer than in the US, and a two- or three-page document is not unusual for experienced professionals. Even so, a tight two pages generally reads better. Some applications, especially in government, ask for responses to selection criteria separately, so keep the resume itself focused.

If you're applying across regions, keep one master document and create a trimmed version for each market rather than padding or squeezing a single file.

## What to Cut When You're Over

Work through this list in order until you reach your target length:

1. **Objective statements and generic summaries.** Replace with a two-to-three-line summary of what you do and your strongest result.
2. **Duties without outcomes.** If a bullet says "responsible for reporting," either add what the reporting led to or delete it.
3. **Roles older than 10–15 years.** Condense them into a single "Earlier experience" line with titles and employers, or drop them if they're irrelevant.
4. **Duplicate bullets.** If three roles have nearly identical bullets, keep the strongest version.
5. **Outdated or basic skills.** Everyone is expected to know email and basic office software. Cut them unless the posting asks for them specifically.
6. **"References available upon request."** It's assumed.
7. **Small certifications and courses** that don't relate to the target role.
8. **Personal interests,** unless they're genuinely relevant or a conversation starter you want to keep.

Don't fix length by shrinking the font below a readable size or cutting margins to a sliver. Stay at a comfortable body size and keep clean spacing. If the page looks crowded, it will be skimmed rather than read.

## What to Keep, Even When Space Is Tight

- **Quantified results** that show scope and impact.
- **Keywords from the job posting** that honestly match your experience, in context rather than stuffed into a skills dump.
- **Recent roles in depth,** because hiring teams care most about your last few years.
- **Clear job titles, employers and dates** so your history is easy to follow.
- **Evidence of the skill the role actually needs,** even if it came from a side project or volunteer work.

## Remote-First Details Worth the Space

For remote roles, a few extra lines can answer questions employers have before they ask:

- **Location and time zone.** A city, region or time zone in your header helps teams plan overlap.
- **Work authorization,** if relevant and appropriate for the country you're applying in.
- **Remote or distributed experience.** Note when you worked on distributed teams, and name the collaboration or project tools you used.
- **Written communication and self-direction.** Show these through results, such as documentation you created, processes you improved or projects you ran independently, rather than just claiming them.

These details are worth a line or two, but don't let them push you into an unnecessary second page.

## A Simple Process for Deciding

1. Build a full master resume with everything you might include.
2. Pick a target job and highlight the posting's top five requirements.
3. Keep only the bullets that prove those requirements, starting with your most recent roles.
4. Check length. If you land at one page and the content is strong, stop. If you land at one and a half to two pages of strong, relevant content, use two.
5. If the second page is less than half full, trim or tighten until it fits on one.
6. Save as a PDF unless the application asks otherwise, then check how it displays on a phone and in a plain-text preview.

The goal isn't a particular page count. It's a document where every line gives the reader a reason to keep going, and the length follows from that.
