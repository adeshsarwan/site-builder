You got the offer, and then you saw the footnote: pay is set by your location. The number is lower than you hoped, the recruiter says the band is "standard," and you are not sure what you can push on.

A location-based band is a starting position, not a verdict. Companies build these systems to keep payroll predictable, but exceptions, level changes and non-salary trades happen all the time. This guide shows how to find out how your band was built, research your own market, challenge the placement, and negotiate the parts of the package that vary most across the US, Canada, Australia and the UK.

## Step 1: Learn how the band was built

Before you argue with a number, understand what it represents. Ask the recruiter or hiring manager these questions, in writing if possible:

- **What is the band for this role and level, and where does my location sit within it?**
- **Is the band tied to where I live, where the company hires from, or a tier assigned to a city or region?**
- **Is the band a range with a midpoint, and where in the range is this offer?**
- **How often are location tiers reviewed?**
- **Do the bands apply equally to bonus, equity and benefits, or only to base salary?**

The answers tell you which lever to pull. If the offer sits at the bottom of the range, argue for a better position within it. If the tier itself looks wrong, argue for a different tier. If the policy is fixed, shift the conversation to other components.

Also check whether you are being hired as an employee or a contractor. That changes what you get in benefits, taxes and leave, and it makes offers hard to compare unless you account for it.

## Step 2: Research your own market

The company's pay tool is only one data source. Build your own picture from official and primary sources, and treat the results as ranges, not exact answers.

### Official salary data by country

- **US:** The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes occupational wage data by area through its Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics program (bls.gov/oes). CareerOneStop (careeronestop.org) has a salary lookup that lets you filter by occupation and state.
- **Canada:** Job Bank (jobbank.gc.ca) has wage reports by occupation and region.
- **UK:** The Office for National Statistics publishes the Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ons.gov.uk), which breaks down pay by occupation and region.
- **Australia:** Jobs and Skills Australia's Labour Market Insights pages (jobsandskills.gov.au) give occupation-level information for the Australian market.

These datasets are broad and lag behind current hiring. A software or marketing title in the data may cover many different seniority levels, so use them to establish a plausible range rather than to prove a single figure.

### Market-specific evidence

Official data gets you a range. These sources help you narrow it:

- **Pay-transparent job postings.** Some US jurisdictions require pay ranges in postings, and many employers elsewhere now include them. Collect postings for comparable remote roles and note the stated range, location rules and level.
- **Recruiter conversations.** Recruiters who place people in your field can tell you what companies are actually paying for your skills. Ask what they are seeing for people with your experience.
- **Peers and professional communities.** Ask people at a similar level, in your country, what total compensation looks like. Ask for ranges rather than exact salaries, and offer your own in return.
- **The company's own postings.** Compare the role's listed range with your offer. If the company has posted the same role for other locations, you have a direct reference point.

### Decide which market you belong to

The strongest argument is usually not "I live somewhere expensive." It is "The talent market for this skill is national or global, and I am competing in it." Think about where your skills are scarce, where else you could work, and whether the role is truly location-dependent. If your scope or seniority is higher than the offered level, that is a stronger argument than geography.

## Step 3: Build a case that does not depend on location

Employers rarely move on "I need more money." They move when you give the hiring manager a reason they can defend internally. Prepare three or four points:

1. **Scope and level.** Show how your experience matches a higher level than the one offered.
2. **Specific results.** Describe outcomes you have delivered that match the role's top priorities.
3. **Replacement cost.** A strong hire reduces the time and cost of an ongoing search.
4. **Alternatives.** If you hold another offer, you can mention it without giving details. If you do not, rely on market data.

If your location tier looks like a mismatch, bring evidence. For example, you may live in a regional hub whose labor market is similar to a higher-paid tier, or you may be willing to work during the company's core hours, which makes you more useful than a candidate in a distant time zone.

## Step 4: Scripts for challenging the band

Keep the tone collaborative. You are solving a problem together: how to get you to a number that works.

### Asking for the range

> "Thanks for the offer. I'm excited about the role. Could you share the full band for this level and where my location tier falls within it?"

### Challenging the position in the band

> "Based on my experience leading [specific work], I think I'm positioned toward the upper half of this band, not the lower end. Could we look at moving the base to [number]?"

### Challenging the tier

> "I understand the company uses location tiers. For this role, I'd be hiring into a talent market that competes at [different tier or national range]. Is there a process for reviewing the tier or approving an exception?"

### Asking for a level review

> "The scope we discussed, including [two responsibilities], feels closer to the next level. Could we revisit leveling before we finalize the offer?"

### Trading when base is fixed

> "If the base can't move, I'd like to look at the rest of the package. Could we discuss a signing bonus, additional equity, and a six-month compensation review?"

### Responding to "this is non-negotiable"

> "I appreciate you being direct. If the band is fixed, which parts of the package have flexibility? I'd like to find something that works for both of us."

### Raising a competing offer

> "I have another offer at [range or total]. I'd prefer to join your team because of [specific reason], and I'm hoping we can close the gap."

Only mention a competing offer if it is real, and avoid ultimatums. Ask for the revised offer in writing.

## Step 5: Compare offers across borders

A headline salary in different countries can mislead you. Compare total compensation in the same currency, and compare what you keep, not just what you are paid. A simple table helps:

| Component | What to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Base salary | Currency, pay frequency, review date | Currency and timing change real value |
| Bonus | Target, how it is calculated, past payout | Targets are not guarantees |
| Equity | Type, quantity, strike price, vesting, exercise window | Value can vary widely or be zero |
| Retirement | Employer contribution or match, vesting rules | Money you do not see in your paycheck |
| Health coverage | Premiums, deductibles, extended coverage | Differs sharply between countries |
| Leave | Vacation, sick, parental, public holidays | Statutory minimums differ by country |
| Employment type | Employee, contractor or employer of record | Affects benefits, tax and protections |
| Equipment and costs | Laptop, stipend, internet, co-working | Reduces your out-of-pocket costs |

### Country-specific points to check

- **US:** Look at employer health premium contributions and any 401(k) match. Ask about the plan's vesting rules.
- **Canada:** Public healthcare covers basics, but extended coverage (dental, vision, prescriptions) and any RRSP matching differ by employer. Ask what the plan covers.
- **UK:** Check the employer pension contribution and whether it exceeds the statutory minimum, as well as annual leave and any private medical cover.
- **Australia:** Confirm whether the quoted salary is *plus* superannuation or *inclusive* of it. This is a common source of confusion, and it can change your real pay noticeably.

For tax questions, speak to a qualified adviser in your country. Take-home pay depends on your situation, and a flat comparison of gross figures will not be accurate.

## Step 6: Negotiate currency

If the employer is in a different country from you, ask which currency you will be paid in. This is a real part of the negotiation, not a technicality.

- **Paid in your local currency:** The employer carries the exchange-rate risk. Ask how they converted the band and whether the figure will be reviewed if rates move significantly.
- **Paid in the employer's currency:** You carry the risk. A stronger or weaker currency can change your spending power without any change to your contract.

Questions worth asking:

- Which currency is the contract in, and which is the payroll currency?
- If the band was converted, what rate and date were used?
- Is there a periodic review to adjust for exchange-rate changes?
- Who pays transfer or conversion fees, if any?

If you are paid in a foreign currency, plan for volatility. Decide how much you can absorb, and consider asking for a currency review clause or a slightly higher base to offset the risk. Do not guess at rates in your negotiation. Check a current rate on the day you compare numbers.

## Step 7: Negotiate equity and benefits

Equity is often the most flexible component, and the easiest to misread.

### Questions for any equity grant

- How many shares or units, and what percentage of the company does that represent?
- What is the strike price, and when was the last valuation?
- What is the vesting schedule, and is there a cliff?
- What is the exercise window if I leave?
- Is the grant adjusted by location, or is it the same across tiers?
- How is equity taxed in my country? (Ask an adviser: rules differ widely.)

If equity is not location-adjusted, that is a point in your favor. Ask for a larger grant to offset a lower base, and ask for the number in writing.

### Other things you can ask for

- **Signing bonus** to bridge the gap in the first year.
- **Early review** at six months with defined criteria.
- **Higher level or title**, which affects future raises.
- **Additional leave** or a flexible schedule.
- **Home office, equipment or internet allowance.**
- **Professional development budget.**
- **Relocation policy clarity:** if you move, will pay change, and with how much notice?

Be specific. "A signing bonus to cover the difference for the first year" is easier to approve than "more benefits."

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Accepting the first tier without asking how it was set.**
- **Comparing gross salaries across countries** without checking benefits, leave and tax.
- **Ignoring currency risk** when paid in a foreign currency.
- **Treating equity as cash.** It may never be worth what you hope.
- **Negotiating by email only, with no follow-up call.** Tone matters.
- **Not getting the final terms in writing** before resigning from another job.

## A simple plan for this week

1. Ask for the full band and how your tier was assigned.
2. Pull occupation data from your country's official source and note a realistic range.
3. Collect two or three comparable postings or peer data points.
4. Draft your case around scope, results and market competition.
5. Choose three asks: base, one non-salary item, and a review date.
6. Make the ask by phone or video, then confirm in writing.

The band may not move. Often, though, you can improve the offer by clarifying the structure, making a specific ask, and offering the company an easy way to say yes.
