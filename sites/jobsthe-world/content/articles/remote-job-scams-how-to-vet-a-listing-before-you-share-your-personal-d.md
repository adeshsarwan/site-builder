Remote work removes the one check that used to protect job seekers: walking into an office. When the whole process happens through a screen, a scammer only needs a convincing logo, a polished message and a sense of urgency. Your job is to slow the process down and make the employer prove it is real *before* you hand over anything that can be misused.

This guide gives you a step-by-step vetting routine, a list of the most common scam patterns, and a plan for what to do if you've already shared something you shouldn't have.

## The Core Rule: Information Follows Verification

Most job-scam damage comes from sharing the wrong information at the wrong time. Use this order of operations:

1. **Before any interview:** share only your resume (with a personal email and phone number, and no full home address or date of birth).
2. **After interviews, before an offer:** share references and work history. Nothing sensitive.
3. **After a written offer you've verified:** share identity and payroll details through the company's official onboarding system.

Government ID numbers (Social Security number in the US, Social Insurance Number in Canada, Tax File Number in Australia, National Insurance number in the UK), bank details and copies of passports or driver's licenses belong in step 3 only. Legitimate employers need them for tax and payroll paperwork, and that paperwork happens after you accept an offer, not during an application.

## Step 1: Vet the Listing Itself

Start before you ever click "Apply."

### Check where the job actually lives

Find the company's own website, go to its careers page, and look for the same role. If the posting appears only on a job board or social platform and nowhere on the employer's site, that isn't proof of a scam, but it means you need to verify more carefully. Search for the job title together with the company name and see whether the details match.

### Read the posting for warning signs

- **Vague duties with big pay.** Descriptions like "process orders" or "assist the team" paired with high pay for little experience.
- **No clear requirements.** Real roles name skills, tools and experience levels.
- **Generic company details.** No location, no leadership names, no explanation of what the company does.
- **Spelling and formatting that don't fit.** A "global brand" posting full of odd phrasing deserves a second look.
- **Pressure language.** "Positions fill fast," "respond within the hour," or "limited openings."
- **Contact through messaging apps only.** Requests to move to Telegram, WhatsApp, Signal or text-based chat.

### Check the company is real and that you're looking at the right one

Scammers copy real companies' names, logos and job descriptions. Confirm the basics:

- Does the company have a working website with a history, products or services, and named staff?
- Is it registered where it says it is? In the US, search the relevant state business registry; in Canada, provincial or federal corporate registries; in Australia, the Australian Business Register's ABN Lookup (abr.business.gov.au); in the UK, Companies House.
- Does the company's real LinkedIn page list the recruiter as an employee?
- Is the website domain brand new or oddly spelled? A WHOIS lookup will show a domain's registration date.

## Step 2: Vet the Recruiter

A fake listing is only half the problem. Many scams start with an unsolicited message from someone claiming to be a recruiter.

### Email and domain checks

- Official recruiters usually write from a company domain. A free webmail address (Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo) claiming to represent a large employer is a warning sign.
- Look closely at the domain. Swapped letters, extra words ("-careers", "-hr") or an unusual ending like .co instead of .com are common tricks.
- Don't trust the display name. Check the actual sending address.

### Identity checks

- Search the recruiter's name on LinkedIn. Look for a real work history, connections at the company and activity that predates your message.
- Run their profile photo through a reverse image search. Stolen or AI-generated photos are common.
- Call the company using the main number on its official website (not the number in the message) and ask whether that person works there and whether the role exists.
- Ask for a video call with camera on. Someone who refuses every live conversation is not behaving like a hiring professional.

### Interview format checks

Be cautious if the "interview" happens entirely through typed chat, with a rapid-fire list of generic questions, followed by an offer within hours. Real hiring involves a human conversation, usually with the hiring manager, and takes more than a few messages.

## Step 3: Spot the Money Patterns

Most fraudulent offers eventually want money or access to your bank account. Learn the shapes these take.

### The equipment-check scam

This is one of the most convincing remote-work scams because it mimics normal onboarding. The pattern:

1. You receive a quick job offer, often without a real interview.
2. The "employer" says you need a laptop, monitor or software, and offers to send you a check or payment to buy it from a "preferred vendor."
3. You deposit the check. The bank may make funds available quickly.
4. You're told to send some or all of the money to a vendor, or to buy gift cards or crypto.
5. The original check bounces days later. The money you sent is gone, and you owe your bank the difference.

A deposit showing as "available" does not mean the check has cleared. The safest rule: **never send money to anyone because an employer's check told you to.** Real employers either ship equipment directly from their own IT department or a known supplier, or reimburse you under a written policy after you've started.

### Other money-related warning signs

- **Fees for training, background checks, uniforms, software or "registration."** Legitimate employers don't charge candidates to be hired.
- **Requests to use your own bank account to move money.** This can make you part of a money-laundering chain, even if you didn't know.
- **Reshipping jobs.** You receive packages at home and forward them elsewhere. This often involves stolen goods.
- **Task or "review" jobs that require deposits.** You're asked to pay in crypto to "unlock" earnings or complete tasks.
- **Payment in crypto or gift cards.** No ordinary payroll process works this way.

## Quick Reference: Red Flags and What To Do

| Red flag | Why it matters | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Offer without a live interview | Real hiring involves real conversation | Ask for a video call with a named manager |
| Recruiter uses a free email address | Official recruiters typically use company domains | Verify through the company's main phone number |
| Request for SSN/SIN/TFN/NI number at application | Payroll details are needed only after hiring | Decline until you have a verified written offer |
| Check sent to buy equipment | Classic overpayment/bounced-check pattern | Don't deposit it; ask for company-shipped equipment |
| Asked to pay fees to start | Employers cover hiring costs | Walk away |
| Move chat to Telegram or WhatsApp | Harder to trace, avoids platform protections | Keep communication on email and official channels |
| Pay that seems too high for the work | Bait to override caution | Compare against similar listings from verified employers |
| Urgent deadlines | Pressure prevents checking | Take the time you need; real offers can wait a day |

## Step 4: Verify the Offer Before You Share Sensitive Details

Once you receive an offer, treat it like a document to be audited.

- **Get it in writing** on company letterhead or through an applicant tracking system, with job title, pay, start date, manager's name and employment terms.
- **Confirm the sender's domain** matches the company's official domain.
- **Ask where onboarding forms will be completed.** Legitimate employers use a secure HR portal. Be wary of requests to email photos of IDs, or to fill out forms hosted on unfamiliar sites.
- **Ask who your manager is, and talk to them.** Ideally speak with a future teammate too.
- **Look for the standard paperwork in your country.** In the US, that's typically Form W-4 and Form I-9. In Canada, TD1 forms. In Australia, the TFN declaration and superannuation choice. In the UK, a right-to-work check and P45 or starter checklist. Expect these after an offer, handled through a company system.
- **Share the minimum.** If a form asks for something that seems unrelated to payroll or legal requirements, ask why.

## Step 5: Protect Yourself While Job Hunting

Small habits reduce your exposure:

- Use a separate email address for job searching.
- Consider a free voice number for applications rather than your personal mobile.
- Keep your public resume free of your home address, full date of birth and ID numbers.
- Use unique passwords and two-factor authentication on job platforms and your email.
- Don't click links in unexpected messages or install software for a "skills test" or "interview platform" without verifying it first.
- Keep a record of every job you apply for, the contact names and the channel used. It makes a suspicious message easier to spot.

## If You've Already Shared Something

Act quickly. The right response depends on what you gave out.

### If you shared an ID number or ID documents

- Contact your bank and credit providers and consider placing a fraud alert or credit freeze with the relevant credit bureaus.
- In the US, the Federal Trade Commission's IdentityTheft.gov provides a recovery plan and reporting tool.
- In Canada, contact Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada, and report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre.
- In Australia, report to Scamwatch and, for cybercrime, ReportCyber, and contact your bank.
- In the UK, report to Action Fraud (check for the current name of the national reporting service) and contact your bank.

### If you deposited a suspicious check or sent money

- Call your bank immediately and explain what happened.
- Don't send any more funds, even to "fix" the problem.
- Keep all messages, screenshots, check images and payment receipts.

### If you gave out passwords or installed software

- Change passwords starting with your email account, and enable two-factor authentication.
- Run a security scan and remove software you installed at their request.
- Watch your accounts for unusual activity.

### Report it

Reporting helps investigators spot patterns and may help you recover losses. Useful starting points:

- **US:** ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (ic3.gov).
- **Canada:** Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca).
- **Australia:** Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au).
- **UK:** Action Fraud (actionfraud.police.uk).

Also report the posting or profile to the platform where you found it.

## A 60-Second Pre-Share Checklist

Before you send any personal detail to a hiring contact, answer yes to all of these:

- I found the role on the employer's official careers page, or confirmed it with the company directly.
- The recruiter's email domain matches the company's, and I confirmed their identity independently.
- I've had a live conversation with a real person on the hiring team.
- I have a written offer I've checked.
- The request for my information comes through a secure, company-controlled system.
- No one has asked me to pay money, deposit a check, buy equipment upfront or move money for them.

If any answer is no, pause. A genuine employer will understand a candidate who verifies, and a scammer's reaction to being questioned usually tells you what you need to know.
