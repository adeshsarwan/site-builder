You know the company, the tools and most of the people. That feels like an advantage, and it is. It also creates a trap: because the work is so familiar to you, you describe it the way your job description does. "Managed client accounts." "Supported the team." "Coordinated weekly reporting." Everyone reading already knows you do those things. None of it answers the question they are really asking: *can this person do the next level?*

This guide shows you how to rebuild your resume and your spoken pitch so that familiar work reads as proof of readiness. It is written for people in remote-first roles in the US, Canada, Australia and the UK, where being "known" is often less reliable than it sounds.

## Why internal applications are judged differently

An outside candidate has to prove they can do the job. An internal candidate has to prove something subtler: that they can do a *different* job from the one they are doing now.

Three things work against you:

- **Your reputation is a snapshot.** People remember you as the person who was great at your current role. They may not have updated that picture in a year.
- **The panel is wider than your manager.** A hiring manager from another team, an HR partner or a director may read your application cold. They only know what is on the page.
- **Remote work narrows visibility.** In an office, people see you solve problems in passing. In a remote-first company, much of your impact happens in documents, threads and calls that decision-makers never attend.

So write the application as if the reader has never worked with you. Say it plainly and give evidence. Don't leave gaps for them to fill with assumptions.

## Step 1: Decode the bigger role before you touch your resume

Start with the target role, not your own history. Pull up the posting or, if there isn't one, ask the hiring manager for a role description or a conversation about what the position needs.

Then identify the **four or five things the role is really for**. Read for verbs and scale words such as *own*, *lead*, *set direction*, *influence*, *budget*, *cross-functional*, *stakeholders*. These signal what changes between your level and the next.

Typically, a step up involves some mix of:

- Wider scope (more clients, products, regions or systems)
- Decisions made with less supervision
- Influence over people who don't report to you
- Responsibility for results rather than tasks
- Developing or coordinating other people

Write those criteria at the top of a blank page. Everything you include later has to connect to at least one of them.

## Step 2: Translate duties into evidence

Your job description lists what you were *responsible for*. Promotion evidence shows what you *did beyond that* and what *changed because of it*.

For each criterion, hunt through the last 12 to 24 months for moments when you:

- Handled something that would normally go to your manager or a more senior colleague
- Fixed a problem nobody assigned to you
- Trained, onboarded or informally guided others
- Worked across teams or time zones to get something done
- Changed a process, template or habit that the team still uses

Then run each one through a simple structure: **situation, your action, result, and the level it signals.**

### Before and after examples

| Duty-style bullet | Readiness-style bullet |
|---|---|
| Responsible for weekly team reporting | Rebuilt the weekly report so leadership could read it in five minutes; it replaced three separate updates and is now the standard format across the department |
| Supported onboarding of new hires | Wrote the onboarding checklist and ran the first two weeks for four new hires across three time zones; new starters now work independently sooner, according to their managers |
| Managed customer escalations | Took over escalations that had been stalling, set up a shared tracker and agreed response windows with Engineering; repeat escalations on those accounts dropped |
| Attended cross-team meetings | Represented our team in the product planning group and pushed through two of our requested changes by bringing usage examples |

Notice what the right-hand column does. It names a decision you made, shows the outcome, and implies a level of trust. If you have real numbers, use them: hours saved, revenue protected, error rates, turnaround time. If you don't, don't invent them. A specific, honest description ("replaced three separate updates") beats a vague percentage.

## Step 3: Rewrite the resume itself

An internal resume still matters. Many companies require one, and it often travels to people you have never met.

### Lead with a summary aimed at the next role

Replace any generic objective with two or three lines that frame you as already operating at the next level. For example:

> Senior analyst who already owns reporting standards for a 40-person department, mentors junior colleagues and works directly with finance and product leads. Ready to move into a team lead role with responsibility for planning and delivery.

Adjust the details to your reality, and keep it truthful. The point is to show the reader how to see you.

### Break your current role into parts

Rather than one long list under your current title, consider splitting it:

- **Current role: scope and core responsibilities** (two bullets, brief)
- **Beyond-the-role contributions** (three to five strong bullets)
- **Projects, acting or stretch assignments** (named, with outcomes)

If you have been "acting up" (the term used in Australia and the UK for temporarily doing a higher-level role), or on a secondment or stretch assignment, list it clearly with dates. In the US and Canada, a phrase like "interim team lead" does the same job.

### Cut the insider shorthand

Internal acronyms, project nicknames and tool names mean little to a reader from another department. Spell them out once or describe what they are. Replace "Led Project Falcon" with "Led a six-month migration of customer records to a new platform."

### Mind regional conventions

- In the US and Canada, "resume" is typical, and it is usually one to two pages.
- In the UK and Australia, "CV" is common and a bit more length is acceptable, but keep it focused.
- Some employers also ask for a short statement addressing selection criteria, often called an expression of interest in Australia or a supporting statement in the UK. Answer each criterion directly, with one example per point.

Follow whatever format your company's internal process specifies.

## Step 4: Make remote work visible

Remote-first colleagues are often promoted on what others can *see*. Put your invisible work on the page.

Good remote-specific evidence includes:

- **Written communication:** documents, proposals or decision memos you wrote that others used
- **Asynchronous ownership:** running a project through updates and handoffs with few meetings
- **Cross-time-zone coordination:** how you kept work moving across regions
- **Documentation:** playbooks, wikis or recorded walk-throughs that other people rely on
- **Initiative in public channels:** problems you flagged and resolved where others could see them

Then add a short link or attachment list, if allowed, with work samples you are free to share. Never include confidential material without permission.

You can also ask two or three colleagues who have seen your work to send a short note to the hiring manager, or to say yes if asked for a reference. Pick people who can speak to the specific criteria, not only people who like you.

## Step 5: Have the conversation with your manager first

Unless your company's process says otherwise, tell your current manager before the application surfaces. Surprises tend to sour the relationship, and you may need their support.

Keep it short and forward-looking:

> "I'm planning to apply for the senior coordinator role. I'd like to be considered, and I wanted you to hear it from me. I'd appreciate your view on where my case is strongest and what I should address."

Then listen. A good manager will often give you the most useful feedback you'll get on the application. If they're lukewarm, ask what specifically would make them confident, and treat the answer as a to-do list.

Also talk to the hiring manager before you apply, if that's acceptable. Ask what a successful first six months looks like. Their answer gives you language to use.

## Step 6: Build a 90-second pitch

You will need a spoken version of your case. Use this four-part structure:

1. **The direction:** "I've been growing toward X for the past year."
2. **The proof:** two examples that match the role's main criteria.
3. **The gap and how you are closing it:** one honest area you're still developing, and what you're doing about it.
4. **The first move:** what you'd focus on in the first 90 days.

The third point matters more than most people expect. Naming a gap shows judgement. Choose a real but non-fatal one, such as "I haven't managed a budget directly, but I've built forecasts that our lead signed off on, and I'm taking the finance course the company offers."

Rehearse it out loud, on video if you can, since remote interviews reward a clear, compact delivery.

## Step 7: Handle the interview with people who know you

Interviewers who know you may skip questions that outsiders would ask, or assume answers. Don't let them.

- **Answer as if they don't know you.** "As you know" is the enemy. Give the full context.
- **Talk about the next level, not your current one.** When asked about challenges, choose examples involving ambiguity, people or trade-offs.
- **Be careful with colleagues and confidentiality.** Credit teammates by role or name, and don't criticise the current team or your manager.
- **Ask a strategy question.** For example: "What does this team need to be doing differently a year from now?"

## If you don't get it

Ask for specific feedback, ideally within a week, and ask what would make you a strong candidate next time. Then agree on one or two actions with your manager, with a rough timeline. A "not yet" with a plan is far better than a vague "keep going."

Some companies cannot promote you into a role that doesn't exist. If a promotion path is blocked, you can ask about acting-up opportunities, a stretch project or a formal development plan, and keep your documented evidence current for the next opening, internal or external.

## A final checklist

Before you submit, confirm that:

- Every bullet links to a criterion from the new role
- Your summary targets the next role, not the current one
- Acronyms and internal names are explained
- Your remote-work impact is visible
- You've told your manager
- You can deliver your pitch in about 90 seconds without notes
- You have an honest, specific answer about your development gap

Your employer already knows you. Your job in the application is to help them see the next version of you, with evidence they can take into the room when you aren't there.
