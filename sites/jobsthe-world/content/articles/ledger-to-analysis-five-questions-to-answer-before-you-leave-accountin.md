Accounting teaches you how the numbers get made. Financial analysis asks what the numbers mean and what the business should do next. If you've spent a few close cycles wishing you were in the meeting where the forecast gets argued over, the pull toward analysis makes sense.

Resigning is a different decision from wanting to move. Before you do it, answer five questions. Each one tests a specific fear: the credential you may need, the money, the hours, the title step-down, and the risk of leaving without a landing spot.

## Question 1: Which "financial analyst" job do you actually want?

"Financial analyst" covers very different work. Choosing a target first stops you from buying the wrong credential or taking the wrong pay cut.

| Role type | What you do day to day | How accounting experience helps | Remote-friendliness |
|---|---|---|---|
| FP&A / corporate finance | Budgets, forecasts, variance analysis, business partnering | Strong: you know the ledger, accruals and close | Often good, especially at distributed companies |
| Business or commercial finance | Pricing, deal and margin analysis for sales or product teams | Good: you can sanity-check the underlying data | Varies by team |
| Credit or risk analysis | Assess borrowers, covenants, exposure | Good: financial statement analysis is central | Mixed; some regulated employers prefer hybrid |
| Investment or equity research | Valuation, models, recommendations on securities | Moderate: statement reading helps, market skills must be built | Varies; often tied to a financial hub |
| Data-heavy finance analytics | SQL, dashboards, automated reporting | Moderate: depends on your technical skills | Often strong |

For most accountants, FP&A and corporate finance are the shortest bridge. You keep the context you already have, and the credential question is usually less severe than in investment roles.

**Do this now:** pull ten current postings in the role you're considering, in your country and remote category. Copy the required skills, tools and credentials into a list. Count how many you already meet. That tally tells you more than any general advice.

## Question 2: Do you need the CPA, the CFA, both, or neither?

This is the worry that stalls most people, so it's worth separating the two credentials.

**The CPA (or your country's equivalent)** signals accounting, audit, tax and reporting competence. In the US it's a state-licensed credential, and requirements, including education hours, differ by state. Canada, Australia and the UK have their own chartered or professional bodies, with different routes and names. If you're partway through, or already hold it, don't throw it away. In corporate finance, an accounting designation plus modeling skills is a credible combination, and in many companies it's valued for controllership-adjacent roles.

**The CFA** is an investment-focused program with three exam levels, plus work-experience requirements for the charter. It carries the most weight in asset management, research, and portfolio-oriented roles. It carries less in FP&A, where many hiring managers care more about whether you can build a clean model and explain a variance. Confirm the current exam and experience rules with the program itself before you commit, since they change.

Use this rough decision guide:

- **Targeting FP&A or corporate finance:** keep or finish your accounting credential if you're close. Skip the CFA at first. Invest in Excel modeling, a BI tool and business storytelling instead.
- **Targeting investment research or asset management:** the CFA becomes far more relevant. Check whether job postings list it as required or preferred, and whether employers will fund it.
- **Targeting credit analysis:** either can help; look at what lenders in your region ask for.
- **Unsure:** don't enroll in anything yet. Exam fees and study time are real costs, and a role you haven't tested is a poor reason to pay them.

One practical angle: ask whether a prospective employer sponsors exam fees or study leave. Moving into an analyst role that pays for the CFA is a better deal than paying for it yourself while taking a pay cut.

## Question 3: What do pay and hours really look like?

Be wary of anyone who gives you a single number. Pay for both accountants and financial analysts depends on location, industry, company size and seniority, and a move can be pay-neutral, a step up, or a step down.

Here's how to get a grounded answer without guessing:

1. **Use official data for the broad picture.** The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook has pages for both accountants and auditors and for financial analysts, including median pay and job outlook. Canada, the UK and Australia publish comparable labor-market data through their own government statistics and job-information sites.
2. **Check live postings.** Where pay transparency rules require ranges, compare the postings for your target level, not the senior ones.
3. **Compare total compensation.** Bonus structure, retirement matching, exam support and equity can matter as much as base salary.
4. **Ask about hours by cycle.** Accounting hours spike around close, audit and tax deadlines. Analyst hours spike around budgeting, forecast refreshes, board packs and ad hoc requests from leaders. Different calendar, similar pressure. Ask interviewers when the busiest weeks are and how often urgent requests arrive after hours.

Work out your **minimum acceptable offer** before you interview. Include your fixed costs, any exam fees, and the months your income might dip. If an offer falls below the line, you've already decided.

For remote roles across borders, check whether the employer can legally employ you where you live. Some companies hire only in certain states or countries, or use a third-party employer arrangement, and that can affect benefits, taxes and time-zone expectations. Ask early.

## Question 4: Will this really be an entry-level restart?

Not necessarily. The fear is that moving into analysis wipes out your seniority. Sometimes it does, but often it doesn't, and the route you choose matters.

**Routes that usually protect your level:**

- **Internal transfer.** Your employer already trusts your accuracy. Ask your finance leader about rotating onto the FP&A team, even for a few projects.
- **Bridge titles.** Roles such as financial reporting analyst, cost analyst or revenue analyst sit between accounting and FP&A. They let you do analysis while your ledger knowledge is still an advantage.
- **Smaller or growing companies.** A lean finance team may let a strong accountant cover both the books and the forecast, with a title that reflects your experience.

**Routes that more often mean a reset:**

- Applying cold to investment research or asset management without relevant market experience.
- Changing industry, function and employer all at once.
- Moving into a team where the hiring manager treats "analyst" as a graduate-entry grade.

Use your accounting background as proof of specific things: you understand how revenue is recognized, why a forecast won't tie to the ledger, and where data quality fails. In an interview, tell a short story about a time you found a variance, explained it, and changed a decision. That's analysis, and you've probably done it already.

A step down in title is acceptable if the pathway is clear. Ask in interviews, "What does progression look like from this role, and how long has it taken people on this team?" If nobody has been promoted, treat that as an answer.

## Question 5: Can you test the move before you resign?

The safest version of this career change doesn't start with a resignation letter. It starts with evidence.

**Build proof while employed:**

- Create a three-statement model for a public company or a hypothetical business, and document your assumptions.
- Produce a variance commentary using real or anonymized data: what changed, why, and what you'd do about it.
- Build a short dashboard in the tool your target employers use.
- Volunteer for budget or forecast work at your current job, or for a nonprofit's finance committee.

**Talk to people.** Have short conversations with three or four people already in the role you want. Ask what a normal week is like, what they wish they'd known, and whether their accounting background helped or held them back.

**Set resignation conditions.** Write them down. For example:

- A written offer in hand, with start date and pay confirmed.
- Savings covering a set number of months of expenses if a probation period or contract ends early.
- A clear answer on remote eligibility, equipment, hours and time-zone overlap.
- Confirmation of whether the employer will support any credential you plan to pursue.

If you can't meet these conditions yet, you haven't failed. You've found what to work on over the next six months.

## A quick self-check

Count how many of these are true:

- I can name the specific analyst role I'm targeting.
- I've checked that role's postings for credential requirements.
- I know my minimum acceptable pay and my savings runway.
- I have at least one portfolio piece I can show.
- I've spoken with people in the role.
- I have an internal or bridge-role option to explore.

If most are true, you're probably ready to start applying seriously. If few are, spend the next quarter filling gaps before you consider leaving. Staying put while you prepare is a strategy, not a delay.

## The bottom line

Accountants often make strong analysts because they distrust numbers that haven't been checked. The risk isn't that your skills don't transfer. It's that you pay for the wrong credential, accept the wrong pay, or leave before you have a real offer. Answer these five questions with data from postings, official labor statistics and conversations, and the decision becomes much clearer, whichever way it goes.
