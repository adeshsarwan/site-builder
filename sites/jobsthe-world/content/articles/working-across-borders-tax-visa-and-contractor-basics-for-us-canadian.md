A remote job posting rarely tells you where the employer expects you to live, how you will be paid or who handles your taxes. For job seekers in the US, Canada, the UK and Australia, those gaps matter. A strong offer from a company in another country can become expensive if you misread your work rights, your status or your tax position.

This guide covers the three issues to settle before you accept: whether you are allowed to do the work, whether you will be an employee or a contractor, and which tax questions to ask. It is general information, not legal or tax advice. Rules change and personal circumstances vary, so confirm the details with the official agency or a qualified adviser.

## Start with one principle: where you physically work matters most

In most of these countries, work rights and tax obligations are tied to where you are when you do the work, plus your residency and citizenship. The employer's location is only part of the picture.

Three separate questions come up in almost every cross-border offer:

- **Where will you be physically located** while working, and for how long?
- **Where are you a tax resident**, and does your citizenship create extra filing duties?
- **Where is the employer registered**, and does it have a legal way to employ or pay you there?

If the answers don't line up, expect delays, a changed offer or a withdrawn one.

## Work eligibility: the right to work is not the same as the right to be paid

Work authorization is the permission to work in a particular country. It is usually granted through citizenship, residency or a visa.

### If you stay in your home country

If you live in Canada and work remotely for a UK company, you are generally working in Canada. The question is then less about a UK visa and more about whether the employer can lawfully hire and pay someone there. That is a business and tax issue for them, and it affects you through payroll, benefits and contract type.

Still, ask the recruiter plainly: "Do you already hire people in my country, and through what arrangement?" A confident answer is a good sign. Vagueness suggests the company hasn't worked it out.

### If you plan to move or travel

This is where candidates get caught. A tourist or visitor status does not usually permit employment in the country you are visiting, and rules on remote work for a foreign employer differ by country and visa type. Check the official immigration agency before you book a flight or accept a start date.

| Country | Work-eligibility authority | Tax authority |
|---|---|---|
| United States | USCIS (and the Department of State for visas) | IRS |
| Canada | IRCC | CRA |
| United Kingdom | Home Office (GOV.UK) | HMRC |
| Australia | Department of Home Affairs | ATO |

If the employer expects you to relocate, find out whether they will sponsor a visa, who pays the costs and what happens if the application is refused. Get the answers in writing.

### Employer verification

Employers hiring someone to work inside their country must verify work rights. In the US this means Form I-9 for people employed there. In the UK, employers must carry out right-to-work checks. Expect to supply identity documents early, and be wary of any employer who says they don't need to check anyone anywhere.

## Employee or contractor: the label affects your money and your protections

Many international remote offers arrive as contractor agreements because the employer has no local entity. That may be legitimate, but the label is not decided by what the contract says. Tax and employment agencies look at how the relationship actually works.

### Signs you may be an employee in practice

- You work set hours decided by the company.
- You use their tools and report to a manager as a team member.
- You work for them full-time and mostly or only for them.
- You can't send someone else to do the work.
- You are paid for time rather than for a finished result.

### Signs of genuine contracting

- You control how, when and where the work is done.
- You have several clients or can take them on.
- You invoice for defined deliverables.
- You supply your own equipment and carry your own business risk.

### What changes if you are a contractor

Contractors usually give up benefits and protections that employees receive. Ask what you would lose:

- Paid leave, sick pay and statutory minimum protections
- Employer retirement contributions
- Employer-funded health coverage
- Unemployment or similar insurance
- The employer withholding tax on your behalf

You typically also take on administrative work: invoicing, setting aside money for tax, and registering as a self-employed person or business where required.

### Country-specific points

- **US:** A contractor is normally paid without withholding, and a non-US contractor may be asked to complete Form W-8BEN. A US citizen contracting from abroad is still a US taxpayer and may owe self-employment tax unless a social security agreement applies.
- **Canada:** Self-employed people generally pay both the employee and employer portions of Canada Pension Plan contributions, and may need to register for GST/HST depending on revenue. Check CRA guidance.
- **UK:** If you contract through your own company for a UK client, the off-payroll working rules (often called IR35) may apply. Self-employed people file Self Assessment returns with HMRC.
- **Australia:** Contractors commonly use an Australian Business Number (ABN). A contract that is mainly for labour can still carry superannuation obligations for the payer, and the Fair Work Ombudsman provides guidance on distinguishing employees from contractors.

## The employer of record option

Some companies without a local entity use an employer of record (EOR). The EOR becomes your legal employer on paper, handling payroll, tax withholding and statutory benefits, while the client company directs your daily work.

For you, this often means employee protections without the foreign company needing its own registration. Ask:

- Which company is named on my contract?
- Who processes my pay, and in which currency?
- Which benefits are statutory, and which are extras?
- Who owns my intellectual property and where do disputes get resolved?
- What happens to my employment if the client ends the engagement?

An EOR arrangement is not automatically better or worse than direct hire. Compare the actual terms.

## Tax questions to ask before you accept

Tax is where cross-border offers get complicated. These questions help you get clear answers.

### 1. Where will I be tax resident?

Each country has its own residency tests, which often consider where you live, how long you stay and your ties to the country. Read the relevant agency's guidance, such as the CRA's residency pages, HMRC's statutory residence test, or the ATO's residency tests. Spending extra weeks abroad can change your position.

### 2. Does my citizenship create filing duties?

US citizens are taxed on worldwide income wherever they live. The IRS offers relief mechanisms, including the foreign earned income exclusion and the foreign tax credit, but they have eligibility rules and require filing the right forms. US persons with foreign bank accounts may also have to report them. The IRS says the FBAR requirement applies when aggregate foreign account balances exceed $10,000 at any time in the year.

### 3. Will anyone withhold tax from my pay?

Employees are usually taxed through payroll. Contractors normally receive gross payments and must set aside money to cover tax. Ask the employer whether they will withhold anything and what documents they need from you.

### 4. Do tax treaties or social security agreements help?

The US has tax treaties with Canada, the UK and Australia, and social security (totalization) agreements with all three, which can prevent paying into two systems for the same earnings. Whether they help you depends on your circumstances and may require a certificate of coverage. Ask a tax professional how a treaty would apply to your case.

### 5. Could my work create tax risk for the employer?

If you do significant work in a country, the company may be considered to have a taxable presence there. That risk is the employer's, but it can influence whether they back out or restructure your role. A reasonable employer will have considered it already.

### 6. How will currency and payment timing work?

Ask which currency you will be paid in, who bears the exchange cost and how quickly invoices are paid. Late international payments can strain a contractor's cash flow.

## A pre-acceptance checklist

Before you sign, confirm these points in writing:

1. The country you will work from, and any planned travel or relocation
2. The legal entity that employs or contracts with you
3. Whether you are an employee, contractor or EOR employee
4. Pay rate, currency, payment schedule and who covers transfer fees
5. Tax forms required from you and any withholding
6. Benefits, leave and health coverage, or the lack of them
7. Equipment, expenses and who owns your work product
8. Termination notice terms and dispute resolution

If an employer can't or won't answer these, treat that as information about how organized they are.

## When to bring in a professional

A short consultation with a cross-border tax adviser or an immigration professional is worth it if you are a US citizen living abroad, planning to move countries, setting up a company for contract work, or being offered a large package. Ask for someone with experience in both countries involved, and agree on fees before the work begins.

For primary information, start with the official agencies listed above: the IRS and USCIS, the CRA and IRCC, HMRC and GOV.UK, and the ATO and Department of Home Affairs.

## The bottom line

A cross-border remote offer is only as good as its legal and tax footing. Confirm where you can lawfully work, how you will be classified and who is responsible for tax before you resign from your current job or book travel. Clear answers up front protect both your income and your peace of mind.
