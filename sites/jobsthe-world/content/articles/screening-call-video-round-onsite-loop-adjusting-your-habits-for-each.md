If you're job hunting remotely, you probably run one interview routine. You rehearse your stories, research the company, and show up. That routine works, but it treats three very different situations as one.

A phone screen strips away everything except your voice. A video round adds a face, but through a lens and a lag. An onsite loop, which is now often a half-day of back-to-back conversations, tests stamina and how you treat people in a room. Each format changes what the interviewer can observe, so each changes what you need to do on purpose.

This guide covers four habits that shift most from format to format: pacing, note placement, body language and follow-up. It ends with a quick-reference table.

## Why one routine isn't enough

Interviewers form impressions from whatever information reaches them. On the phone, that's tone, rhythm and clarity. On video, it's those things plus your framing, eye line and reactions. In person, it's all of that plus how you arrive, greet people and handle small talk.

So a habit that helps in one format can hurt in another. Reading from a script is nearly invisible on a call but obvious on video if your eyes track side to side. Leaning back and thinking silently feels thoughtful in a room but can read as a dropped connection online.

Adjust the habits below, and keep the substance the same: your stories, examples and questions.

## The phone screen: your voice does all the work

Phone screens are usually short and early. The recruiter or hiring manager is often checking basics: Do you understand the role? Is your salary range, location and work authorization compatible? Can you communicate clearly?

### Pacing

Without visual cues, people talk over each other and fill silences too quickly. Aim for answers that run roughly one to two minutes, then stop. If you have more to say, end with a short offer: "I can go into the technical side if that's useful."

Slow down slightly on names, numbers and dates. A listener can't glance at your face to catch what they missed.

### Notes

The phone is where notes are most useful and least risky. Use them, but keep them structured:

- A one-page "cheat sheet" with your three or four strongest stories as keywords, not paragraphs.
- The role's top requirements, each paired with one proof point.
- Your salary and availability answers, written out in advance.
- Two or three questions for the recruiter.

Resist the temptation to write out full sentences. Reading aloud has a flat, recited sound that is easy to hear.

### Body language

Nobody sees you, but your posture still shapes your voice. Standing or sitting upright and smiling slightly as you answer tends to make speech warmer. Use verbal signals in place of nods: "That makes sense," "Good question," "Let me think for a second."

### Follow-up

A short email the same day is plenty. Thank them, restate one specific point from the conversation, and confirm any next step they mentioned. Keep it to a few sentences.

## The video round: manage the lens, not just the answers

Video rounds are where remote-first candidates are most comfortable, and where small technical choices quietly shape the impression you make.

### Pacing

Even a good connection adds a slight delay. Pause for a beat after the interviewer finishes so you don't interrupt. If you're not sure whether they've finished, a short "Does that answer it, or should I go deeper?" cleans up the moment.

Keep answers a little tighter than you would in person. On screen, long answers lose attention faster, and you can't read the room as easily.

### Note placement

This is the biggest video-specific skill. Where your eyes go is the main thing the interviewer reads.

- **Put key notes near the camera.** A sticky note or a small document window at the top of your screen, close to the lens, keeps your eye line natural.
- **Keep the interviewer's video window near the camera too.** Drag it up so looking at their face and looking at the lens are almost the same motion.
- **Use a second monitor sparingly.** Glancing sideways repeatedly looks like distraction.
- **Skip paragraphs.** Keywords only. If you scroll or type while answering, they'll notice.
- **Have a paper copy of your résumé or CV** and the job description within reach, face-up, so you don't need to open files mid-answer.

Say it openly if you're taking notes: "I'm jotting that down." It explains the downward glance.

### Body language

Frame yourself from mid-chest up with the camera at eye level. Stack a laptop on books if needed. Sit close enough that your face is clear, and keep your hands visible occasionally; gestures help, but keep them within the frame.

Because subtle expressions get lost on video, make your listening visible. A small nod and a slightly more expressive face than you'd use in person helps. Don't freeze while the other person talks, since a frozen face can look like a frozen feed.

Before the call, test your audio, lighting (a window or lamp in front of you, not behind), and the platform's link. Have a backup plan, such as a phone number, in case the connection fails. If it does, say what's happening calmly and reconnect without apology spirals.

### Follow-up

Send a thank-you email within a day. Because video interviews often involve a panel, ask for names at the end or check the invite so you can personalize each note, or send one note to the main contact and mention the others by name.

## The onsite loop: stamina and presence

Whether it's in an office, a co-working space or a team offsite, an onsite loop usually means several conversations in a row, sometimes including a meal, a tour or a presentation. Remote workers sometimes assume this is "just more interviews." It's also a test of how you'd be as a colleague.

### Pacing

You'll repeat yourself. Each interviewer may ask similar questions, and you'll need to give each one a fresh, energetic answer. Prepare slightly different versions of your best stories so you can match the interviewer's role: technical detail for an engineer, outcomes for a manager, collaboration for a peer.

Plan the day: eat beforehand, bring water, and use any break to reset rather than scroll your phone. Ask about the schedule ahead of time so there are no surprises about presentations or meals.

### Notes

Bring a padfolio or notebook with a printed résumé or CV, a list of questions and the interviewers' names if you have them. A paper notebook is expected in person, and writing a few words signals attention. Keep your phone away and silenced.

Don't read from the notebook during answers. Glance at it before questions, not during stories.

### Body language

Here you're being judged on the whole picture, including things that never come up on a call:

- Arrive early, but not so early you're interrupting someone's morning. A few minutes before the start is typical.
- Be polite to everyone: reception, coordinators, the person who brings you water. Interviewers often hear how you treated them.
- Offer a firm, comfortable greeting that fits the local custom and the other person's lead.
- Keep your posture open, make natural eye contact, and mirror the pace of whoever you're talking to.
- Dress to the company's norm, or slightly above it. If you're unsure, ask the recruiter what people usually wear.

### Follow-up

Send individual thank-you emails, ideally the same day or next morning. Each should mention something particular from that conversation. A brief note to the recruiter or coordinator is also good practice, since they often shape the next steps.

## Thank-you etiquette across the four countries

Follow-up notes are widely appreciated in the US and Canada. In the UK and Australia, a brief, polite message is welcome too, though a long or effusive one can feel out of tune with a more understated style. Across all four, the same principle applies: short, specific and prompt. Three to five sentences usually do it. Don't use the note to reopen your answers at length. One quick clarification is fine.

Proofread every note, and double-check names and email addresses, especially after a panel.

## Quick-reference table

| Habit | Phone screen | Video round | Onsite loop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pacing | Short answers; slow on numbers and names | Pause after they finish; keep answers tight | Fresh versions of your stories for each person; plan for stamina |
| Notes | Keyword cheat sheet in front of you | Notes near the camera; say you're jotting things down | Paper notebook; glance before answering, not during |
| Body language | Upright posture; verbal acknowledgments | Camera at eye level; visible listening | Greetings, arrival and courtesy to everyone |
| Thank-you | Brief email, same day | Email within a day, personalized | Individual emails, plus the coordinator |

## A simple prep checklist by format

1. **Before a phone screen:** Write your cheat sheet, take the call somewhere quiet, and charge your phone or use a headset.
2. **Before a video round:** Test your tech, move notes next to the lens, and check your lighting and background.
3. **Before an onsite loop:** Confirm the schedule, map your route and timing, pack your padfolio, and plan your meals and breaks.

After each round, write down what you were asked and what you'd do differently. That record is more valuable than any generic advice, because it shows how you perform in each format.

## The takeaway

Keep your content steady and adapt your delivery. On the phone, make your voice and structure carry. On video, manage your eye line and timing. In person, show stamina and courtesy. Then close each round with a short, specific thank-you. Small format-specific changes add up, and they show an interviewer you can adapt, which is part of what they're hiring for.
