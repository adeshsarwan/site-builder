In an office, a lot of onboarding happens without anyone planning it. You overhear how a manager gives feedback. You notice who stays late before a launch and who gets pulled into every decision. You see a colleague's face when a deadline moves. None of that is written down, and all of it helps you work out what "good" looks like.

When you start a remote or hybrid role, those cues mostly disappear. Your calendar and your messaging tool replace the hallway, and neither tells you much unless you ask. The answer is to replace passive absorption with a deliberate system. That system has two parts: **scheduled conversations** that surface expectations, and **written benchmarks** that record what you learned so you can check it against reality.

This guide gives you both, built for roughly your first three months.

## What the hallway used to give you

Before building a replacement, it helps to name what's missing. Most new hires lose access to five kinds of information:

- **Pace and urgency.** How fast do people reply? Which requests are truly urgent?
- **Quality bar.** Is a rough draft welcome, or do people expect polished work first?
- **Decision style.** Who decides, who is consulted, and who just needs to be told?
- **Communication norms.** Which channel is used for what, and when is a call better than a message?
- **Relationships and context.** Who has history with whom, and which projects carry politics?

Each part of the system below targets one or more of these gaps.

## The system at a glance

| Element | Frequency | Who | What it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manager 1:1 | Weekly (30 min) | You and your manager | Priorities, feedback, expectations |
| Onboarding buddy or peer chat | Weekly for the first month, then every two weeks | A teammate at your level | Unwritten norms, tools, shortcuts |
| Stakeholder intro calls | 1–3 per week in the first month | People you depend on or serve | Context, needs, how they like to work |
| Working notes document | Updated daily or weekly | Just you | Your record of what you learned |
| Expectations summary | Sent after the first two weeks | You to your manager | Written confirmation of what success means |
| 30/60/90 reviews | Three scheduled points | You and your manager | Calibration against the benchmarks |

You don't need to run all of it perfectly. Even a lighter version beats waiting to be told.

## Week one: set the structure before you need it

In your first few days, ask for the structure rather than hoping it appears.

**Request a recurring 1:1 with your manager.** If one isn't already on the calendar, propose it. A message like this works: "To get up to speed quickly, could we set a recurring 30-minute weekly 1:1 for my first three months? I'll bring an agenda and send notes afterward."

**Ask for a buddy.** If the company didn't assign one, ask your manager who would be a good person for day-to-day questions. A peer is often better than your manager for the small stuff, such as "Do people actually read that channel?"

**Ask for a list of people to meet.** Tell your manager you'd like to talk with the people you'll work with most, and ask them to suggest names. Then book those calls yourself.

**Start your working notes document.** More on this below. Start it on day one, while everything still seems strange enough to be worth writing down.

## The manager 1:1: your most important channel

Your manager holds most of the information you're missing, but they may not realize what you can't see. Use the first few 1:1s to cover specific ground.

### First 1:1 questions

- What does a successful first 30, 60 and 90 days look like to you?
- What are the two or three most important things for me to deliver early?
- How do you prefer to receive updates: written, in a meeting, or only when something changes?
- How quickly do you expect replies, and does that differ by channel?
- What's your approach to feedback? Will I hear it as it happens or in scheduled reviews?
- What do people new to this team commonly get wrong?

### Ongoing 1:1 agenda

Keep a shared document with the same four headings each week:

1. **Done since last time**
2. **In progress and blockers**
3. **Decisions I need or questions I have**
4. **Feedback, both directions**

That last item matters. Ask directly: "Is there anything about how I'm working that I should adjust now, while it's easy?" People rarely volunteer small concerns in a remote setting, so they may otherwise sit unsaid until a formal review.

## The peer conversation: where unwritten norms live

Your manager can tell you what the team is supposed to do. A peer can tell you what actually happens. In weekly or biweekly peer chats, ask things like:

- Which meetings are essential, and which are fine to skip or watch later?
- When someone posts a question in the team channel, who usually answers?
- Do people work visible hours, or is it about output? Is anyone expected to be online at certain times?
- How does the team handle disagreement? Publicly in threads, or in a side conversation?
- What's something nobody tells new people?

Keep these chats casual. Offer something back too, such as a fresh-eyes observation about the onboarding materials. That builds a two-way relationship rather than a stream of requests.

## Stakeholder calls: borrow context from the people you'll serve

Short intro calls with colleagues in other functions fill in the relationship map. Use the same three questions each time so you can compare answers:

1. What does your team need most from mine?
2. What would make working together easier?
3. Who else should I talk to?

The third question keeps your network growing without much effort. After each call, add two lines to your notes: what they need and how they prefer to communicate.

## Written benchmarks: make the invisible visible

Conversations fade. Writing keeps them useful. Build these three documents.

### 1. Your working notes

This is a private running log. Organize it under headings that mirror the five gaps above: pace, quality bar, decisions, communication, and people. Add dated entries such as:

- "Manager replies to messages within a few hours but prefers a call for anything with multiple moving parts."
- "Project updates go in the team thread, not email."
- "Finance reviews anything over a certain amount; ask the budget owner early."

After a month, patterns emerge that no single conversation would have shown you. Review the log before each 1:1 and pick the open questions.

### 2. An expectations summary

At around the end of week two, send your manager a short written summary of what you've understood. Keep it to a page. Include:

- Your understanding of your top priorities
- What you believe "done well" looks like for each
- How and when you'll report progress
- Anything that remains unclear

Frame it as a check, not a demand: "Here's what I've taken from our conversations. Please correct anything I've got wrong." If your manager replies with edits, you've just turned a vague expectation into a documented one. If they agree, you have a reference point for later reviews.

### 3. A 30/60/90 plan

Draft a simple plan with a few concrete outcomes per period, ideally ones your manager has already named. Make each item observable. "Understand the reporting process" is weak. "Produce the monthly report independently, with one review pass" can be checked. Share the plan, ask for edits, and revisit it at each milestone.

## Send a recap after important conversations

A two- or three-line follow-up message prevents many misunderstandings:

> "Thanks for the time today. My takeaways: (1) the vendor migration is the priority through the end of the month, (2) I'll send a Friday written update, and (3) I'll check with Priya's team before changing the template. Let me know if I misread anything."

Use real names from your own context, of course. The point is that your recap becomes the shared record. It also shows reliability, which matters when people can't see you working.

## Hybrid roles: watch the two-speed information problem

If your team splits time between home and office, important context can pass through in-person moments you aren't part of. Counter this in three ways:

- **Ask what gets decided in person.** Then ask that those decisions be posted in writing, or ask a colleague to share a summary.
- **Plan your office days around conversations.** Use in-person time for the relationship-building and ambiguous discussions that are harder on a call, and save heads-down work for home.
- **Don't assume silence means approval.** If feedback is thin, ask for it explicitly.

## Calibrate at 30, 60 and 90 days

Put three review conversations on the calendar at the start. At each one, bring your working notes and your 30/60/90 plan, and ask:

- Which of my goals are on track, and which aren't?
- Is there anything I'm doing that doesn't match how the team prefers to work?
- What should I prioritize for the next 30 days?
- Is there anyone else I should be learning from?

Also check your contract and your company's policies for any probation or formal review period, since the rules vary by country, province, state and employer. Knowing the timeline helps you schedule your own check-ins ahead of any official assessment.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Waiting to be invited.** Remote managers are often busy and assume silence means things are fine. Take the first step.
- **Asking only in public channels.** Some questions are better in a direct message or a 1:1, particularly about tone or politics.
- **Over-documenting.** If your system takes more than a few minutes a day, shrink it. A short, current note beats an elaborate, abandoned one.
- **Treating one person's view as the norm.** Cross-check important points with at least two people.
- **Skipping the follow-through.** If you ask for feedback and then don't act on it, people will stop giving it.

## A simple starting checklist

1. Book a weekly 1:1 and a buddy chat.
2. Create your working notes document and a shared 1:1 agenda.
3. Schedule your first five stakeholder calls.
4. Send your expectations summary at the end of week two.
5. Share a draft 30/60/90 plan by week three.
6. Put the 30, 60 and 90-day reviews on the calendar.

The hallway taught people by accident. Your system teaches you on purpose, and it leaves a written trail you can build on long after your first months are over.
