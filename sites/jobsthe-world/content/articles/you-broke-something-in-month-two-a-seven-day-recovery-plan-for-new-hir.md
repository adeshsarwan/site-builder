You pushed the wrong file, emailed the wrong list, overwrote a shared spreadsheet, or approved something you should have escalated. It's visible, and you've been in the job for eight weeks. Your stomach has dropped.

This is recoverable. Managers rarely remember the mistake for long. They remember how the person handled it. In a remote-first job, your manager can't see your face or read the room, so your messages are the whole story. This plan helps you make them good.

## First: decide whether this is an "act now" problem

Before you write anything polished, sort the error into one of two groups.

**Act within the hour, using the fastest channel:**
- Customer or personal data went to the wrong person or place
- Money moved incorrectly
- Something is down, broken or exposed right now
- A legal, safety, security or compliance process may have been triggered

**Act the same working day:**
- Internal errors with no immediate outside impact
- Wrong numbers in a report that hasn't been used yet
- Missed deadlines or botched handoffs
- Awkward messages sent to the wrong channel

If it's in the first group, check whether your company has an incident or security reporting process. Most do, and new hires often haven't read it. Use it, and tell your manager at the same time. Don't wait to draft the perfect message. In regulated fields such as health, finance and education, and anywhere personal data is involved, reporting delays can turn a small error into a serious one.

## Hour 0–2: Stop the damage, then tell your manager

Do two things, in this order.

### 1. Contain it, if you safely can

Undo the change, pull the email back, restore the previous version, or pause the process. Only do this if you're sure the undo won't make things worse. If you're unsure, don't improvise. Leave things as they are and ask for help.

Take screenshots or notes as you go, including timestamps. You'll need them later, and you'll forget details.

### 2. Message your manager directly

Don't post it in a team channel first. Don't wait for a scheduled one-to-one. Send a direct message, and ask for a short call if the issue is significant.

A good first message has four parts: what happened, what you know about the impact, what you've done, and when you'll update them next.

> **Script (direct message):**
> "Hi [Name], I need to flag a mistake I made. This morning I [specific action], and it resulted in [specific effect]. As far as I can tell, it affects [who/what]. I've already [containment step]. I'm checking [what you're verifying] now and will send you an update by [time]. Do you have 10 minutes today for a quick call?"

Notice what's missing: long apologies, excuses and speculation. One plain "I'm sorry for the extra work this creates" is enough. Over-apologizing pushes your manager into the job of comforting you, and you want them focused on the fix.

### If your manager is offline

Time zones matter in remote-first teams, especially across the US, Canada, the UK and Australia. A mistake at 4 p.m. in Sydney lands in the middle of the night in London.

- If it's urgent, use the escalation route (on-call contact, a manager's manager, or the incident channel) and say that you couldn't reach your manager.
- If it can wait, send the message anyway so it's the first thing they see, and say what you'll do in the meantime.

> **Script (manager offline):**
> "Hi [Name], flagging this before your day starts. I made an error on [thing]. Nothing further will happen until you've seen this, and I've [contained it / left it untouched]. I'll prepare a fix proposal and a short write-up so we can decide quickly when you're online."

## Day 1: Verify the impact and offer a fix

Your update should arrive when you said it would, even if all it says is "still checking, nothing new." Missing your own deadline on day one is the worst outcome.

Spend the day answering four questions:

1. **Who or what was affected?** Be exact. "Possibly everyone" isn't an answer.
2. **How long was it live?** Include the first and last time it could have caused harm.
3. **What's the smallest reliable fix?** Think about the quickest safe repair, not the most elegant one.
4. **Who else needs to know?** Don't contact them yourself without agreement. Ask your manager whether they want you to do it.

Then send a second message with the fix proposal.

> **Script (day-one update):**
> "Update: the issue affected [scope] between [time] and [time]. I propose [fix], which should take about [time]. I'd like to [do it now / do it after you've reviewed it]. The people who may need to hear about it are [names/teams]. Do you want to tell them, or shall I draft something for you?"

Giving your manager a proposed fix and a draft message shows you're already doing the work. It also keeps decisions with the person who owns the outcome.

## The seven-day plan at a glance

| Day | Goal | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Hour 0–2 | Disclose | Contain if safe; direct message to your manager; use the incident route if it's urgent |
| Day 1 | Scope and propose | Confirm impact; send fix proposal and draft notices |
| Day 2 | Repair | Apply the agreed fix; verify it worked; confirm to your manager |
| Day 3 | Inform | Send the agreed notice to affected colleagues or customers |
| Day 4 | Find the cause | Work out why it happened (process, access, unclear instructions, your own gap) |
| Day 5 | Prevent | Add a checklist, a review step or a permission change; share a short write-up |
| Day 6–7 | Close the loop | Short conversation with your manager; ask for feedback; return to normal work |

## Day 2: Fix it and prove it's fixed

Make the repair, then check it. Don't write "should be fixed now." Write what you tested.

> "The fix is live. I verified it by [specific check], and the results match [expected outcome]. I'll check again tomorrow morning to make sure it holds."

Then do the follow-up check you promised. Small, kept promises rebuild trust faster than anything you say.

## Day 3: Tell the people who were affected

If colleagues or customers were affected, a short, factual message is better than silence. Agree the wording with your manager first.

A useful notice has four lines:
- What happened, in one sentence
- What it meant for them
- What has been done
- Who to contact with questions

Leave out your internal reasons, lengthy apologies and anything that blames a tool, a person or the time of day. If you're writing to a client, your manager or the account owner will almost always want to send or approve it.

## Day 4: Work out the real cause

This is the step that separates a recovery from a cover-up. Ask honestly why the error was possible.

Common causes for new hires:
- **A process nobody documented.** You did what seemed normal.
- **Too much access.** You could do something you didn't realize was risky.
- **Unclear instructions.** You made a reasonable assumption.
- **A knowledge gap.** You didn't know the check existed.
- **Rushing or overload.** You skipped a step because you were under pressure.

Be fair to yourself, but don't hide behind "nobody told me." Even if the onboarding was weak, you can still say what you'd do differently. Two sentences about your own part and two about the system is usually the right balance.

## Day 5: Prevent a repeat, in writing

Write a short summary your manager can forward without editing. Keep it to a page or less.

**A simple structure:**
1. What happened
2. Impact and timeline
3. Cause
4. Fix
5. What will change

The "what will change" section is the most valuable. A checklist for the task, a second-person review for risky changes, a naming convention, a restricted permission or a new line in the onboarding doc will all do. Choose one or two changes you can complete yourself. Offering to improve the process can turn an embarrassing moment into a visible contribution.

## Days 6–7: Close the loop with your manager

Ask for a short conversation, ideally on video. You don't need to relitigate the mistake. You're asking for information.

> **Script (closing conversation):**
> "I wanted to check in now that everything is resolved. Is there anything about how I handled it that you'd do differently? And is there anything you'd like me to check with you before I do it in the future, so we can build trust again?"

That second question matters. It asks your manager to define what "careful" looks like for this role, and that's useful for the next several months.

Then go back to your regular work. Don't keep apologizing each time you speak. One clean closing conversation is better than a month of nervous check-ins.

## What not to do

- **Don't hide it and hope.** In remote teams, logs, version histories and customer replies usually surface problems. Being found out is far worse than coming forward.
- **Don't tell a teammate first.** If your manager hears from someone else, the story is no longer yours.
- **Don't write a long confession.** Keep it short and factual.
- **Don't blame the tools or the team.** If a system contributed, say it calmly in the cause analysis.
- **Don't quietly fix it and say nothing.** The fix is only half of what your manager needs to see.
- **Don't assume the worst about your job.** Probation and notice terms differ by country and employer, so if you're anxious, read your contract. Ask HR only about process, not your fears.

## When it's more serious than a slip

Some mistakes carry real consequences: a data breach, a financial loss or a safety issue. The sequence is the same, but the speed matters more. Report through the official route, keep factual notes, and don't speculate. If you're being asked to attend formal meetings or sign statements, it's reasonable to ask what the process is and to seek independent advice where your local rules allow.

## A final note on how this lands

Your manager is probably thinking about three things: Is the problem contained? Can I trust this person to tell me bad news? Will they learn from it? The seven-day plan answers all three. You tell them early, you fix it visibly, and you leave the team's process better than you found it.

Most new hires don't get credit for what went smoothly. A well-handled error can show your judgment more clearly than months of uneventful work.
