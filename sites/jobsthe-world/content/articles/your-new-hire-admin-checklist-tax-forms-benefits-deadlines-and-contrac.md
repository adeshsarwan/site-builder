Remote onboarding often arrives as a stream of automated emails from a payroll platform. Each one says "action required", and none explains why. Some of those tasks are legal deadlines. Others are company policies that only feel urgent.

This checklist separates the two. It covers the usual paperwork in the US, Canada, the UK and Australia, the timing rules I'm confident about, and the consequences of missing them. Your employer's instructions always come first. Where I don't know a deadline for certain, I say so rather than guess.

## First, work out what kind of "new hire" you are

Before filling in anything, check three things in your offer paperwork:

- **Employee or contractor.** Employees complete tax withholding forms. Contractors usually invoice and handle their own tax. If you're told you're a contractor but you work set hours, use company equipment and take direction like an employee, ask HR or a local adviser about it. Misclassification is a known problem in remote hiring.
- **Which entity employs you.** Some remote companies hire through a local subsidiary or an employer-of-record service. That decides which country's forms you complete.
- **Where you physically work.** Your home address usually sets your tax residency and often your payroll registration. If you plan to work from another state, province or country for a while, tell your employer *before* you go. It can change withholding, social contributions and your right to work there.

## The universal first-week list

Whatever your country, expect these:

1. **Identity and right-to-work verification.** Usually done by video call or secure upload for remote staff.
2. **A tax withholding or declaration form.** This tells payroll how much tax to take.
3. **A government ID number.** Examples are a Social Security number, SIN, National Insurance number or TFN.
4. **Bank details** for direct deposit.
5. **Your written contract or terms** and any policy acknowledgements.
6. **Benefits and retirement choices.**
7. **An equipment and security agreement.**

## United States

**Form W-4.** This sets your federal income tax withholding. You complete it when you start. If you don't submit one, your employer generally has to withhold as though you were single with no adjustments. You can fix it later by submitting a new W-4. Many states, and some cities, have their own withholding form, so check whether you need one for the state where you live and work.

**Form I-9.** You complete Section 1 no later than your first day of work. Your employer completes Section 2 within three business days of your start by examining your original documents. For remote staff, that may mean an in-person check by an authorised representative. Some employers enrolled in E-Verify can use an approved alternative procedure involving a live video check. Employers can face penalties for incomplete I-9s, and an unresolved one can put your employment at risk. Don't let it sit.

**Benefits enrollment.** Windows are set by the employer and insurer. Many are around 30 days from your start or eligibility date, but your plan may differ, so find the date in your enrollment portal. Miss it and you may have to wait until the next open enrollment, unless a qualifying life event gives you a special enrollment period. Check how the plan handles the following:

- Health, dental and vision, and whether coverage starts on day one or after a waiting period
- Whether your preferred doctors and pharmacies are in network where you live, which matters for remote workers far from headquarters
- 401(k) enrollment, which is often separate from health benefits, with its own start date and possibly automatic enrollment
- Flexible spending or health savings accounts, where elections can be locked in for the year

**Later in the year.** Your employer issues a Form W-2 by the end of January. Check the state wages shown, especially if you moved or worked from several states.

## Canada

**TD1 forms.** You complete the federal TD1 and a TD1 for your province or territory. They tell your employer which personal tax credits to apply. If you don't submit them, your employer may only be able to apply the basic personal amount, so you could have more tax withheld than necessary. You'd then reclaim it when you file your return. Quebec residents also complete a Quebec provincial form. Update your TD1 if your circumstances change.

**Social Insurance Number.** Employers must ask for your SIN, and you generally have three days from starting work to provide it. If you're a newcomer with a temporary SIN, your employer should know its expiry date. Make sure your work permit and SIN status stay in sync.

**Benefits and retirement.** Group health and dental plans are provided through insurers. Waiting periods and enrollment deadlines vary, and late enrollment may require evidence of good health or mean losing the option. Take the enrollment deadline in your welcome email seriously, even though I can't give you a universal number. If your employer offers a group RRSP or pension plan, check the matching rules and when contributions start.

**Later in the year.** T4 slips are issued by the end of February. Compare the figures with your final pay stub before filing.

## United Kingdom

**Starter checklist and P45.** If you have a P45 from your last employer, give it to your new one. If you don't, you'll complete a starter checklist. It asks whether this is your only job and whether you have a student or postgraduate loan. HMRC then issues a tax code. If your employer doesn't have enough information, you may be placed on an emergency tax code. That can mean overpaying for a while, though HMRC generally corrects it once details are filed.

**Right to work.** Employers must check your right to work before you start. Many checks can now be done online, such as with a share code, which suits remote hires. Have your documents ready before day one, because a pending check can delay your start.

**Written contract terms.** Employers are required to give you a written statement of main terms on or before your first day, including pay, hours and notice. Compare it with your offer letter. If something doesn't match, raise it in writing before you sign anything or accept the first payment.

**Workplace pension.** Eligible workers are normally automatically enrolled, though employers can use a short postponement period. You can opt out, but there's a time limit that starts after you get the enrollment notice. Opting out means giving up the employer contribution, so think before you do.

**Later in the year.** You receive a P60 after the tax year ends in April. When you leave, you'll get a P45.

## Australia

**TFN declaration.** You give your employer a tax file number declaration. If you don't provide a TFN within 28 days of starting, your employer must withhold tax at the highest rate. You can later get the extra tax back through your tax return, but your take-home pay will hurt until then. The declaration also covers whether you claim the tax-free threshold. Claim it only from one payer if you have several jobs.

**Super choice.** Employers must offer a standard choice form within 28 days of your start. You can choose your own fund or use your existing one. If you don't choose, your employer pays into its default fund. Consolidating several small super accounts can save fees, so find your existing fund details before you start.

**Fair Work Information Statement.** Employers must give new employees this as soon as possible after they start. It summarises the National Employment Standards and your basic rights. Keep it with your contract.

**Contract details.** Check whether you're full-time, part-time or casual, as well as your award or agreement coverage, notice period and any probation. Ask what applies if your role is remote and you work from another state.

**Later in the year.** Your employer finalises your income statement after the financial year ends on 30 June. You can see it through your myGov account linked to the ATO.

## Equipment and home-office paperwork

This part is less regulated but causes many disputes. Ask for answers in writing:

- **What's provided.** Laptop, monitor, headset, chair, phone or internet stipend.
- **Ownership and returns.** Who owns the equipment, and when you must return it after leaving. Keep tracking numbers for returns.
- **Reimbursement process.** Whether you need pre-approval, what receipts you need and how long claims have to be filed.
- **Security rules.** Device encryption, VPN and password-manager policies, and whether you can use personal devices.
- **Tax treatment.** Whether stipends are taxable in your country. Ask payroll, and ask a local tax professional if your situation is unusual.

Don't buy expensive gear before approval, and don't assume the company will pay for it afterwards.

## What to do if you've already missed a deadline

1. **Tell HR or payroll straight away.** Say which item is outstanding and why. Most administrators would rather hear from you than chase you.
2. **Submit it right away,** even if it's late, then ask what the consequence is.
3. **Check your pay stub** after the first payroll run for odd tax amounts, missing deductions or no benefits.
4. **Keep records.** Save copies of submitted forms and confirmation emails.
5. **Contact the tax authority** if an issue can't be fixed through payroll, such as a wrong tax code, a missing SIN or a mistaken withholding rate.

## A simple first-30-days plan

- **Before day one:** gather ID, bank details, your tax number and previous employer documents.
- **Day one:** complete the tax form, the identity or right-to-work step and the contract acknowledgements.
- **Week one:** submit your SIN, TFN or equivalent, set up direct deposit and calendar your benefits deadline.
- **Weeks two to four:** select benefits and retirement options, confirm equipment and check your first pay stub carefully.

None of this is glamorous, but a clean paperwork trail means your first pay arrives correctly, your coverage starts on time and your remote setup is documented. Rules change and individual situations vary, so use this as a guide for asking the right questions rather than as legal or tax advice.
