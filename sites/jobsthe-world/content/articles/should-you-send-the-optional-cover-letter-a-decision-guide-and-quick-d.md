You've found a remote role that fits. The form asks for a résumé, and next to the cover letter upload it says "optional." Now you're stuck. Skip it and you might look less interested. Write one and you might spend an hour on something nobody opens.

Nobody outside the hiring team can tell you how that team treats optional letters. Some read every one. Others never click the attachment. What you can do is decide using the signals in the posting and the odds that a letter changes your outcome. This guide gives you both: a way to decide, and a drafting method that keeps the cost low.

## What "optional" usually means

"Optional" is rarely a test of obedience. In most cases it means the employer won't reject you for leaving it out. It does not mean the letter is ignored if you send it.

Think of it as a tiebreaker you control. If your résumé already makes the case clearly, a letter adds little. If your résumé leaves a gap, such as a career change, a missing requirement, or an unusual location, the letter is the only place you can fill it in.

That framing helps you stop asking "Will they punish me for skipping?" and start asking "Is there something my résumé can't say for me?"

## The decision checklist

Answer these six questions. Each "yes" is a point in favor of writing one.

1. **Is there a story your résumé can't tell?** Examples: switching fields, returning after a career break, moving from an office-based role to remote work, or applying with a non-linear background.
2. **Does the posting ask for something a résumé shows poorly?** Written communication, self-direction, and async collaboration are common in remote listings. A letter is itself a writing sample.
3. **Is the company small?** At a startup or small team, a person is more likely to read applications personally. A large company running thousands of applications through software is less likely to read a letter.
4. **Is the role competitive for you?** If you meet nearly every requirement, a strong résumé may be enough. If you're a partial match, a letter can explain why you'd still succeed.
5. **Is there a specific reason you want this company?** If you can name something real about their product, mission, or way of working, a letter has substance. If you'd have to invent enthusiasm, skip it.
6. **Does the posting hint it matters?** Phrases like "tell us why you're interested," "we'd love to hear from you," or a prompt question are signs someone reads the letters.

### How to read your score

- **0–1 yes:** Skip it. Put the time into tailoring your résumé and applying to more roles.
- **2–3 yes:** Send a short one. Use the quick-draft method below.
- **4–6 yes:** Write a better one, and consider finding a named contact to address it to.

## When to skip it

Skipping is the right call more often than people admit. Don't write one when:

- The application is a one-click or quick-apply flow where the letter would be buried.
- You'd be repeating your résumé in paragraph form.
- You have no specific reason for choosing this employer beyond "it's remote and it pays well."
- You'd have to choose between sending a rushed letter and sending a polished application to a second job.

A generic letter can cost you more than no letter. A vague, copy-pasted note signals low effort, while a missing optional document signals nothing at all.

## When you should write one, even if it's optional

### You're working around a gap or a stretch

If you're missing one listed requirement but have a close equivalent, say so briefly and show the equivalent. Do it plainly, without apologizing.

### Your location or work authorization needs explaining

Remote-first roles often come with geographic limits that a résumé doesn't reveal. Employers may hire only in certain countries, states, provinces, or time zones, for tax, legal, or collaboration reasons. If you're in Canada applying to a US-headquartered company, in Australia applying to a team centered on European hours, or in the UK applying to a role tied to US business hours, one sentence about where you are and what hours you can work answers a question the recruiter will otherwise have to ask.

Only state work authorization facts you're sure about. If the posting is unclear, ask the recruiter or check the employer's careers page instead of guessing.

### The posting emphasizes written communication

Distributed teams run on writing: updates, documentation, handoffs. If the listing mentions async work, a clear, well-organized letter shows the skill directly.

### A person, not a system, is likely reading

If a hiring manager's name is on the post, or you were referred, a short letter turns a cold application into a personal one. In a referral, name the person who referred you in the first line, with their permission.

## Regional notes

The expectations differ a little by country, so check the posting wording.

- **US and Canada:** "Cover letter" is the standard term. One page or less is the norm.
- **UK:** You may see "covering letter" or "supporting statement." A supporting statement, especially in public-sector or education postings, is often a core part of the application rather than a bonus, so treat it as required if the listing describes it that way.
- **Australia:** Many government and public-sector roles ask you to address selection criteria. That's not an optional cover letter, so follow the instructions exactly. For private-sector roles, a short letter is common and a brief one is usually enough.

Whatever the country, follow the posting's formatting instructions, file type, and length limits exactly. Leave out personal details that don't belong in an application, such as a photo, date of birth, or marital status, unless the employer specifically requests something.

## The 20-minute quick-draft method

The aim is a letter of roughly 150 to 250 words that a busy reader can finish in under a minute. Set a timer.

### Minutes 0–5: Gather three things

From the posting, pick **the two or three requirements that look most important**. They're usually near the top or repeated. Then pick **one specific thing about the employer** you can speak to honestly: a product you've used, a published approach to remote work, a problem the company is solving. Finally, choose **one or two achievements** from your own work that match those requirements.

### Minutes 5–15: Write four short paragraphs

**1. The opening (1–2 sentences).** Say the role you're applying for and the strongest reason you fit. Skip "I am writing to apply for…" as a lead. Start with substance.

> I'm applying for the Customer Support Lead role. I've spent four years running a remote support team across three time zones, which looks close to what you're building.

(Replace the details with your own real experience.)

**2. The proof (3–4 sentences).** Give one or two concrete examples tied to the posting's top requirements. Say what you did, how, and the result. Use a number only if it's yours and accurate.

**3. The reason for this employer (2–3 sentences).** Show you've looked at the company. Be specific and honest. Avoid flattery that could apply to any firm.

**4. The close and logistics (2 sentences).** Mention anything practical: your time zone, availability, or location. Then say you'd welcome a conversation. That's enough.

### Minutes 15–20: Edit and check

- Cut every sentence that repeats your résumé without adding context.
- Read it aloud. If you stumble, simplify.
- Check the company name, role title, and any contact name. Mistakes here do real damage.
- Save as a PDF unless the posting asks otherwise, with a sensible file name like "Firstname-Lastname-Cover-Letter."

## Build a reusable base, not a template you paste

You can speed up future applications by keeping a base document with three or four achievement blurbs, each written in two or three sentences. For each application, choose the ones that match the posting and rewrite the opening and the employer paragraph from scratch. Those two parts are what a reader notices.

Avoid dropping in text that says "I'm passionate about" without a reason. Show the reason instead.

## A note on AI drafting tools

Drafting tools can help you get past a blank page, but their output tends to sound alike, and employers who read many letters notice it. If you use one, feed it your own facts, then rewrite in your own voice and verify every claim. You're the one who has to talk about it in the interview.

## Making the call: a short example

Say you're applying to two roles in one afternoon.

**Role A** is at a 40-person company. The listing says "tell us what draws you to this work," and you're moving from in-person teaching into instructional design. You score at least four yes answers. Write the letter.

**Role B** is a large employer's quick-apply listing, and you match the requirements closely. You can't point to anything particular about the company. You score one yes at most. Skip the letter and spend the time on a stronger résumé match or on another application.

That's the pattern. The letter is worth writing when it carries information your résumé can't, and not worth writing when it only fills a slot.

## Final check before you submit

- Does the letter answer a question the résumé raises?
- Is it under one page, and would it hold up if the reader only skimmed the first two lines?
- Have you handled time zone or location, if relevant?
- Is every claim true and something you can discuss?

If you can say yes to these, send it with confidence. If the honest answer is that it adds nothing, skipping an optional letter is a reasonable, professional choice.
