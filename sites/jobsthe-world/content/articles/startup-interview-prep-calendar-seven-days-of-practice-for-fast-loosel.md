Small startups rarely run interviews the way large employers do. You might get a thirty-minute chat with a founder who is also answering support tickets, followed by an unannounced working session, followed by a question like "How would you approach our onboarding problem?" There may be no rubric and no HR coordinator to tell you what comes next.

You can't predict the format. You can practice the skills every format draws on: understanding the business quickly, reasoning out loud, and showing how you'd work with a small team. This seven-day calendar assumes about 45 to 90 minutes a day and a remote-first setup, where most of your rounds will happen over video and your written communication counts as part of the evaluation.

If your interview is sooner than a week away, compress the days, but keep the order. The early days feed the later ones.

## The seven-day plan at a glance

| Day | Focus | Output you should have by the end |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Company research map | One page: product, customers, money, open questions |
| 2 | Your story and fit | A 90-second intro and three examples |
| 3 | Thinking aloud | Two recorded practice answers, reviewed |
| 4 | Product and role critique | Three specific ideas tied to the role |
| 5 | Working-session rehearsal | One timed mini-task, plus a note on your assumptions |
| 6 | Full mock and your questions | A mock run with a friend and a list of questions for founders |
| 7 | Logistics and light review | Tested setup, calm notes, a follow-up draft |

## Day 1: Build a research map, not a pile of tabs

Startup research fails when it becomes passive reading. Your goal is to answer four questions in writing:

- **What do they sell, and to whom?** Use the website, pricing page, product demos, help documentation and any public changelog.
- **How do they make money, or plan to?** Look for pricing tiers, customer logos, job listings and any funding announcements. Be careful with inference. If you can't tell, write "unclear" and make it a question.
- **What is changing?** Recent hires, new job postings, blog posts, launches and founder posts on professional networks suggest priorities.
- **Where might the role fit?** Read the job post twice. Circle what sounds like an urgent problem versus a nice-to-have.

Then use the product if you can. Sign up for a free trial, or watch a demo. Write down what confused you in the first five minutes. That note is often more valuable than a polished compliment.

End the day by listing three things you don't know. Those become your smartest questions later.

## Day 2: Prepare your story for a loose conversation

Founders often open with "So, tell me about yourself," then follow whatever thread interests them. Prepare for that by building modular material instead of a memorized script.

Write a 90-second introduction with three parts: what you do now, a thread connecting your past work, and why this kind of company appeals to you. Be specific about the last part. "I like fast-moving teams" is weak. "I've done my best work when I owned a problem end to end with few handoffs" is better, provided it's true.

Next, choose three examples from your work:

1. Something you started with little direction.
2. Something that went wrong and what you changed.
3. A time you worked across functions or with someone senior who had limited time.

For each, jot down the situation, your decision, the result, and what you'd do differently. Skip any numbers you can't back up. Startups tend to probe details, and an inflated claim unravels fast.

## Day 3: Practice thinking out loud

This is the core skill. Open-ended questions are usually testing how you structure ambiguity, not whether you land on a hidden right answer.

Use a simple spoken pattern:

1. **Restate the question** in your own words and check it.
2. **Ask one or two clarifying questions**, such as who the user is or what success looks like.
3. **State your assumptions** explicitly.
4. **Walk through your approach** in two or three steps.
5. **Name a tradeoff or risk**, then commit to a recommendation.

Pick two prompts and record yourself on video or audio, five minutes each. Examples:

- "How would you figure out why new users stop using our product after a week?"
- "We have no marketing budget. How would you find our first fifty customers?"

Adapt them to your field. An engineer might get "How would you design a simple notification system?" and an operations candidate might get "Our support inbox is overwhelmed. What do you do first?"

Play the recording back and check for three things: did you go silent for long stretches, did you jump to a solution before clarifying, and did you finish with a clear recommendation? Fix one habit and re-record.

## Day 4: Prepare a product and role critique

Many founders like candidates who have already thought about their problems. Use your Day 1 map to prepare three observations, each in this shape: what you noticed, why it may matter, and what you'd test.

For example, if you noticed a confusing signup step, you wouldn't say "your onboarding is bad." You'd say what happened, guess at the effect, and propose a small experiment or a question you'd ask the team.

Keep the tone curious. You've seen the product for a few days, and they've lived with it for months. Phrases like "I may be missing context, but..." are honest and keep the conversation open.

Then draft a rough first-30-days outline for the role: who you'd talk to, what you'd read, and what small thing you might ship or fix. Treat it as a conversation starter, not a promise.

## Day 5: Rehearse the working session

Startups often skip long take-home projects in favor of a live exercise, or they assign a short task with vague instructions. Either way, practice the habits that make you easy to work with:

- Set a timer for 45 to 60 minutes and give yourself a small, realistic task from your field: outline a launch plan, review a rough landing page, debug a small script, or draft a customer email.
- Narrate your choices as you go.
- Write a short note at the end listing your assumptions, what you'd do with more time, and what you'd ask the team.

If a real take-home arrives, ask about expected time and format before starting. Reasonable companies will answer. If an exercise seems to ask for substantial unpaid work that resembles their real product needs, it's fair to raise that politely.

For remote roles, written clarity counts. Practice sending a concise message that summarizes a task, a decision and a next step.

## Day 6: Run a full mock and prepare your questions

Ask a friend or peer to play the founder. Give them permission to be unstructured: interrupt, change topics, ask a vague question, and then say "What would you do?" The aim is practice recovering when the conversation doesn't follow your outline.

Run 30 to 40 minutes, then ask for feedback on clarity, pacing and whether your answers sounded specific to this company.

Afterward, finalize your questions for them. Good questions at a startup are practical:

- What does success look like in the first three months?
- What's the hardest problem the team is working on right now?
- How are decisions made when founders disagree?
- How does the team communicate across time zones, and what's expected for overlap hours?
- What would make this role a bad fit?

Also consider how your location affects the details. If you're in Canada, Australia or the UK and the company is US-based, ask whether they hire you as an employee through a local arrangement or as a contractor, how pay currency is handled, and how benefits and leave work. Terms differ by country and by company, so ask rather than assume. You don't need to settle all of this in a first call, but it helps to know early.

## Day 7: Check logistics and keep it light

Don't cram. Spend the day on setup and calm review.

- **Test your tech.** Check camera, microphone, internet and the exact video tool they've named. Have a phone hotspot or backup plan.
- **Confirm time zones.** Check the invitation's time zone, especially if the founder is in a different country. Daylight saving changes happen on different dates across the US, Canada, the UK and Australia, which has caught people out.
- **Prepare a one-page note.** Include your research map, three examples, three observations and your questions. Keep it where you can glance at it.
- **Draft your thank-you.** Write a short follow-up skeleton, then add one specific detail from the conversation afterward.

Sleep matters more than another hour of reading.

## Handling the unexpected during the interview

Even with preparation, surprises will come. A few steady responses help:

- **If the format changes:** Say "Happy to do that. Can I take a minute to clarify what you're hoping to see?"
- **If you don't know:** Say what you do know, how you'd find out, and who you'd ask.
- **If you freeze:** Restate the question slowly. It buys time and often unlocks the next step.
- **If the founder talks a lot:** Listen for what they care about and connect your examples to it.

## A note on judging them back

Loose structure can be energizing, but it can also signal disorganization. Notice whether the founders respect your time, answer questions plainly and describe the role consistently across conversations. Their answers about runway, priorities and expectations deserve attention. You're not just trying to pass; you're deciding whether you want to work there.

## Quick recap

Spend the first two days understanding the company and your own story. Use days three through five to practice thinking aloud, offering useful observations and working on a small task. Save day six for a messy mock and day seven for logistics and rest. If you do that, an unpredictable interview becomes a conversation you've already rehearsed from several angles.
