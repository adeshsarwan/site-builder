When you work in an office, your manager picks up a lot about you without trying. They see you at your desk, hear you on a call, and notice you helping a colleague. Remote work removes all of that. Your manager has your output and your messages, and that's about it.

For a newcomer this is both a risk and an opening. The risk is that silence gets read as slowness, or as being stuck. The opening is that a few well-built updates can make you look more reliable than someone who sits ten feet from the boss and says nothing.

This guide gives you formats you can copy, a way to decide which one to use, and some habits that keep your updates from turning into noise.

## Why managers skip updates (and what they read instead)

Most managers aren't ignoring you. They're triaging dozens of messages, and updates tend to get skipped for predictable reasons:

- **They're too long.** If the first line doesn't tell the reader whether action is needed, they postpone it, and then forget it.
- **They narrate activity instead of outcomes.** 'Worked on the onboarding doc' tells a manager nothing about where things stand.
- **They bury the ask.** A blocker in paragraph four is a blocker nobody sees.
- **They arrive unpredictably.** A manager can't build a habit around updates that come at random.

The updates that get read share three traits: they're **predictable** in timing, **scannable** in format, and **clear about what's needed** from the reader, even if the answer is 'nothing'.

## Before you write: ask three setup questions

In your first week, ask your manager these directly. It takes five minutes and saves weeks of guessing.

1. **Where do you want updates?** A team channel, a direct message, a project tool, or email. Don't choose for them.
2. **How often?** Daily, a few times a week, or weekly. Newcomers usually benefit from a slightly higher frequency at first, then a step down once trust builds.
3. **What counts as urgent?** Ask what they'd want to hear about immediately, as opposed to in the next regular update.

Write the answers down and repeat them back: 'So I'll post a short update every Friday in the team channel, and message you directly if something's blocking me for more than half a day. Does that sound right?' You've now set the system, and your manager has agreed to it.

## The core formats

### 1. The weekly progress update

This is your anchor. Send it at the same time each week, ideally before your manager's planning or one-to-one time.

**Template:**

> **Week of [date]: [Your name]**
>
> **Headline:** [One sentence: overall status, e.g., 'On track for the Q3 report; one dependency at risk.']
>
> **Done:**
> - [Outcome, not activity. 'Finished customer-segment analysis; findings in the shared folder.']
> - [Outcome]
>
> **Next week:**
> - [Priority 1]
> - [Priority 2]
>
> **Blocked or at risk:**
> - [What, who could unblock it, by when. Or 'Nothing at the moment.']
>
> **Need from you:** [A decision, a review, or 'Nothing; FYI only.']

The 'Need from you' line is the most useful one. It lets your manager know in two seconds whether they have to act.

### 2. The short daily check-in

If your manager asked for daily updates, or you're in your first few weeks and want to be visible, keep it to three lines:

> **Today:** [Top 1–2 things]
> **Progress since yesterday:** [One line]
> **Blockers:** [None / specific item]

If you're writing more than this every day, it's too much. Daily updates should take under two minutes to write and thirty seconds to read.

### 3. The blocker flag

Don't save blockers for your weekly update. Flag them separately, as soon as you've given the problem a fair try on your own. A good blocker message is self-contained, so the reader can act without asking you questions.

**Template:**

> **Blocked: [short label]**
>
> **What I'm trying to do:** [One sentence]
> **What's in the way:** [Specific issue: missing access, unclear requirement, waiting on a person]
> **What I've tried:** [One or two items]
> **What would unblock me:** [Specific action or person]
> **Impact if unresolved by [date/time]:** [What slips]

The 'what I've tried' line shows initiative, and the 'impact' line helps your manager decide how urgent it is without guessing.

### 4. The decision request

Sometimes you don't need help, only a choice made. Make it easy to answer.

> **Decision needed by [date]: [topic]**
>
> **Option A:** [Description, main tradeoff]
> **Option B:** [Description, main tradeoff]
> **My recommendation:** [A or B, and one reason]
>
> If I don't hear back by [date], I'll go ahead with [default].

The default-if-silent line keeps your work moving, and it still gives your manager a chance to object.

### 5. The completion note

When you finish something meaningful, say so once, briefly, and link to it:

> **Done: [project/task].** [One-sentence result.] [Link.] Let me know if you'd like changes; otherwise I'll treat this as final and move on to [next item].

This stops work from vanishing into a folder that nobody opens.

## Which format to use when

| Situation | Format | Where | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular rhythm, any week | Weekly progress update | Agreed channel, same day each week | 5–8 lines |
| First weeks, or manager asks for more visibility | Daily check-in | Direct message or team thread | 3 lines |
| Stuck and it affects a deadline | Blocker flag | Direct message, plus tag the person who can help | 5 short lines |
| Need a choice made | Decision request | Direct message or relevant thread | 4–6 lines |
| Finished a deliverable | Completion note | Where the work was discussed | 1–2 sentences + link |

## Writing rules that keep updates readable

### Lead with the conclusion

Put the status first: 'On track,' 'At risk,' or 'Blocked.' Your manager shouldn't have to read the whole message to learn which one applies.

### Write outcomes, not hours

Compare these:

- Weak: 'Spent time on the vendor spreadsheet.'
- Strong: 'Vendor comparison complete; recommending Vendor B on cost and support terms. Review needed.'

The second tells your manager what exists now that didn't before.

### Name owners and dates

Vague phrases like 'waiting on the team' or 'will follow up soon' leave your manager unsure what to do. Say who, what and when: 'Waiting on Priya for the data export; she said Thursday. I'll chase Friday morning if it hasn't arrived.'

### Use links instead of pasting content

Link to the document, ticket or dashboard. Keep the message itself to the summary so it stays quick to scan.

### Separate FYI from action

Label things clearly. If your manager has to respond, say so at the top. If they don't, say that too. Managers learn to trust updates that tell them honestly when they can skip the details.

### Be honest about risk early

A flagged risk a week early is a planning input. The same risk revealed on deadline day is a surprise. Newcomers sometimes avoid raising problems because they fear looking incompetent. In practice, most managers respond better to 'here's the problem and my plan' than to a missed date.

## Keeping noise down

More updates aren't better updates. A few habits help:

- **Batch small items.** Don't send five messages in an hour; collect them into your next scheduled update unless something is urgent.
- **Use threads.** Reply in the original thread so context stays together.
- **Don't over-tag.** Mention only the people who need to act.
- **Skip the apology preamble.** 'Sorry to bother you' adds length and signals uncertainty. Just state what you need.
- **Cut anything nobody would miss.** If a line wouldn't change a decision or reduce a worry, delete it.

## Handling time zones and distributed teams

If you're in Sydney, your manager is in London and a colleague is in Toronto, your update may land while the reader is asleep. A few adjustments help:

- **State times with a zone.** Write '3 pm GMT' or 'Friday, end of day AEST' rather than 'end of day.'
- **Write so the reader can act without a reply from you.** Include the context, the options and the default, so they can respond when they're online.
- **Note public holidays and leave.** Holidays differ across the US, Canada, Australia and the UK. A line such as 'Out Monday for a public holiday here; will pick this up Tuesday' prevents confusion.
- **Time your update for their morning.** If possible, send it so it's waiting when your manager starts the day.

## A worked example

Here's how a weekly update might look for someone in their third week as a marketing coordinator:

> **Week of 9 June: Alex**
>
> **Headline:** On track for the newsletter launch; one approval pending.
>
> **Done:**
> - Drafted and edited all four newsletter sections; doc linked in the project board.
> - Set up the email template and ran a test send to the internal list.
>
> **Next week:**
> - Incorporate legal feedback and finalise copy.
> - Schedule the send and build the reporting view.
>
> **Blocked or at risk:**
> - Legal review of the offer wording. Requested Monday, no response yet. If not back by Wednesday, the Friday send date slips.
>
> **Need from you:** Could you nudge legal, or tell me who else can approve?

It runs well under 150 words, states its status in the first line, and tells the manager exactly what to do.

## Build the habit in your first 30 days

- **Week 1:** Ask the three setup questions and confirm the cadence in writing.
- **Week 2:** Send your first updates on schedule, using the formats above. Ask for feedback: 'Is this the right level of detail?'
- **Week 3:** Adjust based on what your manager says. Trim sections they ignore and add ones they ask about.
- **Week 4:** Review your own updates. Are you flagging blockers early? Are your 'done' items outcomes? Is there a clear ask?

Consistency matters more than polish. A plain update sent every Friday builds more trust than a beautifully written one sent whenever you remember.

## Final thought

An async update is a small piece of writing with one job: letting someone who can't see you work know where things stand and what, if anything, they need to do. Keep it predictable, lead with status, make your asks specific, and raise problems early. Over time your manager will stop wondering what you're doing, and that's when you can start asking for bigger responsibilities.
