You've probably heard that many jobs never make it to a job board. Some are filled by referral. Some are created around a person the team already trusts. Others sit in a manager's head for weeks before anyone writes a posting.

For remote-first seekers, this is both good and bad news. The good news is that distance matters less when the conversation happens by message or video. The bad news is that you can't bump into someone at a conference or in a hallway, so you have to start the conversation yourself.

Most people stall at that point. They don't know who to contact, and they worry that "Are you hiring?" sounds like begging. This guide covers both problems. It gives you five approaches, each built to give the other person something easy to say yes to, and none of them asks for a job.

## First, change what you're asking for

The fear of sounding desperate usually comes from asking for something large: a job, a referral, an hour of someone's time. Large asks put pressure on the other person, and people respond to pressure by going quiet.

Instead, ask for something small and specific:

- A two-line answer to a question
- A 15-minute call, with an easy way to decline
- A reaction to a piece of work you made
- A pointer to the right person

Small asks work because they cost the reader almost nothing. They also leave room for the bigger conversation to happen on its own, if there's a fit.

Before you send anything, do two things:

1. **Pick a narrow target.** "Remote customer success roles at B2B software companies" is a target. "Anything remote" isn't, and it makes you harder to help.
2. **Have a one-sentence description of your background.** For example: "I've spent six years in support operations and recently moved into onboarding for SaaS products." People can't refer someone they can't summarize.

## 1. React to a hiring signal, not a job post

A company doesn't need an open listing for you to have a reason to write. Look for moments when a team is likely to need people:

- A funding announcement or new product launch
- A leader announcing a new team or region
- A manager posting that they're "building out" a function
- A company opening a new market, such as expanding from the US into the UK or Australia
- A recent departure that someone mentions publicly

These signals tell you a need may be forming. Writing at that moment is timely rather than random.

**What to say:**

> Hi Dana, I saw the announcement about the new onboarding team. I work in implementation for mid-market SaaS and have been thinking about how teams handle the handoff from sales. Is that something your team is still shaping? Happy to share a short example of how I've approached it if useful, and no worries if the timing isn't right.

Notice what's missing: no résumé attachment, no "I'd love to work for you." You've shown you read the news, named your area of expertise, and offered something small.

**Who to contact:** the person who would lead the work, usually a team lead, director or head of function. Avoid sending to a general careers inbox, which is designed to route you back to the job board.

## 2. Ask someone doing the job for advice

People in the role you want can tell you things no job description does: how the team works, what skills matter, and whether hiring is expected. They also hear about openings early. Asking them for advice, rather than for a job, takes the pressure off the exchange.

Pick someone one or two steps ahead of you, not the CEO. They're more likely to reply and more recently remember what it was like to get in.

**What to say:**

> Hi Priya, I'm moving from in-house recruiting into remote talent operations and your path from coordinator to team lead stood out to me. Could I ask you two questions by message, or would a 15-minute call be easier? Specifically, I'm trying to understand which skills actually get tested in interviews for this kind of role.

Offering a message-only option is deliberate. Some people will happily answer in writing but won't commit to a call.

**Ground rules:**

- Prepare real questions. Don't ask "Can I pick your brain?"
- Keep to the time you asked for.
- Thank them and tell them what you did with their advice. A short follow-up two weeks later ("I rewrote my portfolio summary based on your tip and just applied for a role that fits") keeps the relationship warm.
- Near the end, you can ask, "Is there anyone else you'd suggest I talk to?" This is a natural way to get introductions without asking for a job.

## 3. Show your work where hiring managers already look

Some of the best conversations start because you did something visible first. You don't need a big following. You need a few specific, useful contributions in places where people in your field spend time: professional Slack or Discord communities, industry forums, comment threads on posts by team leads, or a small public portfolio.

Ideas that take a few hours, not weeks:

- Write a short teardown of a public product or process, with two or three concrete suggestions
- Answer a technical or practical question in detail in a community channel
- Share a template, checklist or small tool you built for your own work
- Post a short write-up of a project, including what went wrong and what you'd change

This approach is slower than direct outreach, but it changes the dynamic. When you later message someone, you're not a stranger. You're the person who wrote that useful thing.

**What to say after you've shared something:**

> Hi Marcus, I wrote up a short breakdown of how your help center handles refund requests, with three ideas for reducing back-and-forth. Thought it might be useful for your team. No reply needed, but I'd welcome your take if you have a minute.

Be honest in your suggestions and avoid sounding like a critic. Frame observations as ideas, not as failures you've spotted.

## 4. Revisit your existing contacts with a specific ask

People tend to think networking means meeting strangers. Often, the warmest leads are former colleagues, classmates, clients, managers and people you've volunteered with. They already know how you work.

The mistake here is sending a vague "I'm looking for opportunities, let me know if you hear of anything." It's hard to act on. Be specific, and make it easy to forward.

**What to say:**

> Hi Sam, it's been a while! I'm looking for a remote role in project coordination, ideally at a software or health-tech company with a distributed team. If anyone on your radar is building or growing a team like that, I'd be grateful for an introduction. Happy to send a two-line blurb you could forward.

The offer of a forwardable blurb is helpful. It saves your contact the work of describing you.

Before sending, spend a few minutes remembering the last time you worked with or helped that person. If you can mention it, do. A message that begins with a real shared memory feels very different from a mass request.

## 5. Write directly to the hiring manager, with a clear exit

When you've identified a company you'd like to work for, you can contact the likely hiring manager without an open role. This works best when the message is short, specific to their team, and honest about why you're writing.

A reliable structure is:

1. **One sentence of context**: how you found them or their work.
2. **One sentence of relevance**: what you do that connects to their team.
3. **One small question**: something they can answer in a line.
4. **An easy exit**: permission to ignore the message.

**What to say:**

> Hi Elena, I follow your team's public changelog and liked how you've handled documentation for the API. I'm a technical writer with experience on developer docs for payment products, working remotely across time zones. Do you expect to add documentation capacity this year? If not, no problem at all, and thank you for the useful writing.

If they reply with "not right now," respond graciously and ask if you can stay in touch. That reply is still progress. You're now on their radar when a role does open.

## Who to contact, and what to ask

| Target | Best for | Small ask |
|---|---|---|
| Hiring manager or team lead | Roles that may be forming | "Are you expecting to add capacity in X?" |
| Peer in the role | Honest insight and referrals | Two questions, by message or call |
| Former colleague | Warm introductions | "Who should I talk to about X?" |
| Community member or poster | Starting a relationship | A reaction to your shared work |
| Recruiter at the company | Awareness of upcoming roles | "Which profiles are you seeing demand for?" |

## Remote-specific details that matter

Remote hiring has its own wrinkles, and mentioning them shows you understand how distributed teams work.

- **Location and eligibility.** Companies often hire only in certain countries, states, provinces or time zones, and work authorization rules differ across the US, Canada, Australia and the UK. State your location and work eligibility plainly in your first conversation so nobody wastes time.
- **Time zones.** If you're in Australia and the team is mainly in North America, say what overlap hours you can offer. Clarity helps.
- **Terminology.** Use "resume" in the US and Canada and "CV" in the UK and Australia, matching the recipient's usage.
- **Written communication.** Remote teams hire people who write clearly. Your outreach message is itself a work sample, so keep it brief and well organized.

## Keep it sustainable

You don't need to send fifty messages a week. A steady pace is easier to maintain and produces better messages.

- Aim for a handful of well-researched messages each week, split across the five approaches.
- Track who you contacted, when, what you asked and when to follow up, in a simple spreadsheet.
- Follow up once, after about a week or two, with one line adding something new. Then let it go.
- Expect many messages to go unanswered. Silence usually means the person was busy, not that you did something wrong.

## When a conversation turns toward a role

If someone says, "We might be hiring, send me your resume," do it quickly, with a short note reminding them of your conversation. Tailor your resume to their team. If they refer you, thank them and keep them updated, whatever the outcome.

If there's no role, ask politely whether they'd mind if you checked back in a few months. Keep the relationship alive with occasional useful notes, not repeated requests.

## The takeaway

You don't find unlisted roles by asking strangers for jobs. You find them by being useful, specific and easy to answer. Pick one approach, write one message today, and make it small enough that the other person can say yes in thirty seconds.
