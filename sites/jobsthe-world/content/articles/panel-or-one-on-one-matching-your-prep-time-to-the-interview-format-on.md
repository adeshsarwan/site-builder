You get the calendar invite. It says "Panel interview, 60 minutes" or "Conversation with Priya, 45 minutes." You note the time, convert it from your time zone, and open the same prep document you used for your last interview.

That's the mistake. The format tells you how the conversation will run, and that should change how you spend your prep hours. A panel tests different skills than a one-on-one, and on video those differences get bigger.

This guide covers what changes, where to move your hours, and how to prepare for each format when everyone is on a screen.

## The core difference: one relationship versus several

In a one-on-one, you build rapport with one person who is also your sole source of information about the role. You can follow a thread, ask a question mid-answer, and adjust to one person's pace.

In a panel, you're managing several people at once. Each has a different agenda, a different slice of the role they care about, and often a pre-assigned set of questions. Your answers have to land with the hiring manager, a peer, and maybe someone from another team, all in the same sentence.

So:

- **One-on-one prep** is mostly about depth, rapport and a strong, flexible narrative.
- **Panel prep** is mostly about structure, concision, and knowing who is in the room.

## Quick comparison

| | One-on-one | Panel |
|---|---|---|
| Pace | Conversational, can wander | Structured, often scripted questions |
| Main risk | Rambling or going shallow on rapport | Losing the room, ignoring some members |
| Best prep investment | Deep stories and sharp questions for one person | Research on each interviewer, tight answers |
| Eye contact on video | One person, easier | Several tiles, harder to manage |
| Follow-up | One thank-you note | Individual notes, each tailored |
| Rehearsal style | Talk-through with one friend | Mock with two or three people if you can |

## How to move your hours

I'm not going to give you a magic ratio, because it depends on the role and how much you already know about the company. But here's a sensible way to think about a fixed block of prep time, say a few evenings.

**For a one-on-one**, put the largest share into your stories and your questions. Spend a bit less on interviewer research, because there's only one person to research, and more on practicing a natural back-and-forth.

**For a panel**, put noticeably more time into interviewer research and rehearsing answers out loud. Cut time you'd spend memorizing long, elaborate stories. In a panel, a ninety-second answer that's easy to follow beats a three-minute epic.

A rough guide, as a starting point and not a rule:

- **One-on-one:** most of your time on stories and questions, some on research, a small block on logistics and a run-through.
- **Panel:** a more even split between research, concise answers and mock practice, plus a bigger logistics block because there are more things that can go wrong on video.

## Preparing for a one-on-one

### Treat it as a conversation you steer

With one interviewer, you have more room to shape the discussion. That's an advantage only if you've prepared for it.

1. **Build five to seven flexible stories.** Cover a result you drove, a conflict, a failure, a time you worked with little direction, and a time you changed your mind. For remote-first roles, include one about working asynchronously across time zones.
2. **Practice bridging.** If the interviewer asks about a project, be ready to connect it to the job you're applying for in one sentence.
3. **Prepare questions that depend on their role.** If you're talking to the hiring manager, ask how success is measured in the first six months. If it's a future teammate, ask how work gets handed off between time zones and what a typical week looks like.
4. **Plan your opening.** The "tell me about yourself" answer matters more here because there's no one else to dilute it. Keep it to about a minute and a half, built around the role.

### Watch for the quiet interviewer

Some one-on-one interviewers say little and let you fill the silence. Prepare for that by having a short list of check-in phrases: "Would it help if I went deeper on the budget side?" or "I can give you an example if that's useful." This keeps you from rambling without losing warmth.

## Preparing for a panel

### Find out who is in the room

Ask the recruiter for names and titles, or look at the invite. Then look at each person's public professional profile and any talks, articles or posts connected to the work. You're looking for one thing per person: what do they probably care about?

- A hiring manager usually cares about outcomes and fit with the team.
- A peer often cares about how you'll collaborate and whether you'll be easy to work with.
- A person from another department usually cares about handoffs and communication.
- A senior leader, if present, often listens for judgment and how you think.

Write the names on a sticky note beside your screen with one phrase each. You can address people by name when you answer, which helps in a video call where it's hard to tell who is speaking.

### Rehearse short answers, out loud

Panel questions are often distributed, so each interviewer asks one or two. You may get less time per answer than you would one-on-one. Practice a structure you can finish in about a minute and a half to two minutes: situation, what you did, result, what you'd do differently.

Then practice **answering the whole room**. Even if one person asks the question, spend your answer addressing the panel. On video, that means looking at your camera for most of it and glancing at the tiles occasionally.

### Plan for the awkward moments

- **Two questions at once.** Say "I'll take them in order" and note them down.
- **A panelist is silent or frowning.** Don't chase them. Keep your answer going and, when there's a natural pause, invite questions from the group.
- **Someone interrupts.** Let them. It usually means they're interested in a particular point.
- **A question you didn't prepare for.** Pause for a breath and say "Let me think for a second." Panels are typically fine with that.

## The remote layer: what changes on video

Video affects both formats, but panels expose more weaknesses.

**Camera and framing.** Put your camera at eye level and sit far enough back that your shoulders are visible. For a panel, make your video as large as possible so you can see everyone's faces without moving your eyes much.

**Screen layout.** Before the call, test how the platform shows participants. In a panel, switch to gallery view if you can. In a one-on-one, a larger single view of the interviewer feels more natural.

**Audio.** A headset or good earbuds usually helps more than a better camera. Test it with a friend, not just the built-in test button.

**Backup plan.** Keep the recruiter's phone number or email handy and know what you'll do if your connection drops. Having a phone hotspot ready is a sensible precaution for either format.

**Time zones.** If you're in the US, Canada, Australia or the UK, convert the time yourself and confirm the day. A call that falls on Tuesday morning in London may be Monday evening in Toronto or Tuesday evening in Sydney. Daylight saving changes in March, April, October and November don't happen on the same dates in all four countries, so double-check if the interview falls near one of those changes. Reply to the invite with the time and your time zone written out.

**Notes.** For a panel, keep a notes file open with names, your key stories and your questions. Don't read from it. Glance, then look up.

## A simple prep plan for each format

### Two days before a one-on-one

- Choose your top stories and practice them aloud once each.
- Write three or four questions tailored to your interviewer's role.
- Read the job description again and highlight the three skills it emphasizes.
- Do a short test call to check lighting and audio.

### Two days before a panel

- Get names and roles, then write one line per person.
- Cut your longest answers down to under two minutes.
- Run a mock with at least one other person, or record yourself and watch it.
- Prepare one question for each panelist, plus a couple for the group.
- Test the platform in the same room, at the same time of day if possible.

### The day of, either format

- Join a few minutes early, with the link already open.
- Close other apps and silence notifications.
- Have water nearby.
- Start with a warm greeting, and use names if it's a panel.

## After the interview

For a one-on-one, send a short thank-you the same day, referring to a specific part of the conversation.

For a panel, send a brief note to each person if you have their contact details, or to the recruiter asking them to share it. Make each message different. Mention something that person asked or discussed. If one panelist raised a concern, a short, calm follow-up addressing it can help.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the same answers for both.** Panel versions should be shorter and more structured.
- **Ignoring the quieter panelists.** They may have a vote.
- **Not asking who will attend.** It's a normal question, and recruiters usually answer it.
- **Over-preparing for content and under-preparing for delivery.** Stories matter, but so do pacing, eye contact and audio.
- **Skipping a mock panel.** One run-through with two friends will show you how hard it is to split attention.

## The takeaway

Look at the format on the invite and ask what could go wrong in that setup. In a one-on-one, it's usually rapport and rambling. In a panel, it's structure, attention and technical friction. Spend your hours where that risk is highest, and you'll go in better prepared than a candidate who simply prepares longer.
