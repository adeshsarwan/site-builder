You started a remote job a few weeks ago. You've shipped work, joined the calls and answered the messages. Your manager's response so far is a thumbs-up emoji and the occasional "thanks."

That's not much to go on. Is silence a compliment? A sign you're being quietly written off? Or is your manager just buried in meetings?

You can't tell from the outside, and guessing wastes energy. The better move is to run a short, low-drama experiment: ask three specific questions, then set up a routine so you never have to guess again.

## Why silence is so hard to read when you work remotely

In an office, you pick up small signals without trying. You see how your manager reacts in a meeting, whether they stop by your desk, or whether they look stressed. Remote work removes most of that. What's left is written messages, scheduled calls and whatever you can infer from response times.

Silence is also ambiguous because several different situations produce it:

| What you observe | Likely meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Quick replies on logistics, little comment on quality | Work is on track; manager only speaks up by exception | Ask for calibration (Question 2) |
| Slow replies to everyone, canceled one-on-ones | Manager is overloaded | Make feedback easy and low-effort (Question 3) |
| Vague answers when you ask directly, shorter replies than at the start | Possible unspoken concern | Ask for specifics and a clear definition of success (Question 1) |
| Other teammates get detailed comments, you get none | Could be trust, could be style differences | Compare gently, then ask directly |
| Praise is generic ("great job!") but no specifics | Manager may not know how to give detailed feedback | Offer a structure they can reuse |

Treat the middle column as a hypothesis, not a verdict. The point of the questions below is to replace your hypotheses with information.

## Before you ask: three minutes of preparation

Going in cold makes the conversation vague. Spend a few minutes on this first:

1. **List what you've delivered.** Three to five items is plenty: projects finished, problems solved, processes you've picked up.
2. **Note what you're unsure about.** Maybe it's how detailed your documentation should be, or whether you should post updates in a channel or send them privately.
3. **Check what's already written down.** Your job description, onboarding plan, team goals and any review criteria may already answer part of this. Asking something that's in a document you were sent looks careless.

Then book time. Don't try to squeeze these questions into a chat message. If you already have a recurring one-on-one, use it. If you don't, request one with a clear purpose: "Could we grab 20 minutes this week? I'd like to check I'm focused on the right things in my first couple of months."

## The three questions

### Question 1: "What does a strong first 90 days look like in this role, and how will we both know I'm there?"

This question moves the conversation from feelings ("am I doing okay?") to criteria. It's hard to give feedback against an unstated standard, and many managers haven't actually written theirs down.

Listen for:

- **Concrete outcomes**, such as "ticket backlog under control" or "you've run a client call on your own."
- **Behaviors**, such as "flags risks early" or "writes clear handoff notes."
- **Timing**, meaning when things are expected, not just what.

If the answer is fuzzy, follow up with: "Could you give me one or two examples of work that met that bar?" Examples are more useful than adjectives.

Afterward, send a short written recap: "Here's what I heard: X, Y and Z by the end of month three. Let me know if I've missed anything." This gives your manager a chance to correct you and gives you a reference point later.

### Question 2: "From what you've seen so far, what's one thing I should keep doing and one thing I should adjust?"

Notice the shape of this question. It doesn't ask "how am I doing?" which invites a polite "great!" and ends the conversation. It asks for two specific items, one positive and one developmental, which makes a vague answer awkward.

Why "one" of each? Because it's easy to answer. A busy manager can think of one thing in ten seconds. It also keeps you from walking away with a long list of criticism you can't act on.

If your manager says "honestly, nothing comes to mind," don't push for flaws. Try a different angle: "That's good to hear. Is there anything you'd like to see more of as I settle in?" Sometimes people simply have no complaints. Sometimes they need a prompt about the future, not the past.

If you do hear a criticism, resist the urge to explain or defend. Say thanks, ask one clarifying question ("Could you point me to a recent example?"), and take notes. You can respond properly after you've thought it over.

### Question 3: "What's the best way for me to get feedback from you, and how often?"

This question is the one that fixes the problem long term. It admits something true: your manager has a preferred rhythm and format, and you don't know what it is yet.

Offer options so your manager can choose rather than invent a system on the spot:

- A standing weekly or fortnightly one-on-one (even 15 minutes)
- A written update from you, with comments from them
- Short voice or video notes on specific deliverables
- Comments directly in documents or pull requests
- A brief check-in after each major project

Some managers prefer written feedback because it fits around their calendar. Others find text too slow or too blunt and would rather talk. You can't know until you ask.

Also ask about **urgency**: "If something's going wrong, how would you want me to hear it: a message, a call?" This matters for the quiet manager, because it tells you whether no news really means good news.

## Turn the answers into a routine

One conversation isn't a feedback system. The routine is what keeps feedback flowing after the novelty fades. Here's a simple version you can adapt.

### The weekly update (10 minutes to write)

Send it at the same time each week, in the channel your manager picked. Keep it short enough to read on a phone:

- **Done this week:** two or three outcomes, not a task log
- **Next week:** top priorities
- **Blockers or decisions needed:** anything where you need input
- **One question for feedback:** "Was the level of detail in the client summary right?"

That last line is the key. A specific question gets a specific answer, even from someone who would never write a paragraph of feedback unprompted.

### The 30-day check-in

Put a recurring reminder on your calendar. Come back to the three questions and ask: "We talked about X, Y, Z being the goals. How do you see progress?" Because you've already agreed on the standard, this can take ten minutes.

### A private feedback log

Keep a running note with date, comment, and what you did about it. It helps you spot patterns, and it gives you real material for a formal review. If your employer has a formal probation or review period, it may also help you understand when and how your performance will be assessed. Check your contract or employee handbook for the details, since rules differ by country, state or province, and employer.

## If your manager answers, but the answers are still thin

Some people just aren't natural feedback-givers. You can make it easier for them:

- **Offer a draft, not a blank page.** "Here's what I think went well and what I'd improve. What would you add?" Reacting is easier than originating.
- **Narrow the question.** Instead of "Any feedback?" try "Was the summary too long?" or "Should I have looped in design earlier?"
- **Ask a skip-level or peer for context.** A trusted teammate can tell you how your manager usually communicates, such as "she only comments when something's wrong" or "he gives feedback in the quarterly review." Keep it about style, not gossip.
- **Try asking in a different medium.** If chat gets nothing, a short call may work. If calls feel rushed, send a written question they can answer when they have space.

## When silence is a real warning sign

Most of the time, quiet is neither good nor bad. But take it seriously if you notice a combination of these:

- Repeated refusal to set or keep one-on-one time, even after you've asked directly
- Work being quietly reassigned or you being left out of meetings you used to attend
- Vague answers to every question about expectations, month after month
- Sudden changes in tone after a previously warm start

If that's your situation, make the request more direct and put it in writing: "I'd like to be sure I'm meeting expectations. Could we set aside time this week to review my progress?" If the pattern continues, talk to HR or a mentor inside the company, and consider whether the role is a good fit. Don't assume the worst before you've asked, but don't ignore a pattern either.

## A short script you can use today

If you want a ready-made opener, adapt this:

> "I'm enjoying getting up to speed and want to make sure I'm focused on what matters most to you. Could we take 20 minutes to talk about what success looks like in my first few months, what I'm doing well and should keep doing, what I should adjust, and how you'd like me to keep you updated? I'll come prepared with a quick summary of what I've done so far."

It's polite, specific and shows you're proactive without sounding insecure.

## The takeaway

Silence from a new manager isn't a message. It's a gap, and you're allowed to fill it. Ask what success looks like, ask for one thing to keep and one to change, and agree on how feedback will reach you. Then keep the loop going with a short weekly update and a regular check-in.

In a remote job, you can't wait for feedback to wander over to your desk. Build the channel yourself, and you'll know where you stand long before your first formal review.
