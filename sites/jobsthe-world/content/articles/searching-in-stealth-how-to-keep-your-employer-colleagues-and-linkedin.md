Most stealth searches don't fail because someone hacked your email. They fail because of small, ordinary leaks: a LinkedIn update that pings your network, an interview invite on a shared calendar, a recruiter who phones your manager "for a reference" too early.

Remote work makes some of this easier, since nobody sees you slip out at lunchtime. It also makes some of it riskier, because almost everything you do runs through tools your employer may own, administer or monitor.

This guide walks through what to set up *before* you send a single application.

## Step 1: Check what you're allowed to do (and what's monitored)

Before you change a setting, spend ten minutes on the paperwork and the tech.

**Read your contract and handbook.** Look for:

- **Notice period.** In the UK, Canada and Australia, your contract or employment standards may set a notice period you'll need to work out. That affects when you can tell a new employer you can start.
- **Non-solicitation and confidentiality clauses.** These matter when you talk about clients, projects or colleagues in interviews.
- **Acceptable-use or device policies.** These usually say what the company can monitor on its equipment.

Rules differ by country, state and province, and by contract. If a clause is unclear, a short consult with an employment lawyer or a local employment-standards office is worth it.

**Assume anything on a company device or account is visible to someone.** That includes:

- Your work laptop, including browser history, installed software and screenshots
- Company email, chat and calendar
- Company VPN or network traffic
- Files saved to company cloud storage

Whether anyone actually looks is a separate question. The safe rule: if it touches your job search, it doesn't go on a work device, work account or work login.

## Step 2: Build a clean separation

Do this first, because it's hard to undo later.

### Use personal everything

- **Email:** A personal address with a professional name. Don't use an address that forwards to or from your work account.
- **Phone:** Your personal mobile for recruiter calls and texts. If you don't want to give out your main number, a separate free calling number app works, but check it doesn't sync to anything your employer controls.
- **Devices:** A personal laptop or tablet for applications, video interviews and file editing. If you only have a work laptop, use a library, coworking space or a trusted person's machine for sensitive steps.
- **Browser:** Don't log into job sites in a browser profile that's signed into your work Google or Microsoft account. Browser sync can carry history and saved logins across.
- **Files:** Keep your résumé and portfolio in personal cloud storage. Never email your résumé to yourself from work "to print later."

### Prep the résumé itself

- Remove anything confidential: client names you're not permitted to share, internal metrics, product details not yet public.
- Describe results in ranges or relative terms if exact figures are sensitive.
- Don't use your employer's email signature, logo or templates.

## Step 3: Lock down LinkedIn

LinkedIn is the loudest place to job hunt quietly. The menu names and locations change, so check the current layout, but these are the settings to look for.

| What to look for | What to do | Remaining risk |
|---|---|---|
| Sharing profile updates with your network | Turn it **off** before editing anything | Visible changes still show if someone views your profile |
| "Open to work" | If you use it, choose the **recruiters-only** option, not the public banner | LinkedIn says it tries to hide the signal from recruiters at your company, but it can't be guaranteed |
| Profile viewing mode | Consider semi-private or private mode while researching | You may lose visibility into who views you; recruiters may be less likely to click through |
| Connections visibility | Set to "only you" if you don't want people tracing your network | Others can still see mutual connections |
| Activity and follows | Review what's public: likes, comments, followed companies | Comments on a competitor's post are visible if your activity is public |
| Email and notifications | Use a personal email for the account where possible | Work email on the account may still get alerts |

### Edit your profile in small, quiet steps

A sudden full rewrite, a new headshot and fifteen new skills in one afternoon is a signal in itself. Instead:

1. Turn off update-sharing first.
2. Make changes gradually over a couple of weeks.
3. Keep your headline conventional. Skip "Seeking new opportunities."
4. Update your "About" section to describe what you do well, not what you want next.

### Think about the signals you can't hide

- **Requesting recommendations** pings the person asked and may reach your colleagues.
- **Connecting with a burst of recruiters** can show up in someone's feed.
- **Viewing hiring managers' profiles** while in public mode tells them you looked.
- **Messaging a colleague** about a role at a company you're targeting can travel.

If your current employer has a strong LinkedIn culture, consider doing the bulk of your outreach by direct email or through referrals instead.

## Step 4: Work with recruiters and job boards carefully

Recruiters can be your best stealth asset or your biggest leak. Before you share your résumé:

- **Say it explicitly:** "I'm employed and my search is confidential. Please don't submit me anywhere or contact anyone without my written okay."
- **Ask who they'll send it to.** You don't want your résumé reaching a client who's a customer of your employer, or your own company's HR team.
- **Be wary of mass-blast agencies.** The more places your résumé lands, the less control you have.
- **On job boards,** use confidential or hidden-résumé settings if offered. A publicly searchable résumé can be found by anyone, including colleagues.

On application forms, you'll often see a question about contacting your current employer. Answer **no** or "not at this stage" where it's an option. If it's a required yes/no with no flexibility, add a comment: "Happy to provide once an offer is made."

## Step 5: Get the timing of references right

References are where a quiet search most often becomes a noisy one.

### The sequence that protects you

1. **Application stage:** Don't provide references. If asked, say you'll share them when you're at the final stage.
2. **Final interview:** Offer references who are **not** your current manager or team: former managers, previous colleagues, clients you no longer work with, or senior people who have left.
3. **Offer stage:** Ask that the offer be conditional on references, and that your current employer be contacted only after you've accepted.
4. **Before any contact:** Confirm in writing which names they'll call and when.

### Choose references who can keep a secret

- Ask someone **in private, by personal channels**, not through work chat.
- Tell them what role you're pursuing and what you'd like them to emphasize.
- Ask them to flag it to you if someone from your current company reaches out.

If a prospective employer insists on speaking to your current manager before an offer, that's a reasonable moment to pause. You can say, "I'd be glad to arrange that once there's an offer I'm ready to accept." Most employers who want you will accept it. If they won't, weigh that against how much risk you're willing to take.

## Step 6: Schedule interviews without leaving a trail

Remote-first jobs make this simpler, but only if you plan around the calendar and the camera.

### Calendar hygiene

- **Never put interviews on your work calendar.** Even "Busy" or "Appointment" blocks can show details depending on sharing settings.
- **Keep a private personal calendar,** and decline invitations sent to your work address.
- If your work calendar is visible to colleagues, block time with an ordinary label such as "Focus" or "Personal appointment," consistent with how you normally use it.
- Don't suddenly start blocking out every Tuesday at 1 p.m.

### Choose interview slots smartly

- **Early morning or evening** slots, especially with employers in other time zones, avoid the workday altogether.
- **Lunch breaks** work if you take them regularly. Don't stretch them without cause.
- **Half-days or personal time off** are fine, and you don't need to explain the reason beyond what your policy requires.
- **Batch interviews** into the same day or week where possible, so you're not repeatedly "unavailable."

### Handle the practicalities

- Use a **personal device and personal network** where possible. Join from a quiet spot with a neutral background.
- Don't use your work video-conferencing licence, even if it's convenient.
- Check your **status indicators**: if your chat tool shows "In a meeting" or "Offline" at odd times, someone might notice. Set it as you normally would.
- Test your audio and camera beforehand so you're not scrambling on a work machine at the last minute.

### Be honest, not specific

If a recruiter asks why you're looking, you can say what's true without naming names: "I'm looking for a bigger scope," or "The role has changed from what I was hired to do." Avoid trashing your employer or revealing confidential information.

## Step 7: Prepare for the "someone found out" scenario

Even with careful setup, there's some chance word gets out. Have a plan:

- **Stay calm and factual.** "I'm exploring options, and I'm committed to my work here in the meantime" is truthful and professional.
- **Keep performing.** Missed deadlines or reduced responsiveness will cause more trouble than the search itself.
- **Don't lie outright** if asked directly. You can decline to discuss it, but a flat denial can damage trust if it later comes out.
- **Know your position:** how replaceable you are, your notice terms and your finances all affect how a conversation might go.

## A pre-application checklist

Before you apply anywhere, confirm that you've:

- [ ] Reviewed your contract for notice, confidentiality and non-solicit terms
- [ ] Moved all search activity to personal devices, email and phone
- [ ] Turned off LinkedIn update-sharing and reviewed visibility settings
- [ ] Chosen recruiters-only "Open to work," or none at all
- [ ] Told recruiters your search is confidential
- [ ] Lined up references who aren't your current manager
- [ ] Set up a personal calendar and planned interview slots
- [ ] Scrubbed confidential details from your résumé

## The bottom line

Stealth isn't about secrecy for its own sake. It's about keeping control of the timing, so your employer hears about your plans from you, on your schedule, with an offer in hand. Do the setup first, move gradually, and keep your current work solid while you search.
