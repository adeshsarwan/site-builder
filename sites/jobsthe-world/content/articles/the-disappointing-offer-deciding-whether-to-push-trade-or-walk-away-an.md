You expected one number and got a lower one. Maybe the base is under your target, the title is a level down, or the "flexible remote policy" turned out to have conditions. Before you reply, slow down. A disappointing offer asks you to make three separate decisions: how hard you can push, what to ask for, and what must be on paper before you sign.

## Step 1: Separate the feeling from the facts

Disappointment is information, but it isn't analysis. Give yourself a day. It is normal to say, "Thank you, I'm excited about this. I'd like to review the full details and come back to you by [date]." Reasonable employers expect it.

While you wait, write down:

- **Your number.** Not the number you hoped for, but your walk-away number: the lowest total package you would accept without resentment.
- **Your alternatives.** Other offers, active interviews, your current job, or a savings runway.
- **What's actually below expectations.** Base pay, bonus, equity, benefits, level or scope? Each has a different fix.

## Step 2: Judge your leverage honestly

Leverage is not how much you want the job. It is how costly it would be for the employer to lose you, and how costly it would be for you to lose them.

### Signs you have more leverage

- You hold a competing offer or a late-stage process elsewhere.
- You are employed and comfortable staying.
- The role has been open a long time, or the team has been hunting for a specialized skill you clearly have.
- The hiring manager has been visibly enthusiastic and involved in the process.
- Your skills are hard to find in the time zone or region they need.

### Signs you have less leverage

- You are out of work with a short runway, and you'd struggle to say no.
- The company interviewed many similar candidates and the field is crowded.
- The role is junior, with a clearly defined pay band.
- The company is small or early-stage and the budget is genuinely tight.

You can still ask in the second situation. You just ask more modestly, and you lean toward non-salary items that cost the company less.

### Check the market, not just your feelings

Compare the offer against a few real reference points: the posted range (some US states and Canadian provinces require ranges in job ads, so check the original listing), government wage data such as the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook, CareerOneStop's wage tools, Canada's Job Bank, or comparable official data in the UK and Australia, plus what peers in your network tell you. Treat any single source as a rough guide. Remote roles add a wrinkle: some employers set pay by your location, others pay one rate regardless of where you live. Ask which model applies before you argue about a number.

## Step 3: Decide whether to push, trade or walk away

| Situation | Best move |
|---|---|
| Offer is close to your number and the job is strong | Push once, politely, for a modest improvement |
| Base pay is fixed but the rest is flexible | Trade: ask for other value |
| Offer is well below the posted range or market, with no explanation | Push with evidence, ask how it was set |
| Offer is below your walk-away number and they won't move | Walk away, graciously |
| Terms are vague or keep changing | Pause until it is all in writing |

### When to push

Push when you have evidence: a posted range, a competing offer, or specific experience that exceeds the job description. Be specific and warm.

> "I'm very keen to join, and I'd like to make this work. Based on the range in the posting and my experience leading [specific scope], I was expecting a base closer to [number]. Is there flexibility to get there?"

Name one number, not a vague "can you do better?" And then stop talking. Silence after a request is uncomfortable but useful.

### When to trade

If the recruiter says base pay is fixed by a band, don't treat that as the end. Ask what *is* flexible. Many employers can move other levers more easily than salary.

### When to walk away

Walk away if the package is below what you can live on, if the employer reacts to a polite question with pressure or hostility, or if the offer includes terms that worry you and they won't clarify. Decline kindly and keep the door open: "Thank you for the offer. After careful thought, the compensation isn't a fit for me right now. I'd welcome staying in touch." Employers sometimes return with a better number, but don't decline hoping for that.

## Step 4: What to ask for when base pay is fixed

Rank your requests by what you value, then pick two or three. A long shopping list can look like you're not really committed.

### Money that isn't base

- **Sign-on bonus.** Often easier to approve than a higher salary because it is one-time. Ask whether it has a clawback if you leave early.
- **Bonus target or guaranteed first-year bonus.** Ask how the bonus has paid out in recent years, not just the target.
- **Equity.** If it is offered, ask about the grant size, vesting schedule, any cliff, and what happens if you're let go or the company is acquired. Equity in a private company may be worth nothing, so weigh it accordingly.
- **Earlier pay review.** A written commitment to review compensation at six months rather than twelve can be valuable, especially if you're starting below your target. Make sure the criteria are clear.

### Level and scope

A title or level affects every future raise and your next job's offer. If you were hired a level below the scope you described in interviews, ask to be considered for the higher level, or ask for a defined path to promotion with dates.

### Remote-specific value

- **Equipment and home office budget.** A stipend, laptop, monitor and chair, plus a refresh schedule.
- **Internet and phone reimbursement.**
- **Coworking membership,** if you can't work well from home.
- **Travel and in-person gatherings.** Who pays, and how often are you expected to attend?
- **Time zone and hours.** Core hours, meeting expectations and on-call duties can matter more than a few thousand in salary.

### Time and flexibility

- Extra paid leave days or a longer notice-free holiday.
- A start date that lets you take a break between jobs.
- A four-day week or compressed schedule, if it fits the role.
- Professional development funds, conference travel or paid certification.

### Benefits that vary by country

Benefits differ sharply by country, so don't assume. In the US, health insurance premiums, deductibles and 401(k) matching can swing real value. In Canada, ask about extended health and dental, and any RRSP or pension match. In the UK, ask about employer pension contributions and sick pay beyond the statutory minimum. In Australia, confirm that superannuation is paid on top of your salary or included in it, as "package" and "base plus super" are very different numbers.

## Step 5: What to get in writing before you sign

Verbal promises disappear when managers change. Before accepting, make sure the written offer or contract covers the following. If something was promised on a call, ask for it to be added: "Could you confirm in the offer letter that…?"

### The core terms

- **Job title, level and reporting line.**
- **Base pay, currency and pay frequency.**
- **Bonus, commission or equity:** amounts, conditions, payout timing and what happens if you leave.
- **Start date** and any conditions of the offer, such as background or reference checks.
- **Review timing,** if promised.

### The remote-specific terms

- **Employment classification.** Are you an employee or a contractor? This affects tax, benefits, leave and protections. If an employer uses a third-party payroll provider, find out who your legal employer is.
- **Work location rules.** Where you are allowed to work from, whether you can move, and whether travelling and working abroad is permitted. Pay may change if you relocate.
- **Equipment, expenses and stipends:** what's covered, and whether you return equipment when you leave.
- **Working hours and time zone expectations.**
- **Any in-person requirement,** such as onsite weeks, with who pays for travel.
- **Whether the remote arrangement can be changed,** and with how much notice.

### The exit and restriction terms

- **Probationary or trial period** and the terms during it.
- **Notice period** for both sides, and any severance or redundancy terms.
- **Non-compete, non-solicit and confidentiality clauses.** Rules on enforceability vary by country, state and province, so if the wording is broad, get advice from an employment lawyer before signing.
- **Outside work and intellectual property.** If you freelance or have a side project, check the clause on moonlighting and ownership of your work.
- **Clawbacks** on bonuses, relocation or training costs.

A note on legal protections: they differ by country. In the UK, employers must provide a written statement of employment particulars, and GOV.UK explains what it must include. In Australia, the Fair Work Ombudsman sets out the National Employment Standards, which apply regardless of what a contract says. In the US, most employment is at-will unless a contract says otherwise, which makes written terms about severance and notice more important. Canadian rules vary by province, so check your provincial employment standards office. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Step 6: Making the ask without damaging the relationship

Put your request in an email as well as a call, so there's a record. Keep it short:

1. Thank them and say you're enthusiastic.
2. State the one or two things you'd like changed, with a reason.
3. Say what would make you ready to accept.
4. Ask for the updated offer in writing.

Example: "I'm excited about the role. Since the base is set by the band, could we look at a sign-on bonus and a home-office budget? If we can agree on those, I'm ready to accept."

Tying your request to a clear yes ("If we can do X, I'm ready to sign") shows good faith and helps the recruiter get approvals. Don't bluff about other offers, and don't make demands you wouldn't actually walk away over.

## A quick decision checklist

- Do I know my walk-away number?
- Have I compared the offer to the posted range and reliable wage data?
- Do I know whether pay is location-based?
- Have I picked my top two or three asks?
- Do I have every promised term in writing?
- Am I comfortable with the restrictions and exit terms?

If you can answer yes to all six and the package still works, accept with confidence. If not, you now know exactly what to ask, or what to walk away from.
