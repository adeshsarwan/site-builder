A panel interview on video changes the dynamics of the conversation. In a room, you can read body language, see who leans in, and turn your shoulders toward whoever is speaking. On a screen, you get a grid of small faces, uneven audio and a camera that makes "looking at someone" a technical puzzle.

The good news is that panels are predictable once you understand why they exist. Each interviewer usually represents a different stake in the hire: the manager, a peer, someone from a partner team, maybe HR or a recruiter. Your job is to answer the question in front of you while building a relationship with the whole group.

This guide covers the three problems candidates raise most: eye contact, pacing, and keeping track of who asked what.

## Before the Call: Get the Panel on Paper

Most of the work happens in the 48 hours before you log on.

### Ask for names and roles

If the invitation lists only a time and a link, ask the recruiter or coordinator a simple question: *"Could you share the names and titles of the people I'll be speaking with?"* This is a normal request, and most employers are happy to answer.

Then look up each person on the professional profile they've made public. You're looking for:

- How long they've been at the company and what they did before
- Which team they sit on relative to the role you're applying for
- Anything they've written or presented that connects to the work

Don't mention personal details you found. Use the research to predict what each person will care about.

### Predict each person's likely angle

A rough guide to what different panelists tend to probe:

- **Hiring manager:** Can you deliver results, and will you be easy to manage?
- **Peer or teammate:** Will you be a good collaborator day to day?
- **Cross-functional partner:** Can you communicate with people outside your discipline?
- **HR or recruiter:** Does your story hold together, and does this fit the role's terms and values?

This isn't a rule, but it helps you choose examples. If you have one story about shipping under pressure and another about resolving a disagreement with a colleague, you'll know which to reach for depending on who's asking.

### Prepare a short story bank

Write down five or six stories from your work, each with a situation, what you did, and the result. Label them by theme: leadership, conflict, failure, a tight deadline, working with ambiguity, learning something new. In many hiring processes, particularly structured ones common in the UK and Australia, interviewers score every candidate against the same criteria, so having stories ready that map to common themes is more useful than memorizing scripts.

## Set Up Your Screen for Eye Contact

Eye contact on video is a trade-off. Looking at the camera lens feels like eye contact to the interviewer. Looking at their faces on screen feels natural to you, but to them it looks like you're gazing slightly downward.

With one interviewer, you can mostly look at the lens. With a panel, you have to balance lens time and screen time.

### Position the panel near your camera

Before the call, drag the video window so the faces sit as close to your webcam as possible. If you use a laptop, shrink the call window and park it at the top of the screen. If you use an external monitor with a separate camera, put the camera on the same screen you'll be looking at, centered above it.

The smaller the angle between the lens and the faces, the less obvious it is when you glance between them.

### Use a simple gaze pattern

Try this rhythm when you answer:

1. **Start by looking at the person who asked.** Look at their tile while they finish the question, so they feel heard.
2. **Give your first sentence to the camera.** This makes the opening feel direct.
3. **Glance across the other tiles as you build the answer.** A short look at each person is enough.
4. **Close on the camera or on the person who asked.** End with a clear sentence, then stop talking.

You don't need to hit every person with every answer. Aim for the whole panel to feel included over the course of the interview.

### Tricks that help

- Put a small sticky note with a smiley face or an arrow next to your webcam as a reminder.
- Turn off self-view if you find yourself checking your own image. Many platforms allow you to hide it.
- Minimize other windows so nothing else pulls your eyes away.
- If someone is silent or looks unimpressed, resist the urge to focus on them. People on video are often just concentrating, reading their notes, or typing.

## Pace Your Answers So Nobody Gets Lost

Video adds a small delay, and panelists may be on different connections. Answers that feel fine in person can feel long and shapeless on screen.

### Pause before you answer

Take a short breath after the question ends. It prevents you from talking over someone because of lag, and it signals that you're thinking. You can also say, *"Let me gather my thoughts for a second,"* if the question is complex.

### Keep answers compact

A useful target for most behavioral questions is roughly one to two minutes. Longer answers risk losing people, particularly the ones who didn't ask the question. A simple structure keeps you on track:

- **Context:** one or two sentences on the situation
- **Action:** what you personally did, and why
- **Result:** what changed, with a number or a concrete outcome if you have one
- **Takeaway:** one line on what you learned or would repeat

Then stop. If they want more, they'll ask. Panels often have a long list of questions and a fixed amount of time, and an answer that runs on can cost you the chance to cover something important.

### Check in at the right moments

After a long or technical answer, a light check-in works well: *"Would it help if I went deeper on any part of that?"* Use it sparingly, once or twice in the whole interview, so it doesn't sound like you're unsure of yourself.

### Handle interruptions and overlaps

Panels sometimes talk over one another, especially with lag. If two people start at once, pause, smile, and say, *"Please, go ahead,"* then answer whichever question is left. If a follow-up comes mid-answer, finish your sentence, answer the follow-up directly, and return to your original point only if it still matters.

## Track Who Asked What

It's easy to forget names when four people speak in the first ten minutes. You'll want them for answering follow-ups, referring back to earlier points, and writing thank-you notes.

### Build a one-page cheat sheet

Before the call, make a simple grid on paper or in a document beside your camera. Fill in names and titles in advance, then add notes as you go.

| Interviewer | Role | What they asked | What they seem to care about | Follow-up to mention |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name 1 | Hiring manager | Example: how you prioritize work | Reliability, ownership | Share the roadmap example |
| Name 2 | Peer | Example: how you handle feedback | Collaboration | Mention code review habits |
| Name 3 | Partner team lead | Example: working with other departments | Communication | Offer the cross-team story |
| Name 4 | Recruiter | Example: salary and start date expectations | Logistics, fit | Confirm timeline |

Keep the notes to a few words per cell. You're jotting, not transcribing. Looking down briefly at a page is fine, as long as you come back to the camera quickly.

### Use names naturally

When you respond, use the person's name once, sparingly: *"Thanks, Priya. That's a good question."* If you refer back to something earlier, credit it: *"Going back to what Marcus asked about deadlines..."* This shows you were listening and helps the whole group see you as engaged.

If you forget a name, check your cheat sheet. If the name isn't on it, ask politely. *"Sorry, could you remind me of your name?"* is better than guessing.

### Notice tone shifts

Sometimes a single panelist's questions reveal a concern. If the finance partner keeps asking about budgets or the engineer keeps asking about process, you can address it directly later: *"You've asked a few times about how I manage scope, so let me add one more example."* It's a strong way to show you're paying attention.

## Tech and Logistics Checklist

Panels have more moving parts, so remove anything you can control.

- **Test the platform.** Whether it's Zoom, Teams, Google Meet or another tool, run a trial call with a friend to check audio, camera and screen layout.
- **Join early.** Log in a few minutes ahead, but expect to wait in a lobby.
- **Check time zones.** For remote-first roles, panelists may be in different regions. Confirm the time in your local zone and use the calendar invite, not mental math.
- **Have a backup plan.** Keep the recruiter's email or phone number handy in case the link fails. Have a phone hotspot ready if your internet is unreliable.
- **Light and sound.** Face a window or place a lamp in front of you, and use headphones with a decent microphone.
- **Keep water nearby** and silence notifications on every device.

## Close Strongly With the Whole Group

Panels usually end with "Do you have any questions for us?" This is a chance to speak to each person's perspective.

Try directing questions to specific people:

- To the manager: *"What would success look like in the first six months?"*
- To a peer: *"What's the best part of working on this team, and what's the hardest?"*
- To a partner team member: *"How do your team and this one usually work together?"*

Then give a brief closing statement that ties your strengths to what you heard. Thank everyone by name if time permits, and say you're glad to follow up with anything else they need.

### Send tailored follow-ups

Within a day, send a thank-you email. You can send one message to the group or short individual notes, depending on the contact details you have. Mention one specific moment from each person's questions. Your cheat sheet makes this easy, and it reinforces that you listened.

## A Quick Practice Routine

If you have a few days, rehearse like this:

1. Ask two or three friends to join a video call and play interviewers.
2. Have each ask questions in a different order, with occasional interruptions.
3. Practice your gaze pattern and your pause before answering.
4. Record the session and watch for rambling, looking away or going silent.
5. Repeat once, adjusting based on what you saw.

Even one rehearsal makes the real call feel far less chaotic.

## The Bottom Line

A video panel is less intimidating when you treat it as a series of short conversations rather than a performance. Learn who is in the room, set up your screen so eye contact is simple, answer in tight, structured chunks, and keep a quiet record of who asked what. If you do those four things, you'll come across as organized and attentive, which is what a team wants in a future colleague.
