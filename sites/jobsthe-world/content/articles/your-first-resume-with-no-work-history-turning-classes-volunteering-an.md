A recruiter opening your resume is not looking for a job title. They are looking for proof that you can do some part of the work, and that you can do it without someone standing over you. That second point matters even more for remote roles.

The good news: school, volunteering and personal projects can supply that proof. The catch is that most first resumes bury it under vague phrases like "responsible for" and "participated in." This guide shows you how to dig the evidence out and format it so it survives a skim.

*A note on vocabulary: "resume" is standard in the US, Canada and Australia, while "CV" is the usual term in the UK. For an early-career application, the document is essentially the same: one or two pages of evidence.*

## What a skimming recruiter actually sees

In the first few seconds, people tend to scan the top third of the page, the left edge of each line, and anything bolded or set apart. That means your structure does a lot of the work before your content is even read.

Design for that:

- **Put the strongest section first.** If you have no jobs, don't lead with an empty "Work Experience" heading. Lead with "Projects," "Relevant Experience" or "Education," whichever best matches the job.
- **Start every bullet with a verb.** The left edge is what gets scanned, so make it informative.
- **Keep bullets to one or two lines.** A third line rarely gets read.
- **Show a result or a visible output.** A link, a number you actually tracked, a finished thing.

## Step 1: Mine your experience for raw material

Before writing, collect. Open a blank document and list everything you've done in the last few years that involved a task, a deadline or other people. Include:

- Classes with a final project, lab, presentation or group assignment
- Volunteering, including one-off events
- Clubs, societies, student government, sports teams
- Personal projects: a website, a spreadsheet you built, a podcast, a game mod, a community group chat you moderate
- Informal work: helping a family business, tutoring friends, running a social account for a local group
- Self-directed learning with a finished output, such as an online course where you built something

For each item, jot down three things: what you did, what tools you used, and what came out the other end. Don't polish yet.

## Step 2: Use a simple bullet formula

A reliable structure is **action verb + what you did + how/with what + outcome**. You won't always have all four parts, and that's fine. Aim for at least the first three.

Compare these:

- Weak: *Responsible for managing social media for a school club.*
- Stronger: *Planned and scheduled three weekly posts for the debate club's Instagram using Canva and a shared content calendar; tracked which formats got the most replies and shifted toward them.*

The second version tells a recruiter what you used, how you organised yourself and that you paid attention to results. No invented numbers are needed.

### On using numbers honestly

Numbers help, but only real ones. Count things you actually know: attendees at an event you helped run, the number of pages in a report, how many people were in your team, how many weeks the project lasted. If you don't have a figure, describe a concrete output instead ("a 12-slide deck," "a working prototype with a public link"). Never round up or guess. Interviewers often ask you to walk through your bullets, and an inflated number is the fastest way to lose their trust.

## Step 3: Translate each source into bullets

Different kinds of experience need slightly different framing. Here is a quick reference.

| Source | What recruiters want to see | Example bullet |
|---|---|---|
| Class project | Real tools, teamwork, a deliverable | *Built a customer survey with Google Forms for a marketing course, analysed 40 responses in Excel and presented three recommendations to a panel of classmates and the instructor.* |
| Volunteering | Reliability, responsibility, people skills | *Checked in guests and answered questions at a weekend community food drive; trained two new volunteers on the sign-in process.* |
| Club or team role | Initiative, coordination | *Organised monthly meeting agendas and minutes for a 15-member robotics club, shared through a team Notion page.* |
| Side project | Self-direction, technical or creative skill | *Designed and launched a portfolio site using HTML and CSS; added a contact form and a project gallery.* |
| Informal work | Customer service, accountability | *Managed bookings and payments by message for a neighbourhood dog-walking service across four regular clients.* |

Use only examples that match your real history. The numbers in the table are illustrations of format, not targets.

## Step 4: Show you can work remotely

Remote-first employers worry about a few things that an office employer can handle by walking over to your desk. Your bullets can quietly answer them.

- **Written communication.** Mention documentation, summaries, reports, written updates or a blog. "Wrote weekly progress updates for a four-person group project" says a lot.
- **Self-management.** Projects you finished without a teacher chasing you are valuable. Say how you tracked work: a task board, a calendar, a checklist.
- **Async and cross-time-zone teamwork.** If you collaborated with people online, name the tools you used, such as Slack, Discord, Google Workspace, Trello, GitHub or Zoom, and what you used them for.
- **Reliability.** Volunteering for a recurring shift, or keeping a project alive for months, is evidence you show up.

Don't list tools as decoration. A tool belongs on your resume only if you could answer a basic question about it in an interview.

## Step 5: Choose a layout that fits your situation

For most first resumes, a single page works well. A workable order:

1. **Header:** name, city or region and country (or just time zone), email, and a link to a portfolio, GitHub or LinkedIn if it's tidy. Skip a street address.
2. **Short summary (optional):** two lines, specific to the role. "Recent business graduate seeking a remote customer support role; strong written communication and experience handling enquiries for a volunteer organisation."
3. **Projects or Relevant Experience:** three to four bullets each, for your two or three best items.
4. **Education:** school, program, graduation date. Add a short line of relevant coursework only if it echoes the job posting.
5. **Skills:** grouped and honest, e.g. "Tools: Excel, Canva, Notion. Languages: English, Spanish (conversational)."

### Regional details to watch

- **US and Canada:** don't include a photo, date of birth or marital status. Keep it to one page.
- **UK:** a CV for early-career roles is typically one to two pages; a photo is generally not expected. A brief personal profile at the top is common.
- **Australia:** "resume" is widely used; include referee details only if the employer asks, and consider noting your work rights if relevant.
- **Remote applications across borders:** if you're applying to employers in another country, check whether they hire where you live, and state your location and time zone clearly so there's no guesswork.

Always follow the instructions in the specific job posting if they differ from these general conventions.

## Step 6: Tailor in ten minutes

You don't need a new resume for every application. You need a quick pass.

1. Read the posting and highlight the three or four repeated requirements.
2. Reorder your bullets so the closest matches sit at the top of each section.
3. Borrow the posting's wording where it's honestly accurate. If it says "stakeholder updates" and you wrote "progress emails to the project supervisor," you can adjust the phrasing.
4. Cut the least relevant bullet to keep the page clean.

Many companies use applicant tracking systems to store and search resumes, so use plain headings, a standard font, and a simple one-column layout. Avoid text boxes, icons for contact details and graphics-heavy templates, which can scramble when parsed.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Listing duties instead of actions.** "Attended weekly meetings" is not evidence. "Took minutes and sent action items after weekly meetings" is.
- **Padding with unrelated items.** A short list of strong items beats a long list of weak ones.
- **Using "team player," "hard worker" or "passionate."** Show the behaviour in a bullet instead.
- **Dead links.** If you link to a project, open it in a private browser window to confirm it loads.
- **Typos.** For remote roles, writing is part of the job. Read the resume aloud, then ask a friend to read it.

## A quick before-and-after

**Before**

*Volunteer, local library. Helped with events and assisted patrons.*

**After**

*Library Volunteer, Community Branch (Jan–Jun)*
- *Ran the check-in desk for a monthly children's reading event, welcoming families and keeping a running attendance sheet.*
- *Helped patrons find books and use public computers; escalated technical problems to staff using a shared log.*
- *Created a one-page guide to the self-checkout machine that staff began handing out.*

Same experience, but now each line shows a task, a skill and a result. That's the whole technique.

## Your checklist before you hit send

- Does each bullet start with a verb and show something you did?
- Are the numbers real, and could you explain each one?
- Is the most relevant section at the top?
- Is there at least one signal of remote readiness: written work, tool use or self-directed effort?
- Do all links work, and is the file named clearly, such as "Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf"?

You are not hiding a lack of experience. You are organising the experience you already have so a busy reader can see it fast. Start with your single best project, write three strong bullets, and build outward from there.
