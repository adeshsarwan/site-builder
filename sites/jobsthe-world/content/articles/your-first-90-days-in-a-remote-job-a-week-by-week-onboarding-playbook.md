In an office, a new hire absorbs a lot by accident. You see who eats lunch together, which meetings run long, and how the manager reacts when something slips. Remote work removes those cues. Nothing is learned by accident, so you have to learn on purpose.

This playbook gives you a schedule for that. It splits the first 90 days into stages, and each stage has three jobs: **learn** specific things, **meet** specific people, and **deliver** something visible. Adjust it to your role, but don't skip the structure.

## Three principles that make remote onboarding work

**1. Make your progress visible.** In a remote job, work that nobody can see may as well not exist. Short written updates replace the hallway impression you would have made in person.

**2. Ask early, in writing, in public when appropriate.** Questions asked in a shared channel build a searchable record and show others how the team works. Save direct messages for anything sensitive.

**3. Agree on expectations explicitly.** An office lets you guess what "good" looks like. Remotely, you need your manager to say it. Get it in words, then confirm it in a follow-up message.

## Before day one

Most of the early friction is avoidable with a little preparation.

- **Confirm your equipment and access.** Ask HR or IT when your laptop, accounts and software logins will arrive. If anything is late, flag it before your start date, not on it.
- **Check your workspace.** Test your internet connection, camera, microphone and headset. Have a backup option, such as a phone hotspot.
- **Ask for a first-week schedule.** A good employer will send one. If not, ask your manager for the meetings and tasks planned for week one.
- **Clarify your hours and time zone.** Find out your core overlap hours with teammates, and when you are expected to be reachable.
- **Handle the paperwork.** Complete tax, payroll and benefits forms promptly. The details vary by country, but late paperwork can delay your first pay.

## Week 1: Orient and connect

Your only goal this week is to understand how things work and be easy to reach.

### Learn

- The team's tools: chat, project tracker, shared drive, video platform
- Communication norms: which channel for what, how fast people reply, whether cameras are expected
- The company's products, customers and how the team fits into the larger organization
- Your role's scope in writing: responsibilities, success measures and who you report to

### Meet

- Your manager (daily or near-daily check-ins this week are reasonable to request)
- Your onboarding buddy, if you have one. If you don't, ask for one.
- Every direct teammate in a short one-to-one video chat

### Deliver

- A short introduction post in the team channel, with a line about your background and one personal detail that gives people something to respond to
- A working setup: tools accessed, accounts logged in, calendar organized
- A written note to your manager at the end of the week: what you learned, what is unclear, what you plan to do next week

## Week 2: Learn the work

Now shift from setup to substance.

- **Read the history.** Review recent project documents, meeting notes and past decisions. This replaces the context you would normally pick up by overhearing.
- **Shadow.** Ask to sit in on calls, reviews or customer conversations. Camera on, microphone off, notes ready.
- **Keep a running glossary.** Every team has acronyms and shorthand. Write them down and ask about the ones you can't decode.
- **Take on a small first task.** Ask your manager for something low-risk that you can finish within days.

By the end of week two, you should be able to explain in your own words what the team is trying to achieve this quarter and where you fit.

## Weeks 3–4: Deliver the first small win

The goal is a finished piece of work, however modest. Completing something early builds trust faster than months of preparation.

### Do this

- Complete your first real task and share it with a short note: what you did, how, and what you'd improve
- Ask for feedback directly: "What's one thing I could do differently next time?"
- Write down the unwritten rules you've noticed, such as who approves what and when people typically go quiet
- Schedule your first 30-day conversation with your manager

### The 30-day conversation

Bring a one-page summary with three parts: what you've learned, what you've finished, and what you need. Then ask your manager three questions:

1. What should I focus on for the next 30 days?
2. Where am I meeting expectations, and where do you want more?
3. How do you prefer to receive updates from me?

## Days 31–60: Build momentum and relationships

The second month is where remote workers either become part of the team or drift to its edges. Work on both your output and your network.

### Learn

- How your work connects to other teams, including who depends on your output and who you depend on
- The metrics your team is judged on, and how they are tracked
- Where the recurring problems and bottlenecks are

### Meet

- One person from each team you work with regularly, in a 20–30 minute introductory chat
- A colleague a level or two above you, to understand how people grow in the organization
- Someone outside your department, just to learn how the wider company works

Ask each person the same few questions: What does your team do? What makes a good working relationship with you? What do you wish new people understood sooner?

### Deliver

- Own a project or workstream, even a small one, with clear start and finish points
- Send a brief weekly update. Three bullets are enough: done, doing, blocked.
- Document something. A how-to, a checklist or a cleaned-up process note helps the team and shows initiative.

## Days 61–90: Own your lane and look ahead

By now you should be working with less hand-holding. This stage is about proving you're reliable and starting to improve things.

- **Run something end to end.** Take a project from planning to completion and present the result.
- **Suggest one improvement.** Base it on what you've observed as a fresh pair of eyes. Frame it as an offer, with a clear plan, not a criticism.
- **Revisit your role description.** Compare what you were hired to do with what you're doing. Raise any gaps.
- **Set goals for the next quarter.** Draft them yourself and bring them to your manager for refinement.

### The 90-day review

Treat this as a conversation, not a verdict. Prepare a short summary of your accomplishments with links to the work itself. Ask what success looks like over the next six months, and what skills or support would help you get there. If your employer has a formal probation or review period, ask when it ends and what the criteria are.

## The plan at a glance

| Stage | Learn | Meet | Deliver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before day one | Schedule, hours, tools | HR or IT contact | Equipment and workspace ready |
| Week 1 | Tools, norms, role scope | Manager, buddy, teammates | Intro post, end-of-week note |
| Week 2 | Project history, team goals | Anyone you're shadowing | A small first task started |
| Weeks 3–4 | Unwritten rules, feedback style | Manager (30-day talk) | First finished piece of work |
| Days 31–60 | Cross-team dependencies, metrics | Partner teams, senior colleague | Owned workstream, weekly updates |
| Days 61–90 | Quarter priorities | Wider network | End-to-end project, improvement idea, 90-day review |

## Remote habits that matter at every stage

### Over-communicate, briefly

If a task will take longer than expected, say so early. If you're offline for an appointment, put it in your calendar. Short, clear messages beat silence.

### Protect your boundaries

New remote workers often overcompensate by being online all day. Agree on your hours, set your status, and stick to them. Sustainable habits matter more than a strong first week.

### Plan for social connection

Schedule virtual coffee chats, join optional team channels, and attend any in-person meetups. Relationships built in the first three months make later collaboration easier.

### Keep a work log

Write down what you finish each week, with links. This makes your 30-, 60- and 90-day conversations easy and gives you material for future reviews.

## Regional notes: US, Canada, Australia and the UK

The 90-day framework works everywhere, but employment terms differ.

- **United States:** Many employers use an informal introductory period, but employment is usually at-will. Ask what review points exist, and check your benefits enrollment deadlines.
- **Canada:** Probationary periods are common but governed by provincial or federal rules. Check your offer letter and your province's employment standards.
- **Australia:** Employers often use a probation period, and the Fair Work system sets rules around minimum employment periods and notice. Read your contract and check the Fair Work Ombudsman's resources if anything is unclear.
- **United Kingdom:** Probation periods are common and should be written in your contract. Ask about review dates and notice terms.

In every country, your contract and the official labour authority's guidance take priority over general advice. If your team spans time zones, ask early about overlap hours, regional public holidays and how urgent requests are handled.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Waiting to be told what to do.** Propose next steps instead.
- **Staying silent when stuck.** Ask after you've made a genuine attempt, and show what you've tried.
- **Skipping one-to-ones.** Teammates you never speak to become strangers quickly.
- **Hiding your work.** Share progress even when it isn't polished.
- **Assuming expectations.** Confirm them in writing.

## Final thought

A remote job doesn't give you cues, so build your own. Learn on a schedule, meet people deliberately, and deliver something visible at every stage. By day 90, your manager and teammates should know what you do, trust you to do it, and see where you're headed next.
