Most of what gets you a reputation as a strong new hire in your first year is not talent. It is a short set of communication habits — reading a brief back before you start, batching your questions, flagging bad news early — repeated on a predictable schedule until people stop having to wonder what you are doing.

The stakes are real early on. Nearly one-third of new hires quit within their first six months, according to research cited by SHRM (Society for Human Resource Management, 10 August 2017), and the same body has been cited as finding that up to 20% of turnover happens in the first 45 days (HiringThing blog, citing SHRM, 2024). Onboarding researcher Talya Bauer notes that employees get roughly 90 days to prove themselves (SHRM, 10 August 2017). The habits below are the ones you can start on day one and still be running at month twelve.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - A Friday five-line update sent before 3pm means your manager never has to ask what you worked on — and you have a written record when review season arrives.
> - Reading the brief back in your own words before you start ("so you want a one-page draft by Thursday, Priya reviews it") catches the misunderstanding while it still costs ten seconds instead of three days.
> - Asking for feedback on one named thing — not "any feedback for me?" — gets you a usable answer, because the other person does not have to invent a topic on the spot.

## The Habits

1. **The Friday five-line update.** A short written summary stops your manager from having to reconstruct what you did, and it gives you a paper trail for your first review. Every Friday before 3pm, send five bullets by email or direct message: finished this week, in progress, blocked on, learned, planned for next week.

1. **The batched question list.** Interrupting a busy colleague eleven times a day costs them their concentration; one list costs them twenty minutes. From your first week, keep a running note titled "Ask [manager's name]" in whatever your team already uses — a plain doc or notes app is fine — and bring it to your 1:1 instead of sending each item as it occurs to you.

1. **The read-back.** Repeating an assignment in your own words surfaces the mismatch before you spend three days building the wrong thing. Do it at the end of every briefing conversation for your first 90 days: "So what I'm hearing is you want a one-page draft by Thursday, Priya reviews it before it goes to the client — have I got that right?"

1. **The 30-minute stuck rule.** This protects you from both failure modes — the new hire who asks before trying, and the one who disappears for two days. Set a timer when you hit a wall; at 30 minutes, ask, and open with what you already tried: "I've been trying to get the export to run for half an hour. I checked the permissions and the date range. Where would you look next?"

1. **Naming your own deadline.** Volunteering a date makes you easy to plan around and removes the need for anyone to chase you. When you take a task, say the date out loud: "I'll have a draft to you Wednesday at noon. If it's going to slip, you'll hear from me Tuesday morning."

1. **Asking for feedback on one named thing.** Feedback-seeking behaviour — the deliberate effort to find out whether what you are doing is adequate — is a well-studied habit in organisational research (Ashford, 1986, as cited in academic work on the topic), and positive supervisor feedback has been found to improve employee performance in a three-wave study of 368 employees and their direct supervisors (research study hosted on ResearchGate, undated). Pick one artefact per week for your first 90 days: "On Monday's deck — was the level of detail right for that audience, or too much?"

1. **Early bad news.** Managers can usually fix a late deliverable; they cannot fix one they learn about on the due date. The moment you know a date is at risk, send one message with three parts: what slipped, the new date, what you need. "The data pull is two days behind because the source file changed. New date is Friday the 14th. If you want it Wednesday, I'd need to drop the regional breakdown."

1. **The connection map.** Bauer describes four levels of onboarding effectiveness — compliance, clarification, culture and connection (SHRM, 10 August 2017) — and connection is the one nobody schedules for you. In your first 30 days, book three 15-minute intro calls a week and ask one question in each: "What does a good week look like for you, and where does your work touch mine?"

1. **The written close-out.** Verbal agreements in meetings evaporate; a three-line recap makes ownership visible without anyone having to be the enforcer. Within an hour of any meeting where work was assigned, post a short recap in the shared channel or thread the team already uses, with a name and a date on each item.

Two of these are worth hearing in full, because the wording does most of the work.

> Raising a slipping deadline on day 40
> 
> "Quick heads-up before you hear it elsewhere: the vendor hasn't sent the figures, so the Thursday report will be Monday instead. I've emailed them and copied you. If Thursday is firm, I can send it with last month's numbers flagged as provisional — your call."

> Asking for feedback in a 1:1 in month two
> 
> "One thing I'd like your read on. In the client call yesterday I jumped in to answer the pricing question — was that the right call here, or should I have handed it to Dan? I genuinely can't tell yet how this team handles that."

## Habits That Backfire vs. Habits That Work

The habits that damage a new hire's reputation rarely look lazy. They usually look like effort pointed in the wrong direction. Each row below is the same instinct, played two ways.

| Situation | What backfires | What works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You have questions in week two | You send your manager nine separate messages between 9am and 4pm. Each one is reasonable; together they teach her that answering you is a full-time job, and she starts replying slowly. | You keep the "Ask [manager]" note, send only the one item that is blocking you right now, and bring the other eight to Thursday's 1:1. She answers all eight in fifteen minutes and feels helpful rather than interrupted. |
| You want to show you are working | You message "just checking in — making good progress!" three times a week. There is nothing in it she can act on, so it reads as needing reassurance. | You send the Friday five-line update at 2:45pm with one line under "blocked on." She reads it in forty seconds and unblocks you on Monday. |
| You are briefed on a task | You nod, say "got it," and go away. Three days later you hand in a twelve-page analysis when what was wanted was a one-page summary for a board pack. | You read the brief back — "one page, Thursday, Priya reviews it" — and she says "actually make it two pages and skip the methodology." Ten seconds, three days saved. |
| You want to improve | You end every 1:1 with "any feedback for me?" Your manager says "no, you're doing great," because she has not prepared and cannot think of anything on the spot. | You ask about one named thing: "Was Monday's deck too detailed for that audience?" She has an actual opinion about that deck and gives it to you. |
| A deadline is slipping in month three | You stay quiet and work weekends to rescue it. You miss it anyway by a day, and your manager finds out from the person who was waiting on it. | You send the three-part message on the Tuesday — what slipped, new date, what you need — and she reshuffles the review slot. The work is late; your reliability is not in question. |
| You are building relationships | You send a blanket "would love to connect!" message to twenty people in your first week. Most ignore it, because there is no reason attached. | You book three 15-minute calls a week with people whose work touches yours and ask what a good week looks like for them. By day 30 you know who to ask about what. |
| Something gets decided in a meeting | You rely on everyone remembering. Two weeks later, two people each thought the other owned the follow-up, and nobody wants to be the one who raises it. | You post the three-line recap within the hour, with a name and a date beside each item. Nobody has to chase anyone, and corrections arrive the same day while the meeting is fresh. |

How much of this is formalised for you depends heavily on the employer. Organisations with a standard onboarding process have been reported to see 50% greater new-hire productivity, and 69% of employees are said to be more likely to stay three years after a strong onboarding experience (Click Boarding statistics cited by SHRM, 10 August 2017). If your employer has none of that — common in small firms and startups — these habits are the structure you build yourself. Norms also vary by workplace and country: how direct you can be about a slipping deadline, and whether written recaps read as diligent or distrustful, differ between teams. Watch what your most-respected colleague does for two weeks and copy the format, not the personality.

## Frequently asked questions

**My manager keeps cancelling our 1:1s. What then?+**

Fall back on the two habits that do not need a meeting. Keep sending the Friday five-line update before 3pm — it is the one thing that keeps your work visible when calendar time disappears — and convert the batched question list into a single message sent at a fixed time each week, with the questions numbered so they can be answered in order. If three 1:1s in a row are cancelled, raise it once with a specific ask rather than a complaint: "Can we move our 1:1 to a slot that protects it better, or would you rather I send questions in writing on Thursdays?"

**How long do I need to keep this up before I can relax?+**

Treat the full set as a 90-day protocol, since conventional wisdom and research both suggest employees get about 90 days to prove themselves (Talya Bauer, cited by SHRM, 10 August 2017). After that, the read-back can become occasional — you will know the house style by then — but keep the Friday update, the early bad news message and the written close-out running through month twelve. Worker productivity has been reported to peak after a year of employment (Gallup, cited in a HiringThing blog post, 2024), so your first year is still a ramp, not a plateau.

**I work fully remote. Does any of this change?+**

Three habits carry more weight. The written close-out after meetings matters more because there are no corridor corrections; the connection map needs deliberate scheduling because nobody will bump into you; and naming your own deadline substitutes for the visible signal of being seen at your desk. Keep the 30-minute stuck rule tighter too — say 20 minutes — because remote teams cannot see you struggling.
