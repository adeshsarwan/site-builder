A below-expectation offer is usually a starting position, not a verdict on you. The useful work happens before the call: knowing what the job pays in your metro, knowing what else you have in play, and knowing which parts of the package the recruiter can actually move without more approvals.

This playbook covers how to assess leverage, a tactics table with the specific risk attached to each move, two sample dialogues including the pushback you should expect, and a checklist of what to get in writing before you accept.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Sign-on bonus is often the most movable item when base is pinned to a published pay band — ask what the band ceiling is for your level before you name a number.
> - Start date is usually a scheduling question rather than a budget one in salaried office roles; an extra two to four weeks is a common ask, but check how it shifts benefits eligibility.
> - Non-cash levers — remote days, title, a written six-month salary review, a relocation or equipment allowance — cost less approval than base and are where capped offers usually give.

## Know Your Leverage

Leverage is not confidence. It is a short list of facts you can name on a call.

Start with market data for your occupation in your location. The federal Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) program is described by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics as the only comprehensive source of regularly produced occupational employment and wage rate information for the U.S. economy, covering the 50 states, DC and several territories (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, undated), with geographic detail down to metro area, state and national level (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, undated). The easiest way in is CareerOneStop's Salary Finder, which covers more than 800 occupations and lets you compare wages by local area, state or nation (CareerOneStop, sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor, undated). Its help page notes the tool shows low, median and high wages, hourly and yearly, for your chosen location against state and national figures (CareerOneStop, undated), drawn from the BLS OEWS May 2025 estimates (CareerOneStop, 2025-05). O*NET OnLine shows the same low/median/high structure in its Wages & Employment Trends section (O*NET OnLine, undated).

Then add the things data cannot show you:

- **A competing offer in writing.** A signed or emailed offer with a deadline is leverage. A conversation with another recruiter is not.
- **Scarce skill.** If the posting lists a certification, clearance, language or system that took you years to acquire, the replacement pool is small.
- **Time-to-fill pressure.** A role reposted several times, a vacancy covered by someone on secondment, or a start date tied to a quarter or a contract all push the employer toward closing.
- **Where you are in the process.** Leverage peaks after the offer and before you accept. It drops sharply the moment you say yes.

## Negotiation Tactics and When to Use Them

| Tactic | Best Used When | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchor on local wage data for the occupation | You can cite a specific location-level figure, not a national average | Recruiter asks which source and level it reflects; a national number for a lower-cost metro gets rebutted and you lose the anchor for the rest of the call |
| Name one figure, not a range | The recruiter asks "what would it take" and you have done the research | You will usually be offered the bottom of any range you say out loud, so a range costs you the top of it |
| Disclose a competing written offer | You would genuinely consider taking it and can state its deadline | Recruiter may match the other deadline rather than the other number, compressing your decision to 48 hours; some ask to see the letter |
| Ask for a sign-on bonus instead of base | Recruiter says the base is at the ceiling of the pay band for your level | Sign-on bonuses commonly carry a clawback — leave inside the stated window and you repay, sometimes on the gross amount |
| Trade scope or title for pay | Base is band-capped but the hiring manager controls the job description | You can end up with extra responsibilities and no title change; worthless unless the review date and criteria are in the offer letter |
| Ask for a written salary review at six months | The role is new, the band is rigid, or the budget year is nearly closed | A "review" is not a raise; without named metrics and a date it is unenforceable and routinely forgotten after a manager change |
| Go quiet after stating your number | You have stated it clearly on a call and the recruiter needs approval | Can stall the offer by several days while it goes to finance or compensation; on a video call, long silence can read as a refusal |
| Ask for more time to decide | You have other processes at a final stage | Some offers expire on a stated date; others hold the start date fixed, so every day you take eats your notice period at your current job |

## Sample Negotiation Conversations

Names, companies and figures below are invented. Replace them with your own occupation's local wage data and your own situation.

### Salary: the base is below your target

> **Recruiter (Dana, Harborlight Analytics):** We've landed on $96,000 base for the mid-level data analyst role in the Denver office. How does that sound?

> **You:** Thank you for getting it approved. Based on local and state wage figures for this occupation and one written offer I'm holding, I was targeting $108,000.

> **Recruiter:** That's above the band for this level. Without a title change the ceiling is $102,000.

> **You:** Then let's do $102,000, plus a salary review at six months with the criteria written into the offer. If the title is what unlocks the higher band, I'd like to see what the promotion criteria are.

> **Recruiter:** I can take $102,000 today. The review I'd need to confirm with the hiring manager.

> **You:** Understood. Send both in writing and I'll come back to you by Thursday.

### Non-cash: remote days inside an onsite policy

> **Recruiter (Marcus, Trellis Logistics):** The offer assumes four days onsite at the Columbus site, standard for operations roles.

> **You:** The pay works. I'd like two remote days a week, and I'd rather agree it now than ask after I start.

> **Recruiter:** The policy is team-wide, and new hires normally stay onsite through the first 90 days.

> **You:** That's reasonable. Can we write it as four days onsite for the first 90 days, then two remote days from day 91, reviewed by my manager against the same delivery metrics as everyone else?

> **Recruiter:** I'd need the operations director to sign that off.

> **You:** Please ask. If it's a no, tell me and I'll decide on the offer as written — I'm not using it as a condition.

## Before You Say Yes

Get all of this in the offer letter or an email from someone at the company, not from a phone call. Verbal terms disappear when the recruiter leaves.

1. **Base pay, pay frequency and effective date.** "Annualized" figures sometimes fold in shift differentials, on-call pay or an assumed overtime load that you cannot count on.
2. **Bonus: target versus guaranteed.** Confirm whether the percentage is a target or a floor, what triggers it, whether year one is prorated from your start date, and whether you must still be employed on the payout date — that last clause is the one people miss.
3. **Equity terms in units, not dollars.** Ask for the number of shares or units, the vesting schedule and cliff, the valuation or strike price and its date, and what happens to unvested units if you resign or are laid off. A dollar value quoted at today's valuation is not what you will receive.
4. **Sign-on bonus clawback.** Confirm the repayment window, whether you repay the gross or net amount, and whether a layoff triggers it.
5. **Start date and benefits start date.** These are often different. Check the waiting period for medical coverage so you know whether there is a gap between jobs to cover.
6. **PTO: accrual rate, start of accrual, carryover and payout.** Rules differ by employer and by state, so confirm your state's position with the employer's written policy rather than assuming.
7. **Work location and remote terms, and who can change them.** If pay is adjusted by location, ask what happens if you move or if the office relocates.
8. **Contingencies and restrictive clauses.** Background check, references, and any non-compete or non-solicit. Enforceability varies by state and by role — if a clause worries you, have an employment lawyer licensed in your state read it before you sign.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I negotiate a verbal offer, or should I wait for the written one?+**

You can negotiate on the call, but ask for the headline terms in writing first so you are both arguing about the same numbers. Keep the conversation warm and non-committal: thank them, confirm you want the role, and ask for the written offer and a decision deadline. Treat nothing as settled — a verbal figure that never appears in the letter is not part of your package.

**Is it acceptable to counter a second time?+**

A second counter is reasonable only if it introduces something new: a competing written offer that arrived since, or a term you have not yet raised, such as a start date or remote days. Re-asking for the same base with the same argument stalls the process and uses up goodwill with the hiring manager. If the second ask fails, accept or decline cleanly rather than circling back a third time.

**Can an offer actually be rescinded because I negotiated?+**

It is uncommon, but offers do get withdrawn — usually because a headcount freeze hit, a background check turned up a discrepancy, or the candidate's demands signalled they would not accept anything on the table. Reduce the risk by keeping every ask attached to a clear yes: say what you will sign if they meet it. Avoid negotiating a role you have already decided to turn down.

**What if the recruiter asks for my current salary?+**

Some states and cities restrict employers from asking salary history, and the rules differ by location, so check your own state's position. Either way, you can redirect: give the figure you are targeting for this role and the local wage data behind it. If pressed, ask what the budgeted range is for the level. A recruiter who refuses to name any range is telling you something useful.
