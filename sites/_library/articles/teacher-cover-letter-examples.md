The samples below are templates for three common situations: a first job after student teaching, an experienced secondary teacher changing districts or states, and a career changer coming in through an alternative certification route. Each one has bracketed placeholders so it cannot be sent as it stands, and each is followed by a short explanation of why it is built that way.

Use the example closest to your situation, then work through the customization list. Most of the editing is concrete: your certification area, your grade levels, your own numbers, and the name of the school you are actually writing to.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Example 1 fits you if your only classroom experience is student teaching or substituting — it leads with certification status and one placement result.
> - Example 2 fits you if you already have your own classroom and are moving districts or states — it pairs resume bullets with a short letter that explains the move.
> - The biggest formatting difference: the new-teacher letter devotes a full paragraph to a single placement, while the experienced letter compresses years of work into scannable resume bullets and keeps the letter to four short paragraphs.

## Example Resumes & Cover Letters

Every sample here is a draft to adapt, not a document to submit. Anything in square brackets is a decision you have to make, and the numbers are written as [X%] on purpose — use your real figures, or describe the result in words if you do not have a number you can stand behind.

### Example 1: First teaching job after student teaching, elementary

> Template — replace every bracket before you use this
> 
> [Your name] | [City, State] | [Phone] | [Email]
> 
> [Date]
> 
> Dear [Principal's name],
> 
> I am applying for the [grade level] teaching position at [School name], posted on [where you saw it]. I hold a [degree] from [university] and [state] certification in [certification area], and I completed [X] weeks of student teaching in a [grade] classroom at [placement school].
> 
> In that placement I planned and taught daily [subject] lessons for [X] students, including [X] students with IEPs, and rebuilt the small-group rotation with my mentor teacher so that guided reading ran four days a week instead of two; [X%] of the group met their [assessment name] growth target by the end of the term. I also ran [second concrete thing — family phone calls, a morning tutoring block, a club] and kept [X] families updated in [language(s) you used].
> 
> I want to teach at [School name] specifically because of [one named thing: the dual-language strand, the PBIS framework, the project-based units in upper grades]. [One sentence in your own words about why that matches how you teach.]
> 
> I would welcome the chance to talk about the [grade level] opening and can bring [your portfolio, a sample unit plan, your edTPA materials] to an interview.
> 
> Sincerely, [Your name]

This structure works because, with no classroom of your own yet, the second paragraph has to do the proving — one placement, described concretely, with a result attached. Certification appears in the first paragraph because public school teachers must hold a state-issued certification or license (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025), and a principal reading quickly will look for that before anything else. The fourth paragraph stays one sentence so the whole letter fits on one screen.

### Example 2: Experienced high school teacher moving to another district or state

> Template — resume summary and bullets to adapt
> 
> **Summary:** [Subject] teacher certified in [state] for grades 7–12, [X] years teaching [course names]. [One line on your specialism: AP, IB, co-teaching, CTE pathway, newcomer ELs.]
> 
> - Taught [X] sections of [course] per semester to roughly [X] students, raising the [exam or benchmark] pass rate from [X%] to [X%] across [X] school years. - Rewrote the [course] curriculum map to align with [standards], reducing re-teach days by about [X%] over two years. - Mentored [X] first-year and student teachers, with [X] of [X] returning for a second year in the department. - Ran [intervention or credit-recovery program] for [X] seniors, moving [X%] to on-time graduation.

> Template — short letter to attach to those bullets
> 
> Dear [Principal's or Hiring Administrator's name],
> 
> I am applying for the [course or subject] position at [School name]. I have taught [subject] for [X] years at [current school], most recently [X] sections of [course], and I am relocating to [city or region] in [month, year]. My [current state] certification is [state the plain status of your paperwork with the new state — applied, in review, issued on (date)].
> 
> Two things I would bring to your department: [specific strength tied to their posting] and [specific strength tied to their school data or program]. At [current school] I [one sentence restating your strongest bullet in plain prose].
> 
> I know [School name] is [one accurate observation about the school — a program, a schedule model, a recent initiative]. [One sentence on how your experience fits it.]
> 
> I am available to interview [your availability] and can teach a demonstration lesson on [topic] if that is useful.
> 
> Sincerely, [Your name]

This version splits the work: the resume bullets carry the years and the numbers, so the letter can stay at four short paragraphs and spend its space on the two things a hiring administrator cannot get from a resume — why you are moving, and where your license stands. Secondary certification generally covers grades 7 through 12 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025), so naming the grade band in the summary line saves the reader guessing whether you can cover a middle school section.

### Example 3: Career changer entering through an alternative certification route

> Template — adapt every bracket
> 
> Dear [Principal's name],
> 
> I am applying for the [subject] position at [School name]. I spent [X] years as a [previous role] in [field], and I am completing [name of your route or program] toward [state] certification in [subject]; my expected certification date is [month, year] and my current status is [intern license, provisional, enrolled].
> 
> My subject background is [degree or professional experience], and I have already worked with students as a [substitute, tutor, coach, volunteer] at [site] for [X] months, where I [one concrete thing you did] with [X] students.
> 
> What I bring from [previous field] is [one transferable, teachable skill — not a personality trait]. [One sentence on how you would use it in a [subject] classroom.]
> 
> I would be glad to discuss how my certification timeline lines up with your [semester or school year] start.
> 
> Sincerely, [Your name]

This letter front-loads the certification timeline because it is the first question a hiring administrator will have, and every state offers some alternative route for people who already hold a bachelor's degree but lack the required education coursework (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025) — so the detail that matters is which route and when it finishes. The second paragraph exists to show you have been in front of students, however briefly, rather than asking the reader to assume it.

## How to Customize These Examples

Work through these in order before you send anything.

- **Replace every bracket, including the ones you skim past.** [Principal's name], [School name] and [where you saw it] in the first line of Examples 1 and 3 are the ones most often left behind when a letter is reused.
- **Swap the certification line for your actual status.** In Example 1 it reads "[state] certification in [certification area]"; in Example 2 it is the sentence about your paperwork with the new state. Write what is true today — applied, in review, issued — and never state a license or degree you do not hold.
- **Change the grade band and subject keywords to match the posting.** Example 2's summary says "grades 7–12"; if the posting is for middle school, say the grades you are certified for and the courses you have actually taught, using the district's own course names.
- **Put your own numbers into the bullets.** Every [X%] and [X] in Example 2's four bullets must become a figure you can explain in an interview, or be rewritten in words: "raised the pass rate over two years" is better than a number you invented.
- **Rename the resume skills or specialism line.** Example 2's summary ends with "[one line on your specialism]" — make that match the posting's language, whether that is co-teaching, AP, MTSS tiers, or newcomer English learners.
- **Fix the dates.** Your relocation month in Example 2 and your expected certification date in Example 3 both need checking every time you reuse the file; stale dates are the most common error in a recycled letter.
- **Replace the school-specific sentence.** Example 1's third paragraph and Example 2's third paragraph each name one real thing about the school. If you cannot name something accurate from the school or district site, cut the sentence rather than fake it.
- **Strip formatting that parses badly.** If you drop these into a resume template with multi-column layouts, tables, graphics, or contact details sitting in the page header or footer, an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) may read them out of order or miss them entirely. Keep your name, phone and email in the body text.
- **Match the heading block to the submission method.** The name-and-contact line at the top of Example 1 is useful for an attached file, but delete it if you are pasting the letter into a portal field that already collects your details.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I send these as PDF or Word files?+**

PDF keeps the line breaks and spacing exactly as you set them, which matters for the bulleted resume block in Example 2. Word (.docx) keeps the text editable and is accepted by most district portals. Judge both on the same two criteria: does the posting name a format, and does the upload field list accepted types? If the posting specifies one, use that; if a portal gives you a plain text box instead, paste the letter without the bullet characters and keep one blank line between paragraphs.

**How do I cut one of these letters down if it runs long?+**

All three templates are built to fit on one page. If yours spills over after you fill in the brackets, cut in this order: the demonstration-lesson or portfolio offer in the closing line, then the second concrete example in the body paragraph, then any clause restating the job title you already named in the first sentence. Do not cut the certification sentence — it is the line a school needs to see first.

**How long should the resume that goes with these be?+**

If you are applying for your first teaching job after student teaching, as in Example 1, one page is normally enough and forcing a second page usually means padding. If you have a decade or more of classroom experience, National Board certification, department leadership, or published curriculum work, two pages is reasonable in K-12 hiring — put the teaching bullets on page one and the coursework, endorsements and professional development on page two.

**Can I use the same letter for several schools in one district?+**

You can reuse the structure, but not the file as-is. Change the school name, the principal's name, the grade level or course in the first sentence, and the school-specific sentence in the third paragraph. With roughly 99,400 openings for kindergarten and elementary school teachers and 63,500 for high school teachers projected each year on average over the decade (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025), you may well be writing several of these in a week — keep one master file with the brackets intact and save each application as a separate copy.
