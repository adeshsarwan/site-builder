The hidden job market is simply the set of roles that get filled before anyone writes a job ad: through referrals, internal moves, and someone remembering a name at the right moment. You can't search it, but you can work your way into it with a target list, a short pitch, and a steady cadence of specific, polite outreach.

This guide sets out what the research actually says, a ten-step process you can start today, and a checklist to run through before you send the first message.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Build a named target list of 20–30 employers before you send a single message — outreach without a list turns into drifting.
> - Use the subject line '[Your name]—informational interview request' (Indeed) and personalise every template before sending.
> - Ask for a referral only after a conversation, not in the first message — referrals were employers' top source of hires, at more than 30% of all hires in 2016 (SHRM, June 2017).

## Why This Strategy Matters

Most job seekers spend their time where the jobs are easiest to see: the big boards. That is also where the competition is thickest and the feedback loop is worst. Reported response rates for cold online applications are low — around 2–3% by one vendor's estimate (Uppl.ai) and as low as 0.1–2% by another (HiringThing), with one careers site reporting that 75% of applications get no reply at all (The Interview Guys). Treat all of these as rough, self-reported figures rather than laws of nature; they vary enormously by industry, seniority and region. The direction of travel is consistent, though: cold applying is a numbers game with poor odds, and one estimate puts the typical requirement at 32–200 applications per offer (The Interview Guys).

Meanwhile, a large share of hiring never reaches a board at all. CareerOneStop, run by the U.S. Department of Labor, states plainly that most job openings are never posted to job banks like Indeed or ZipRecruiter. Other estimates put the unadvertised share at up to 70% (Management Consulted) or 70–80% (The Interview Guys). Those specific percentages are contested and poorly sourced — treat them as directional, not precise. What is better documented is the referral side: SHRM reported in June 2017 that employee referrals were employers' top source of hires, delivering more than 30% of all hires and 45% of internal hires.

So the strategy is not magic. It is a shift in where you spend your hours. CareerOneStop calls networking the most valuable way to invest your time in a job search, and suggests concrete starting steps: build a list of who is in your network, and prepare an elevator pitch. The rest of this guide turns that into a repeatable weekly routine.

Nothing here guarantees an interview or an offer. What it does is put your name in front of people who will be asked "do you know anyone?" before a requisition is ever written.

## Step-by-Step Process

1. **Build a target employer list of 20–30 names.** Open a spreadsheet with columns: Company, Why them, Team I'd join, People I know there, Last contact, Next action. Fill it from industry association member lists, your customers and suppliers, competitors of your last employer, and companies that keep appearing in trade press. Example starting search: `"[your industry]" association member directory "[your city]"`.
2. **Map who you already know, before you look for strangers.** CareerOneStop recommends building a list of who is in your network as a first step. List former colleagues, managers, clients, classmates, neighbours, people from volunteering, and anyone whose job you half understand. Aim for 40 names in one sitting, and mark each one with the companies from step 1 they might connect to.
3. **Find the second ring with LinkedIn's People search filters.** Search a job title, then use the People tab and apply the *Current company*, *Location* and *Connections* filters — set Connections to 2nd to see who you could be introduced to. On a university page, open the *Alumni* tab to filter graduates by where they work and what they do. Example: People search for `operations manager`, Current company set to one of your targets, Location set to your region.
4. **Write a 30-second pitch and say it out loud twice.** CareerOneStop lists preparing an elevator pitch as a core networking step. Structure: what you do, one proof point, what you're looking for next. Example shape — "I'm a logistics coordinator; I ran the depot transfer that cut our picking errors in the last job; I'm looking for a planning role with a mid-sized distributor in [region]." Replace every part with your own facts.
5. **Send five informational interview requests.** Use the subject line Indeed suggests: `[Your name]—informational interview request`. Keep the ask small and specific — 20 minutes, a video call, a date range. Personalise the bracketed parts before sending; a template sent raw reads like a template.

> Sample first-contact email — edit every bracket before sending
> 
> Subject: [Your name]—informational interview request
> 
> Hi [Name], I'm a [your role] moving into [target area], and I came across your work on [specific project, post or team you actually saw]. I'm not asking about openings — I'd like 20 minutes to hear how [their team or function] is structured and what skills matter most there. Would [day] or [day] next week work? Happy to send questions in advance. Thanks, [Your name].

1. **Run the call with three prepared questions and one ask.** Good questions: how work actually arrives in their team, what they wish new hires already knew, and which other teams or companies do this work well. Close with a named ask: "Is there one other person you'd suggest I speak to about [specific topic]?" Write the name straight into your spreadsheet.
2. **Join one place where your trade talks shop.** Pick a professional association branch, a local meetup, an industry Slack or Discord, or a conference attendee list. Example: set a recurring calendar entry for the monthly branch meeting, and commit to asking two people what they're working on rather than handing out your CV.
3. **Set alerts for hiring triggers, not just job titles.** New funding, a new office, a new department head, or a contract win usually precedes hiring by weeks. Create a Google Alert for `"[Company name]" expansion OR "new office" OR appoints` and follow the company on LinkedIn so its posts appear in your feed. When a trigger fires, contact your warmest person there within a few days.
4. **Ask for the referral after the relationship exists, not before.** When a role does appear, message the person you already spoke to and make it easy for them: give the exact job title, requisition link if there is one, and two lines they can paste into the internal referral form. Personalise before sending.

> Sample referral request — rewrite in your own words
> 
> Hi [Name], thanks again for the chat about [specific thing you discussed]. [Company] has just posted [exact job title]. Given what you said about [requirement], I think my experience with [your relevant project] fits. Would you be comfortable referring me internally? If it helps, here are two lines you can use: "[One sentence on your background]. [One sentence on the result you delivered]." No problem at all if it's not something you can do.

1. **Track every touch and follow up on a fixed cadence.** In your spreadsheet, log the date of each message and set the next action seven to ten working days out. One follow-up, then stop. Example follow-up subject: `Following up — 20 minutes on [team/topic]?` with two sentences referencing your original note and an offer to close the loop if the timing is wrong.

## Before You Start Checklist

- Target employer list has at least 20 named companies, each with a reason you want to work there written in one line.
- Spreadsheet or tracker is set up with columns for company, contact, date contacted, response, and next action date.
- LinkedIn headline and About section name the job title you want, not only the one you had, and include three terms that appear in postings for that role.
- Your resume carries the target job title wording, three keywords pulled from real postings for that role, and at least two quantified results (volume, time saved, budget, error rate).
- Elevator pitch is written down and spoken aloud at least twice, under 30 seconds.
- Email templates have had every bracket replaced and at least one sentence that could only apply to that person.
- You can name three specific questions you want to ask on an informational call, none of which is "are you hiring?"
- A recurring slot is blocked in your calendar — for example two hours on Tuesday mornings — for outreach and follow-ups.
- You know your own availability and notice period, and have checked current pay ranges for the role on official labour market statistics for your country and on live postings, so you can answer calmly if it comes up.
- You have a short, honest answer ready for any gap or career change in your history, in two sentences or fewer.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if I don't know anyone at the target company?+**

Start one ring out: alumni from your school, former colleagues who moved on, suppliers, or people who left the company recently and will speak more freely. If there is genuinely no connection, a cold informational request can still work when it references something specific they published or built, and asks for information rather than a job. Expect a lower reply rate from cold outreach than from warm introductions, and send in small batches so you can adjust your wording based on what gets answered.

**Should I stop applying to posted jobs altogether?+**

No. Posted roles are real and some are filled from the queue, particularly in large organisations, the public sector and anywhere hiring is regulated or unionised. A sensible split is to keep applying to well-matched postings while moving a meaningful share of your weekly hours into the target list and outreach. The aim is to be referred into the postings you apply to, not to abandon them.

**Isn't this just asking strangers for favours?+**

An informational interview asks for information, not a job, which is why the ask is twenty minutes and the questions are about how their work functions. Most people answer questions about their own job willingly if you have done the reading first and keep to the time you asked for. If you feel uncomfortable, start with the people you already know — CareerOneStop's advice to list your existing network first exists partly for that reason.

**How long does it take before this produces interviews?+**

It is slower to start and faster to finish than cold applying: the first few weeks often produce conversations and no openings, then several things move at once because people remember you when a vacancy appears. Judge your first month on activity you control — calls held, new names collected, companies added — not on interviews. No approach, this one included, can guarantee an interview or an offer.

**Someone agreed to refer me and nothing happened. What now?+**

Send one short note about ten working days later, restating the exact job title and offering to resend your resume, then let it go. Referrals stall for reasons that have nothing to do with you: the role gets frozen, filled internally, or the person simply gets busy. Keep the relationship intact, because the next opening at that company is the one that matters.

**Does this work if I'm new to a city or country?+**

It works, but your first ring is thinner, so build it deliberately through professional associations, alumni groups, volunteering and local branch meetings rather than relying on past colleagues. Be explicit in conversations that you are new to the area and want to understand how the local market hires, which is a reasonable and easy question for people to answer. For anything concerning work authorisation, check with the relevant official body in that country rather than relying on advice from contacts.
