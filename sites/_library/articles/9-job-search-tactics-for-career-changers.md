Changing careers makes a job search slower, because you are arguing a case as well as applying. The fix is not more applications — it is a small set of repeatable weekly actions that build evidence you can do the new work, plus a way to see which ones are producing replies.

For context on pacing: among all unemployed people in 2024, the average duration of unemployment was 21.6 weeks and the median was 9.6 weeks (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024). Career changers often sit on the longer side of that, so plan a search you can sustain for months rather than sprint for three weeks.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Build a list of 8-10 target companies and contact 1-2 each week — a steady pipeline beats a burst of applications.
> - Book two informational interviews a week with people doing the job now — the fastest way to learn the new field's vocabulary.
> - Scan 10-15 postings for your target title before you touch your resume — tailor to what actually repeats, not to guesswork.

## The Strategies

Pick three to start. Running all nine badly is worse than running three on a schedule.

**1. Scan 10-15 postings for your target title before you touch your resume**

CareerOneStop, the U.S. Department of Labor's job-search site, recommends scanning 10-15 job postings with your targeted job title and noting the requirements that come up most often, then formatting your resume with applicant tracking systems in mind, since those systems have become common in U.S. hiring (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). Tailoring means three specific things: matching the posted job title in your headline, carrying over the exact phrasing of recurring skills, and putting a number on results you can actually verify. Signal of success: you can list the five requirements that appeared in most of those postings, and each one appears somewhere in your resume in the employer's words.

**2. Build a list of 8-10 target companies and contact 1-2 each week**

CareerOneStop's sample job search plan suggests building a list of 8-10 companies to approach with job inquiries, using its Business Finder tool, and aiming to contact 1-2 businesses every week (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). Keep the list in one spreadsheet with columns for company, the person you contacted, the date, and the next action date — that spreadsheet is your tracking system. Signal of success: two logged outreach contacts per week, every week, with no row sitting past its next-action date.

**3. Book two informational interviews a week with people doing the job now**

An informational interview is an informal conversation with someone working in a career or company that interests you, used to learn about the work environment, gain clarity, get advice, and meet further contacts (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). The same guidance says to make contact by phone or email, introduce yourself and your situation — such as exploring a career change — and explain the purpose of the call. Personalize the message below before sending; the bracketed parts are not optional.

> Outreach email — edit every bracket before sending
> 
> Subject: [Your current field] person moving into [target field] — 20 minutes?
> 
> Hi [Name] — I'm [your name], currently [current role] and working toward a move into [target role]. I found your profile through [specific source: alumni directory, a post you wrote, a mutual contact]. I'm not asking you to pass my resume on; I'd like 20 minutes to ask how [specific part of their work] actually operates day to day. [Two options for times]. Happy to work around your calendar.

Signal of success: two conversations booked per week. Reply rates to cold outreach vary widely by field and by how specific your message is, so treat any ratio you read as approximate.

**4. Run the O*NET Interest Profiler before you lock in a target title**

My Next Move, from the U.S. Department of Labor's Employment and Training Administration, helps new job seekers, students, and career explorers investigate over 900 occupations and includes the O*NET Interest Profiler, a standardized assessment that aligns your interests with potential career paths through a series of questions (U.S. Department of Labor; National Center for O*NET Development). Do this before you rewrite anything — a pivot aimed at a title you half-chose wastes months. Signal of success: a shortlist of three occupation titles you can spell out, not a vague field.

**5. Feed your old job titles into mySkillsmyFuture for adjacent roles**

mySkillsmyFuture identifies career options based on your current or past jobs (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). Career changers routinely overlook the adjacent role that reuses 70% of what they already do — the tool surfaces those by starting from where you've been rather than where you'd like to be. Signal of success: at least two roles on your list that you had not previously considered.

**6. Write a two-minute elevator speech that names the pivot up front**

CareerOneStop's networking guidance tells job seekers to write an elevator speech — roughly a two-minute description of who you are and what you're looking for — before reaching out to contacts (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). As a changer, your first sentence should name the move explicitly, then give one piece of proof: a project, a certificate, a volunteer stint. Signal of success: you can say it out loud in under two minutes without reading, and three people repeat your target role back to you correctly.

**7. Filter Job Finder for "Remote Jobs Only" to widen your map**

CareerOneStop's Job Finder lets you enter a job title keyword and a location, and includes a filter to select "Remote Jobs Only" (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). Run the same target title twice each week — once local, once remote-only — and note which version returns more postings; that difference tells you whether geography is your constraint. Signal of success: a saved weekly list of postings for each of your three shortlisted titles.

**8. Draft in your own voice, then run one AI pass for clarity**

CareerOneStop suggests drafting your resume in your own voice first, then using free AI tools such as ChatGPT or Microsoft Copilot to suggest alternatives or improvements (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). Order matters — drafting with the tool first produces generic text that buries the transferable experience a changer needs to foreground. Signal of success: every suggestion you accept is still something you'd say in an interview without flinching.

**9. Book a session at your nearest American Job Center**

American Job Centers are physical and virtual centers across the United States; CareerOneStop maintains their contact details and surveys the centers twice a year (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor). What each center offers varies by state and by local workforce board — some run resume clinics and employer events, others focus on training referrals — so ask what is available in your area rather than assuming. Signal of success: one booked appointment and a written note of which local services you qualify for.

*No single tactic here produces an interview on its own. They work by compounding.*

## Putting It Together

A realistic first week is three strategies, not nine. Start with **Run the O*NET Interest Profiler before you lock in a target title**on day one, because everything downstream depends on knowing which title you're aiming at. Give it an evening, come out with three shortlisted occupations, then move straight into**Scan 10-15 postings for your target title before you touch your resume** for the strongest of the three. Reading those postings is also research for your conversations — you'll pick up the words the field uses for things you already do under a different name.

Week two is where outreach starts carrying the load. Pair **Build a list of 8-10 target companies and contact 1-2 each week** with **Book two informational interviews a week with people doing the job now**. They feed each other: the target-company list tells you who to ask for a conversation, and each conversation usually produces one or two more names to add to the list. Keep both in the same tracker so you can see at a glance which companies you've touched, when, and what you promised to send next.

From there, hold the cadence rather than adding more. Two company contacts and two conversations a week is roughly eight of each a month, which is enough volume to tell whether your framing is landing. If four weeks pass with almost no replies, the problem is usually the message or the target title, not the effort — go back to **Scan 10-15 postings for your target title before you touch your resume** and check whether you are describing yourself in the language those postings actually use.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many hours a week does this take?+**

Two company contacts and two informational conversations, plus one resume revision, fits into roughly five to eight hours if you are working full time. The research-heavy steps are front-loaded in the first week or two; after that the weekly load drops to outreach and follow-through on what you promised people.

**Do these government tools cost anything?+**

CareerOneStop, My Next Move, mySkillsmyFuture and the American Job Center network are public services from the U.S. Department of Labor and its state partners, so they are free to use. What individual American Job Centers provide beyond the basics varies by state and local workforce board, and training funding in particular has eligibility rules you'd need to ask about directly.

**Will informational interviews actually lead to a job, or am I just chatting?+**

They are for learning the field's language, testing whether the work suits you, and meeting people — not for asking for a job, and asking directly tends to end the conversation. CareerOneStop notes that many openings are never posted publicly and that employers often find candidates through networking, referrals and direct contact from job seekers, which is why the conversations matter even when nothing is open that week.

**How long should I expect a career-change search to take?+**

There is no reliable figure for changers specifically, but general unemployment duration gives you a floor: among all unemployed people in 2024, the median was 9.6 weeks and the average 21.6 weeks (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024). Build a plan you can run for several months at a steady pace rather than one that burns out in a fortnight.
