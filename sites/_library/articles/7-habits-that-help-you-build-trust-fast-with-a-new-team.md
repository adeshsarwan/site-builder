People decide whether you are reliable long before they decide whether you are talented. On a new team, that judgment is built from small, observable things: whether you remember what you were told, whether your deadlines hold, whether you check before changing something that already works.

Below are seven habits with the specific mechanism behind each one — what to say, when to say it, and where. How directly you can speak up varies by employer, by industry and by country, so treat the scripts as starting points and adjust them to how your team actually talks.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Send your manager a "here's what I think I understood" summary at the end of week one — it catches wrong assumptions while they are still cheap to fix.
> - Give status updates before anyone asks, including bad news, so people stop checking on you by day 30.
> - Ask "what's been tried before?" for your first 60 days instead of proposing fixes — you find out which problems are already known and which are political.

## The Habits

1. **Keep a week-one glossary and read it back out loud.** People quietly downgrade you when they have to explain the same acronym twice, and a written record stops that happening. Open one note on your first morning, log every acronym, system name, recurring meeting and who owns what, then at the end of day five message your manager: "Here's what I think I understood this week — tell me where I've got it wrong: [your list]."

1. **Ship one small, visible thing by day ten.** A finished small task proves more than a well-argued plan, because it shows you can get something through your team's actual process — the approvals, the review, the place things get posted. Pick something nobody is defending: fix the broken links in the onboarding doc, take and post notes for the weekly team meeting, or clear the oldest low-priority ticket in the queue.

1. **Book 20-minute intro calls with everyone your work touches, inside the first 30 days.** Trust forms faster with people who have met you once on purpose than with people who only see your name in a thread. Send a one-line invite — "I'm new on [team] and our work overlaps; can I get 20 minutes to ask you three questions?" — and ask the same three every time: what are you working on that my work could slow down or speed up, what does a good handoff from someone in my role look like, and what do new people here usually get wrong?

1. **Give the status update before anyone asks for it.** The moment a manager has to chase you, they start building a mental habit of checking on you, and that habit is hard to undo. Set a recurring reminder for Friday at 3pm to send a three-line update (done / in progress / blocked), and if a deadline is going to slip, say so on the morning it becomes obvious, not the afternoon it is due: "The report's at about 80% — I'll have it to you Thursday at 4 instead of Wednesday because the data pull failed twice. Does that break anything downstream for you?"

1. **Batch your questions, but escalate real blockers within 30 minutes.** Constant one-off pings make you feel busy and make other people feel interrupted, while silence on a genuine blocker costs the team a day. Keep a running question list in the same note as your glossary and bring it to your next 1:1, but apply a 30-minute rule: if you are stuck with no next step after half an hour, post it once in the team channel where others can see the answer rather than DMing three people separately.

1. **Ask "what's been tried before?" for your first 60 days.** Most obvious problems on an established team are known problems with a history, and walking into that history uninvited is the fastest way to look careless. When you spot something, phrase it as research rather than a proposal: "I noticed we do the weekly report by hand — has anyone tried automating it? I'd like to know what went wrong before I suggest anything."

1. **Credit people by name, in the channel where the work happened, within a day.** Public, specific credit costs you nothing and makes people willing to help you again, which is most of what you need in month one. Not "thanks everyone" in a DM, but in the team channel: "Thanks to Priya for walking me through the billing flow yesterday — saved me a day of guessing."

One thing to check rather than assume: how new people are expected to speak in meetings. In some workplaces you are expected to push back in week one; in others, and in some countries, that reads as disrespectful. Ask your manager in your first week: "When someone new here disagrees in a meeting, do they say it in the room or bring it to you first?"

## Habits That Backfire vs. Habits That Work

Most trust-damaging behavior in a first month is a good instinct applied without a mechanism. These pairs look similar from the outside and land very differently.

**Asking questions constantly vs. batching with a 30-minute rule**

- Backfires: You DM a teammate seven times on Tuesday — where's the template, what's the password policy, who approves this. Each one takes them 30 seconds and breaks their concentration for five minutes. By Thursday they answer slower.
- Works: You park six of those in your questions list for Thursday's 1:1, and the seventh — a locked system you cannot get past — you post in the team channel after 30 minutes stuck, where two people see the answer and one says "I had that too."

**Promising fast turnarounds vs. promising a date you've checked**

- Backfires: Asked in week one if you can have the analysis by Friday, you say "Absolutely, no problem," then discover on Thursday you need access you don't have. Your first delivery is late.
- Works: You say what you actually know out loud:

> Agreeing to a deadline you haven't verified
> 
> "Friday looks doable, but I haven't run this pull before. Let me confirm by end of day tomorrow — if I can't get access today, I'll tell you Friday is Monday."

**Suggesting improvements early vs. asking what's been tried**

- Backfires: In your second week you say in a team meeting that the approval process should be cut from three steps to one. Two of those steps exist because of an audit finding, and the person who built it is sitting opposite you.
- Works: You ask the person who owns it, privately, "what's the history of the three-step approval?" — learn about the audit, and in week eight propose removing only the step nobody can justify.

**Being available for everything vs. being reliable on a few things**

- Backfires: You say yes to four side requests in your first month, run out of hours, and deliver your actual assignment late. People remember the late assignment.
- Works: You take one side request, finish it early, and tell the next person: "I can't get to that this week without my main project slipping — can it wait until the 14th, or should I check with [manager] about priorities?"

**Over-documenting your work vs. one scheduled update**

- Backfires: You send your manager long daily recaps of everything you touched. They stop reading them by week two, so when you bury "blocked on vendor access" in paragraph four, nobody acts on it.
- Works: One Friday 3pm message with three labeled lines — done, in progress, blocked — where "blocked" is never more than one sentence and always names who can unblock it.

**Socializing broadly vs. 20-minute calls with the people your work touches**

- Backfires: You go to every coffee and happy hour, know everyone's weekend plans, and still have no idea who signs off on your deliverables or what the person downstream needs from you.
- Works: You run the 20-minute intro calls from habit 3, so in week five, when a handoff goes wrong, you already know who to call and they already know your name.

**Taking credit carefully vs. giving credit specifically**

- Backfires: You write "I pulled together the migration plan" in your month-one summary. Two people who spent an hour each explaining the system to you read it.
- Works: "Migration plan is drafted — Dan and Alex walked me through the legacy setup, which is why the rollback section exists." You still get credit for writing it, and both of them answer your next question.

## Frequently asked questions

**What changes if the team is fully remote?+**

The habits that rely on being seen have to be deliberate instead. The 20-minute intro calls in habit 3 matter more, because there is no hallway version of them — book them as video calls in your first 30 days rather than waiting to bump into people. Habit 7 also carries more weight remotely: if you thank someone in a DM, no one else sees it, so post the specific thank-you in the team channel where the work happened.

**My manager keeps canceling our 1:1. Where do my batched questions go?+**

Move habit 5 into writing. Keep the same running question list, and send it as a short numbered message before the slot that got canceled: "Three things I'd have asked today — answer whichever are quick, I'll hold the rest." Pair it with habit 4's Friday update so your manager still sees your status weekly even when the meeting does not happen, and after two cancellations ask directly who else can answer day-to-day questions.

**I was hired specifically to change things. Do I still wait 60 days?+**

No, but you still run habit 6's question first. In your first week, ask your manager what the mandate actually covers: "What am I expected to change, and what should I leave alone for now?" Then before each proposal, ask the person who owns the current process what's been tried before — it takes one conversation and it stops you from relitigating a decision that was made for a reason you can't see yet.
