"Tell me about yourself" is usually the opening move in a screening call or first-round interview, and it is less a warm-up than a filter. The interviewer is listening for whether you can summarise your working life in a way that points at their job, in the time it takes to make coffee.

This guide covers what the question is screening for, three sample answers for different career stages, a template with placeholders you fill in with your own facts, and the specific phrases that make answers fall flat. How long an answer is expected varies by employer: a startup founder may cut you off at 45 seconds, while a structured panel at a large hospital system or government agency may let you run the full two minutes.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Land a two-minute summary that ends with why this specific role, not your life story.
> - Lead with your current work, then relevant history, then the connection to the job.
> - Do not narrate your resume chronologically from your first job onward.

## Why Interviewers Ask This

The hiring concern behind this question is editing. Everything on your resume is already in front of the interviewer. What they cannot see is whether you know which parts matter for the job they are filling, and whether you can say so in order, out loud, without a script.

Indeed's career guide describes the question as a way to ease a candidate into the conversation and to gauge which of your qualifications you see as most relevant (Indeed Career Guide, 2026-08-04). CareerSidekick makes the same point from the interviewer's side: the summary sets the direction for the questions that follow. EmployBridge adds two more signals being read in those two minutes — your communication skills and a first read on personality and fit. Robert Walters notes the answer often sets the tone for the rest of the interview and shows whether you prepared for this opportunity specifically.

The red flags are concrete. Reciting your resume signals you did not prepare, because the interviewer could have read it themselves (FlexJobs). A four-minute life story signals you will also run long in client meetings. An answer that never mentions the job signals you are applying everywhere.

Expectations do vary. In the US, personal details such as age, marital status or a photo are not part of a professional introduction, while CV and interview conventions in some other countries treat them as normal. Match the norms of the country and employer you are interviewing with.

## Strong Sample Answers

All names, employers and figures below are invented. Use your own.

### Career changer — Present-Past-Future structure

> Moving from classroom teaching into instructional design
> 
> "Right now I'm a seventh-grade science teacher at Halverson Middle School, where I also build the digital lesson modules our department uses across four classrooms. Before teaching I spent three years as a training coordinator at Brightline Logistics, writing onboarding materials for warehouse staff, so curriculum design is where I started rather than a late detour. Over the last two years I've completed a certificate in instructional design and rebuilt our science units in your industry's standard authoring tool. I'm looking for a role where that build work is the job, not the evening project — which is why this instructional designer opening caught my attention."

### Entry-level graduate — STAR structure for the experience section

> Recent computer science graduate, one internship
> 
> "I graduated in May with a computer science degree from Carrowan State, concentrating in data engineering. The piece I'd point to is my internship at Mapleford Health. *Situation:* their nightly patient-intake report was failing roughly twice a week. *Task:* I was asked to find out why. *Action:* I rewrote the ingestion script, added validation on three malformed fields, and set up alerting so failures surfaced before the morning shift. *Result:* it ran clean for the final nine weeks of my internship. I want to keep doing pipeline work on a team that reviews code properly, which is what your posting describes."

### Senior hire — short 'why I'm a fit' structure, skipping the career story

> Operations director, fifteen years in manufacturing
> 
> "I'll keep the history short — fifteen years in plant operations, the last six as operations director at Corvid Components, running two sites and a team of about ninety. Three things from that are relevant here. I took a plant through an ISO recertification after a failed audit. I've managed a shift-pattern change without losing the senior line leads. And I've reported production numbers to a private-equity board every quarter, which I understand is part of this job. Your posting says the second site opens next year, and standing up a new site is the part of the work I like most."

CareerSidekick describes this shorter approach for experienced candidates — skip the chronology, state the fit. Robert Half similarly offers separate model answers for experienced professionals, entry-level candidates and career changers, because the three need different emphases.

## Answer Framework You Can Adapt

Fill in the brackets, then say it out loud with a timer. Indeed's guidance is to tell a complete story in two minutes or less (Indeed Career Guide, 2026-08-04), and EmployBridge suggests keeping any personal introduction to about one to two sentences before moving to professional experience.

The three beats are present, past, future (Robert Walters), which matches Indeed's three-step structure: current situation, work backward through your journey, then connect to the job.

> 1. Present — one or two sentences
> 
> "I'm a [job title] at [Company], where I [scope: team size, budget, region, product, caseload]. The part of the role I spend most time on is [core activity]."

> 2. Past — two or three sentences, most relevant items only
> 
> "I came into [field] through [first relevant role or qualification]. The thing I'd point to is [one accomplishment]: I [action you personally took], and [result, with a number if you have one]."

> 3. Future — one or two sentences, naming this job
> 
> "I'm looking for [specific thing this role offers], and your posting mentions [detail from the job ad], which is why I applied."

> 4. Pivot line, if the honest answer includes a weakness
> 
> Gap: "I took [length] away from work for [reason, one clause]. I used part of it to [concrete thing: certificate, contract work, caregiving], and I've been interviewing since [month]."
> 
> Layoff: "My role ended when [Company] closed the [site/team] in [month]. Since then I've [action]."
> 
> Missing credential: "I don't have [credential] yet — I'm [enrolled/scheduled for the exam in month]. What I do have is [the adjacent experience that covers the same ground]."
> 
> Name it in one clause, then move forward. Do not apologise for it twice.

> 5. Realistic pushback — plan for it
> 
> **Interviewer:** "You skipped the eighteen months between Brightline and your current job. What happened there?"
> 
> **You:** "Brightline closed the Dayton site, so the whole coordination team went. I did contract onboarding work for two employers during that stretch — it's on my resume as 'contract', which is easy to miss — and I took the instructional design certificate during the quieter months. Happy to walk through either engagement if that's useful."

If you are asked the alternate version — "Tell me something about yourself that's not on your resume" — the beats change. Give one work-relevant thing the paperwork cannot show, such as a side project, a language, or how you learn new tools.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Don't start at the beginning. "I grew up in Ohio and went to school for business" spends your first thirty seconds on the least relevant decade. Start with what you are doing now.
- Don't say "it's all on my resume" or read it aloud line by line. FlexJobs makes the point directly: the interviewer could have read your resume already.
- Don't say "we" for work you did. Indeed's STAR guidance is explicit — in the Action step, use "I" even when the accomplishment was a team one (Indeed Career Guide, 2026-08-17). "We redesigned the intake form" tells the interviewer nothing about you.
- Don't spend the answer on setup. In a STAR example, keep situation and task short and give the most time to the action, because that is the part that shows fitness for the role (Indeed Career Guide, 2026-08-17).
- Don't end on your last job. If the final sentence is not about this role, you have given a biography rather than an application. End with one detail from the job posting.
- Don't open with "I'm a people person" or "I'm passionate about [industry]". These are unverifiable and every other candidate says them. Replace with something checkable: a system you own, a process you fixed, a certification in progress.
- Don't run past two minutes without checking in. If you can see the answer getting long, stop and ask: "Do you want the full background, or should I jump to why I applied?"

## Frequently asked questions

**What if a second interviewer asks the same question later the same day?+**

Give a different cut of the same material rather than repeating it word for word. Panel members often compare notes. For a hiring manager, weight the answer toward the technical work; for a department head, weight it toward scope and stakeholders. You can also say "Jordan asked me this morning, so I'll focus on the operations side this time" — it shows you noticed who you were talking to.

**What if the interviewer cuts me off after twenty seconds?+**

Stop talking and answer whatever they asked instead. An interruption usually means they heard the hook they wanted or they are short on time, not that your answer failed. Keep your remaining points in your head and work them in later — most interviews end with "anything else we should know?", which is where the unused half of your introduction belongs.

**What if I'm applying to a job unrelated to anything I've done?+**

Build the answer around transferable activity rather than job titles. Name the parts of your current work that the new role also requires — scheduling, writing, handling complaints, reconciling numbers — and say which one you want to do full time. Robert Half publishes separate model answers for career changers for this reason: the chronology matters less than the throughline you choose to draw.

**Does the answer change for a recruiter screen versus the hiring manager?+**

Yes, in emphasis. A recruiter screen is usually checking that you match the posting on the obvious points — level, location, notice period, core skills — so keep the answer under a minute and hit those. A hiring manager wants the substance of the work, so that is where the detailed example belongs. Ask at the start how much time they have.
