Someone presented your analysis, your deck or your fix as theirs, and you're new enough that nobody in the room knows whose it was. The useful response is not a confrontation — it's a short factual check, one direct conversation, and a change to how your work reaches your manager from now on.

This guide walks through the first week after you notice it, with wording you can use out loud, plus what to do if it happens a second time.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Before you react, confirm what was actually said in the room — secondhand accounts of who claimed what are wrong often enough to be worth one question.
> - For a first incident, go to the coworker directly within two or three days, about that one piece of work, not about their character.
> - For a second incident, go to your manager with dates and artifacts, and change how your work reaches them so authorship is visible without you having to argue for it.

## Understanding the Situation

In a new job you have no track record, so credit travels through whoever is in the room rather than through what people already know about you. Your work usually passes up a chain — you to a teammate, teammate to your manager, manager to their boss — and your name can drop off at any link. In a three-person team, everyone knows who built what. On a 30-person team with a weekly review meeting, the senior person summarizing the work is the only name the audience hears.

The mechanics that cause it are usually dull. A draft gets shared as an attachment instead of a file with edit history, so there's no author trail. A handoff happens verbally at a desk or in a direct message that nobody else sees. Someone says "we pulled the numbers" and the room hears "I pulled the numbers." Norms differ by industry too: in agency and consulting work the person who presents to the client is often credited by default, in software the commit and ticket history make authorship visible to anyone who looks, and in sales the account owner gets the win regardless of who built the proposal.

After one incident you usually cannot tell whether it was deliberate or careless, and you do not need to. The first-incident response is the same either way — name the specific piece of work, to the specific person, and make your authorship visible on the record going forward. If it is careless, that fixes it. If it is deliberate, it puts the person on notice and gives you something concrete to point at the second time.

## Step-by-Step Response

1. **Within 24 hours, write down the facts.** Date, the meeting or email, the exact piece of work, and what was said as closely as you can recall. Then collect the artifacts: the message where you sent the draft, the ticket assigned to you, the version history on the shared file. You are not building a case yet — you are making sure that if you need details in three weeks, you have them.
2. **In the next day or two, check what was actually said.** If you heard about it secondhand, ask someone who was there: *"Did the forecast section come up as a team thing, or did it land as Dan's? I built that part and I'm trying to work out whether that was clear to the room."* Sometimes the answer is that your name was mentioned and the person relaying it to you missed it.
3. **Day two or three, speak to the coworker directly.** One incident, specific, no audience, no accusation of intent. Wording is in the next section. Keep it under two minutes and end with what you want next time, not an apology demand.
4. **The same week, change how your work reaches your manager.** Send deliverables to your manager directly rather than only through the teammate who consolidates them, with two lines of context: *"Attaching the churn analysis — I built the model, Dan reviewed the assumptions."* Keep drafts in a shared file that records who edited what, rather than emailing attachments back and forth, so authorship is visible without anybody having to remember it. Put your name in the document header.
5. **By the end of the first week, start a short weekly update to your manager.** Three to five lines, Friday, listing what you finished and what you're on next. This is not self-promotion — it means your manager can describe your work accurately when asked, which is the specific behavior that stops credit disputes before they start.
6. **If it happens a second time, raise it with your manager within a few days.** Bring the two dates and the two artifacts. Ask for a change to how work is attributed, not for a verdict on the coworker.
7. **If it continues past 30 days of raising it, check your handbook before escalating further.** What counts as a formal complaint, who receives it and what happens next vary a lot by employer, and in some countries and US states there are additional internal procedures. Read your own policy rather than assuming the process works the way it did at your last job.

## What to Say

### Directly to the coworker, a day or two after

> You
> 
> "Quick thing about yesterday's review — when the retention slides came up, it sounded like you'd built them. I pulled that data and wrote the summary. Can we say it's both of us next time?"

> Likely response
> 
> "Oh — I didn't mean it that way, I was just answering Maria's question."

> You
> 
> "Fine, I figured. Easiest fix: send it to Maria with both names on it, and I'll send you my part by Thursday so there's time."

### To your manager, after the second time

> You
> 
> "I want to flag something factual. The March pricing model and last week's churn deck were both mine, and both got presented as Dan's. I've talked to him once already. I'm not asking you to referee — I'd like to send my work to you directly instead of routing it through him."

> Likely response
> 
> "Dan does consolidate the team's work, that's his role."

> You
> 
> "Then can the deck list who did each section? That solves it without changing the process."

### In the room, while it's happening

> Coworker
> 
> "...so I rebuilt the query and got it down to four seconds."

> You
> 
> "Happy to take the follow-ups on that one — I wrote the query, so I can explain the caching trade-off if anyone wants the detail."

That offers useful information rather than correcting someone publicly, and it puts your authorship on the record in front of the same people who heard the claim.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if my manager is the one taking the credit?+**

Managers do legitimately present team work upward, so judge it by whether your name ever reaches anyone above them. In your next one-to-one, ask directly: "When the migration plan went to the leadership review, was it clear that I built it? I'd like my work to be visible to that group." If nothing changes within 60 days, that is useful information about whether this job will get you promoted, and worth factoring into how long you stay.

**Should I go to HR about this?+**

Not for a first or second incident of misattributed credit — HR will usually ask whether you raised it with the person and your manager first. HR becomes relevant if the pattern is tied to something your employer treats as discrimination or retaliation, or if it is affecting your formal performance record. What HR will and won't act on varies considerably by employer, and by country, so read your own policy rather than guessing.

**It happened again a month after I raised it. Now what?+**

Go back to your manager with both incidents, dated, and ask for a specific process change rather than a conversation: named sections in shared decks, your deliverables sent to your manager directly, or you presenting your own work at the review. If your manager declines all three and the pattern continues through your next 30 days, treat it as a decision about the job rather than a problem to keep solving.

**The coworker is much more senior and I'm three weeks in. Do I still say something?+**

Yes, but keep it to one sentence about one piece of work and say it privately, not in a group channel. Seniority changes the tone, not the facts: "I built the forecast section — can I be named on it next time?" is a reasonable thing for a new hire to ask. If you are genuinely unsure how it will land, ask the person who onboarded you how that individual usually takes feedback before you go.

**I work remotely and the credit-taking happens in meetings I'm not invited to. What can I do?+**

Fix the artifact rather than the meeting, because you can't attend everything. Keep your work in shared files with visible edit history, put your name in the document header, and send a short Friday summary to your manager so the written record matches what you did. Within your first 60 days, ask your manager which recurring meetings your work gets presented in and whether you can join the one that matters most.
