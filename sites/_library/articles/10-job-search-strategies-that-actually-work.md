Most job search advice fails because it names a category of effort rather than an action you can start on a Tuesday. The strategies below each have a trigger, a cadence and a number you can count at the end of the week, so you can tell the difference between a search that's working and one that's just busy.

None of them guarantees an interview, and the mix that works depends on your field, your location and whether the roles you want are posted publicly at all. Pick three, run them for two weeks, and keep the ones that produce conversations.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Send 5 informational interview requests a week — and log replies, not sends
> - Apply to postings under 48 hours old using the date-posted filter
> - Run one mock interview a week on the employer's video platform

## The Strategies

**1. Send 5 informational interview requests a week**

Pick one target job title or company, find two or three people doing that work, and contact them by call or email explaining your purpose and proposing a couple of times — then confirm the date, time and format once they reply (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). The point is not to ask for a job: it's to learn about the work environment and trends in the field, get a read on whether the company fits you, and meet people who may offer guidance or leads later (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Signal of success: two completed conversations a week, measured by calls held, not messages sent.

**2. Message 3 alumni from your school each week through LinkedIn's alumni tool**

On LinkedIn, go to your school's university page and open the Alumni section, which lets you filter graduates by where they live, where they work and what they do. Shortlist three whose current title matches the job you want, and send a short note referencing the specific thing you want to learn. Signal of success: a reply rate you can actually track — count replies out of messages sent each week and watch whether rewording the first line moves it.

**3. Apply to postings under 48 hours old using the date-posted filter**

Both LinkedIn and Indeed let you narrow job search results by how recently a job was posted (LinkedIn offers a "Past 24 hours" option; Indeed has a "Date posted" filter). Spend your first applying block of the day only on fresh postings, and leave older ones for a weekly sweep. Signal of success: at least 70% of the applications you logged this week came from postings less than two days old — the ratio of applications to interviews varies enormously by field and is never a fixed number, so measure your own.

**4. Build a requirements-versus-qualifications table before every application**

Write down each requirement listed in the posting and, next to it, what you have against it — this shows you where you may be lacking and lets you plan how to address the gap during the interview (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Do this before you write anything, because the gaps tell you what the cover letter has to answer. Signal of success: every application in your tracker has a completed table with at least one planned answer for the weakest row.

**5. Rewrite your resume's top third to match the posting's job title and three keywords**

Tailoring is not a vague instruction: change the headline title to the exact title in the posting where it's honest to do so, and make sure three terms the posting repeats appear in your summary or first role, with a number attached to at least one. Keep a master version and save each tailored copy under the employer name. Signal of success: you can point at three specific edits per application and say which line of the posting each one answers.

**6. Write a 30-second elevator pitch and rehearse it out loud ten times**

CareerOneStop advises preparing an elevator pitch, saying it helps you feel more confident when networking and get better results (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Cover what you do, what you're looking for, and one concrete example — then say it aloud until you stop reading it. Signal of success: you can deliver it in under 30 seconds, from memory, without a filler sentence at the start.

**7. Set three saved searches with daily email alerts and triage them in one 20-minute block**

Indeed and LinkedIn both let you save a search and receive email alerts when new matching jobs appear. Create three — one for your exact title, one for an adjacent title, one for a skill or tool you want to use — and open them all in a single scheduled block instead of refreshing all day. Signal of success: inbox to shortlist in 20 minutes, with five saved roles a week worth a full application.

**8. Keep a one-tab tracker with a "next action date" column**

Columns: company, role, date applied, source, contact name, status, next action, next action date. The next-action column is what turns a list into a system, because it tells you what today's work is without re-reading everything. Signal of success: zero rows with a next action date in the past at the end of each week.

**9. Run one mock interview a week with a friend on the employer's video platform**

CareerOneStop's tips for virtual interviews are to be tech-ready by checking your computer, camera and microphone and practicing on the employer's video platform; research the employer and job description; set up a quiet, uncluttered, professional-looking space; and do a practice run by asking a friend for a mock interview (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Use the friend's feedback to improve your answers, eye contact and engagement before the real thing (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Signal of success: one recorded or reviewed practice run per week, with two specific changes noted.

**10. Follow up seven calendar days after applying, to a named person**

Put the follow-up date in your tracker when you apply, find the hiring manager or recruiter by name, and send one short message. Personalize every line of the template below before you send it — as written it will read like a form letter, which is the one thing it must not do.

> Follow-up note — edit before sending
> 
> Subject: [Job title] — application from [your name]
> 
> Hi [first name], I applied for the [job title] role on [date] and wanted to add one thing: [one specific, relevant result or project that matches a requirement in the posting]. If it's useful, I'm happy to walk through how I'd approach [specific challenge named in the posting]. Either way, thanks for your time.

Signal of success: a follow-up sent on schedule for every application that has a named contact, and a count of how many produce a human reply.

## Putting It Together

Don't run all ten. In a first week, combine **Set three saved searches with daily email alerts and triage them in one 20-minute block** with **Apply to postings under 48 hours old using the date-posted filter**. The alerts do the finding overnight; your morning block does the sorting. Everything you shortlist goes straight into the tracker from **Keep a one-tab tracker with a "next action date" column**, with the follow-up date filled in on the day you apply rather than later.

Attach **Build a requirements-versus-qualifications table before every application** to each shortlisted role, and let it drive **Rewrite your resume's top third to match the posting's job title and three keywords**. The table tells you which requirements you meet cleanly — those are the keywords worth putting near the top — and which ones you'll need to address in conversation. Two thorough applications built this way will usually take the same time as six skimmed ones.

For the people side, pair **Send 5 informational interview requests a week** with **Write a 30-second elevator pitch and rehearse it out loud ten times**, because the pitch is what you'll need the moment someone says yes. Block one hour early in the week for outreach and one slot later for whatever conversations land. If interviews start coming in, add **Run one mock interview a week with a friend on the employer's video platform** and drop the volume of applications rather than the preparation — at that point, conversion matters more than count.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much time a week does this take?+**

A workable version is about six to eight hours: one 20-minute triage block most weekdays, two longer blocks for tailored applications, and one hour for outreach. If you're employed full time, run the alert triage and tracker daily and keep outreach to a single evening slot rather than trying to do everything.

**Do informational interviews work if I'm changing careers?+**

They tend to be more useful for career changers than for people staying put, because you're buying information you don't have — how the work actually runs, which of your existing skills translate, and what the field's trends look like. Be direct that you're exploring a move, and ask about the path people took rather than asking for a referral in the first conversation.

**Is following up after an application worth it, or does it annoy recruiters?+**

One short, specific message a week after applying is normal professional behaviour; repeated chasing is not. Response rates vary by employer size, hiring volume and whether applications route through a shared inbox, so treat silence as information about the process rather than about you.

**Do I need paid tools or a premium subscription?+**

No. Saved searches, email job alerts, date-posted filters and school alumni directories are free features on the major job boards and on LinkedIn, and a spreadsheet does everything a paid tracker does. Check what your state's public workforce agency or local library offers before paying for anything, since availability differs by state.
