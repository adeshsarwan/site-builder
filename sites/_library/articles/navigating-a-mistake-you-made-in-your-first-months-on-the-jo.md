Everyone new gets something wrong. You don't yet know which approvals are real, who reads what before it goes out, or which small step the team treats as non-negotiable. The part you control is what happens in the hours and days after you notice — who hears it from you, how precisely you describe it, and what you change so it doesn't repeat.

This guide covers why early mistakes cluster in the first months, a step-by-step response for the first week, and exact wording for the conversations with your manager and the colleague whose work you affected.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Report the same day you notice, not when you have a full fix: say what broke, who's affected, and what you've already stopped.
> - Decide by consequence, not embarrassment — anything touching money, customers, data, safety or a regulated record goes to your manager before you attempt a quiet fix.
> - Fix the step, not the single instance: name the specific check you'll run from now on, and bring evidence it worked to your next scheduled check-in.

## Understanding the Situation

New-hire mistakes are mostly information problems, not character problems. In your first months you are working from the written process, and almost every team also runs on unwritten steps: the colleague who quietly reviews outgoing client emails, the figure that always gets checked against a second system, the request that looks routine but needs a director's sign-off. Nobody lists those in onboarding because they feel obvious to people who've been there three years.

Team size and reporting structure change how fast a mistake surfaces. On a team of four with one manager, someone usually notices within a day and it gets handled in conversation. In a larger department where your work passes through two or three handoffs, the same error can sit undetected for a week and then land in front of someone who has never met you. Industry norms matter too: in healthcare, finance, aviation, lab work and other regulated settings, there's often a required incident or deviation report with its own deadline, and skipping it is treated as worse than the original error. In a small agency or startup, the expectation may simply be that you say it out loud in the team chat the moment you spot it.

Timing also makes early errors feel heavier than they are. Around 22 percent of wage and salary workers had been with their employer a year or less as of January 2024 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024-09), so managers deal with new-person errors constantly. Many employers run a 90-day probationary period that starts on your first day and ends exactly 90 days later (Indeed, undated), and SHRM suggests managers hold one-on-ones at 30, 60 and 90 days specifically to surface roadblocks early (SHRM, undated). Those check-ins are the built-in place to show the correction stuck.

## Step-by-Step Response

1. **In the first hour, stop the spread before you fix anything.** Pause the next send, the next batch, the next upload. If the mistake touched money, a customer, personal data, safety, or a record someone outside the company relies on, do not attempt a silent repair — an unexplained correction in a system log looks far worse later than a reported error does now.
2. **Spend ten minutes writing the facts down, same day.** Five lines, no adjectives: what you did, when, what it affected, how many people or records, and what you've already stopped. You will need these numbers in the conversation, and writing them stops you from either minimising or catastrophising.
3. **Tell your manager the same day, by voice if you can.** Not a long message, not an apology paragraph. Open with the fact and the number: "I need two minutes — I sent the Thursday run with last month's rates, 41 invoices are wrong, and I've paused the next run." If your manager is out, tell the person who covers for them, and send your manager the same five lines in writing so there's a timestamp.
4. **Ask what the company's actual procedure is, out loud.** Say: "Is there a form or an incident log for this, or does it just go to you?" This varies enormously — regulated employers often require a written report within a set window, while smaller firms may want nothing beyond a message in the team channel. Guessing is the part that creates a second problem.
5. **Within 48 hours, propose the correction and ask who owns it.** Offer a draft of the client email or the corrected file rather than asking what you should do, but ask before sending: "I've drafted the correction. Does it go from me, or should it come from you?" On client-facing errors, the answer is often that it comes from the manager, and sending it yourself first is its own mistake.
6. **By day five, name the step that failed and the check you're adding.** Not "I'll be more careful." Something observable: "I'm reading the rate field back against the contract before I run the batch, and I'll note the date I checked." One check you will actually do beats three you won't.
7. **Put it on the agenda of your next scheduled check-in.** If your employer runs 30-, 60- and 90-day one-on-ones (SHRM, undated), bring two weeks of evidence that the new check is running. SHRM's suggested 30-day question is to ask the new hire to "Tell me what you don't understand about your job or about our organization now that you've had a month" (SHRM, undated) — answer it honestly, because the gap that produced this mistake is usually sitting in that answer.

## What to Say

### Telling your manager the day it happens

> You, in person or on a call
> 
> "I need two minutes. I sent Thursday's invoice run using last month's rates — 41 invoices went out wrong. I've paused the next run and pulled the list of affected accounts."
> 
> **Manager:** "How long have you known?"
> 
> **You:** "About twenty minutes. I checked the list first so I could bring you the number. Do you want me to draft the correction email, or does that go out from billing?"

### When your manager points out it's not the first one

> **Manager:** "This is the second thing this week."
> 
> **You:** "It is, and both came out of the sales handoff form. Starting today I'm reading the form back to the rep before I enter anything. Can we look on Friday at whether that caught it?"
> 
> **Manager:** "Fine. Friday."
> 
> **You:** "I'll bring the count of how many I caught before entry."

### Telling the colleague whose work you affected

> **You:** "I overwrote your version of the deck at four o'clock. The file history still has your 3:40 version — I can restore it or you can, whichever you prefer. It was me, not a sync problem."
> 
> **Them:** "I'd already redone half of it."
> 
> **You:** "Send me what's left and I'll rebuild it tonight. And I'll post in the team chat before I edit anything in the shared folder."

## Frequently asked questions

**What if I only notice the mistake weeks later?+**

Report it the day you notice, and say plainly when you made it and when you found it: "I made this three weeks ago and found it this morning while reconciling." The delay is only a problem if you hide it once you know. Expect a question about why it wasn't caught sooner — answer with the missing check, not with an apology.

**What if my manager puts me on a performance improvement plan?+**

A performance improvement plan is a formal written document identifying specific performance gaps, measurable goals, a timeline, and what happens if targets are met or missed (HR Cloud, 2026), and they're generally designed to run 30, 60 or 90 days (Eddy, undated). Read it for the measurable part: if a goal isn't countable, ask in writing what number or behaviour counts as meeting it. Then schedule a weekly fifteen-minute review against those items for the length of the plan.

**What if I'm still in a probationary period?+**

A probationary period is a set window starting on your hire date, during which an at-will employer can end employment at any time without cause (Indeed, undated), and many employers use 90 days from your first day (Indeed, undated). In 49 states employment is presumed at-will absent an agreement saying otherwise; Montana is the exception once an employee has passed probation, and Montana caps probation at 18 months (Cornell Law School Legal Information Institute, undated; Indeed, undated). Check your offer letter and employee handbook for what your employer actually committed to, and your state labor agency for state rules.

**What if the mistake involved reporting something unsafe or unlawful?+**

An at-will employer may end employment for poor performance or cost reasons, but not for discriminatory reasons or in retaliation for reporting harassment or exercising a legal right (Cornell Law School Legal Information Institute, undated), and most states also recognise a public policy exception covering firings that violate state public policy or a state or federal statute (LawInfo, undated). Keep your own dated written record of what you reported and to whom. If you think you're being treated badly for reporting, contact your state labor agency or an employment attorney — a guide can't assess your situation.

**What if the same kind of mistake happens again after I've addressed it?+**

Stop defending the check you added and treat it as evidence the check doesn't fit the work. Within 24 hours, go back to your manager with the specific failure point: "The read-back works when I enter orders in the morning, but not when three come in at once at five o'clock — can we move that batch to the next day?" Asking to change the process is a stronger position than promising more care.

**What if HR gets involved?+**

Answer factually and keep your own dated notes of every meeting, including who was there and what was agreed. Ask one question at the end: "Is this a documented step, and will I get a copy?" Knowing whether a conversation is informal coaching or part of a written record tells you how much structure to put around the next 30 days.
