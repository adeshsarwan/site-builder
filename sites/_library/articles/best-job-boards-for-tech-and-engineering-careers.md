No single job board covers the tech market. Dice leans toward contract and enterprise engineering roles, Wellfound toward startups, Built In toward city-specific tech scenes, Hired toward a curated matching model, LinkedIn toward everything plus the recruiters behind it, and Teal isn't a board at all — it's where you keep track of what you found elsewhere.

This guide compares what each platform is genuinely good at, what it costs you as a job seeker versus what it costs the employers posting there, and where each one falls short. Most people end up using two boards and one tracker.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Use Dice for contract, defense and enterprise engineering roles — it's free for tech professionals to use as job seekers (Dice, undated).
> - Use Wellfound when you want early-stage startup roles, since its free employer tier means small teams post there that never reach bigger boards (Recooty, undated).
> - Use Teal's Chrome extension to bookmark roles from more than 50 job boards in one click and keep every application in one tracker (ToolRadar, 2026-06).

## Detailed Breakdown

### Dice

Dice is a tech-only board, which is its whole argument. Searching "engineer" there returns engineering roles rather than sales engineers and engineering managers at insurance firms. It is free for tech professionals to use as job seekers (Dice, undated), and the company reports 44,000 new job seekers per month against 7.8 million registered tech professionals (Dice, undated) — platform-reported figures, so treat them as marketing numbers rather than audited ones.

Employers pay per job slot: a single slot runs $399 for 30 days, and the Starter plan includes 3 job slots per month with 250 candidate views per month (Dice, undated). That matters to you for one reason — candidate views are a metered resource, so recruiters there are searching profiles deliberately rather than browsing. Fill out your Dice profile with specific stack keywords (languages, cloud platforms, frameworks, clearance status if you hold one) because that profile is the searchable object.

Dice also offers premium job-seeker features such as increased profile visibility and advanced search filters starting at a monthly subscription price (Scale Jobs, undated). Any claim that paying lifts you in recruiter search results is platform-reported; there is no independent measure of how much difference it makes. Try the free tier for a month first and see whether recruiters contact you at all.

**The real limitation:** staffing agencies post heavily on Dice. You will see one client role advertised by four intermediaries with different titles. Cross-check the job description text against the others before applying twice.

### Wellfound

Wellfound is where startups hire. Its free Access plan lets employers post jobs, review applicants, create branded company profiles, and use built-in ATS tracking (Recooty, undated), and that free tier includes unlimited job posts with no credit card required (Pin, 2026-07). The consequence for you: two-person companies that would never pay $399 for a slot still post there. Pricing spans five tiers up to a Recruit Pro plan according to the official pricing page (Pin, 2026-07), with an Essentials plan adding custom screening questions, applicant filters and response templates (Recooty, undated).

There's also a Promoted Jobs add-on that prioritizes listings for greater visibility starting at a set price (Recooty, undated). So listing order on Wellfound is partly paid. A role near the top isn't necessarily newer or better — it may just be promoted.

**The real limitation:** if you want a bank, a hospital system, a federal contractor or a Fortune 500 platform team, Wellfound is the wrong board. Use it for seed-to-Series-C companies and little else.

### Built In

Built In describes its platform as including personalized job alerts, employer profiles that highlight tech stacks and benefits, and career development resources (Built In, undated). A comparison site describes it as best for tech roles at startups and mid-size companies and for understanding company culture before applying (Skillora, undated).

The tech stack detail on employer profiles is the practical win. Before you apply, you can see whether a company runs the stack you want to keep working in — and you can pull their listed technologies into your resume's skills section where they genuinely match your experience.

**The real limitation:** Built In organizes around metro tech markets. If you're searching outside those hubs, or filtering to fully remote only, the volume drops sharply compared with a national board.

### Hired

Hired inverts the usual flow: you build a profile, and companies send interview requests. It is described as completely free to use for job seekers with no hidden charges (Underdog.io, undated), because employers carry the cost — Hired's Basic employer plan starts at a stated monthly price (SaaSworthy, undated) and it charges a placement fee typically set at a percentage of the hired candidate's base salary (Flexiple, undated).

Understand what that fee structure does to the role mix. When a company pays a percentage of base salary on a successful hire, it uses the channel for roles worth that fee — generally mid-level and above, at funded companies. Entry-level and support-adjacent roles are rarer.

**The real limitation:** it's a curated marketplace. Profiles are screened for acceptance, and acceptance isn't guaranteed. Treat it as a passive channel running in the background, not your primary search.

### LinkedIn

LinkedIn's value for tech job seekers is less the job listings and more the people attached to them. Premium Career, the plan marketed to job seekers, is priced at a stated monthly rate (Salesbread, undated), costs a stated amount when billed annually (BotDog, undated), and includes 5 InMail credits per month along with LinkedIn Learning access and applicant insights (Salesbread, undated).

Five InMails a month is a constraint that forces discipline. Spend them on engineering managers and recruiters at companies where you've already applied — not on cold outreach at scale. Something like:

> InMail to a hiring manager after applying — personalize before sending
> 
> Hi [Name] — I applied for the [exact job title] role on [date] and saw you lead the [team name] team. I spent the last [number] years on [specific system or problem that matches their stack], including [one concrete result with a number you can defend]. Happy to send more detail if it's useful.

Rewrite that in your own words and replace every bracket with specifics from the posting and from their engineering blog or team page. A visibly templated InMail reads worse than no InMail.

Claims that applying within a certain window of a posting improves your odds are widely repeated and usually anecdotal or platform-reported — not established fact. Applying promptly is sensible; treating a specific hour threshold as a rule isn't.

**The real limitation:** applicant volume per posting is high, and a large share of listings are aggregated or reposted from company sites. Use LinkedIn to identify the opening and the people, then apply on the employer's own careers page where that option exists.

### Teal

Teal is a tracker and resume tool, not a board. Its Chrome extension was named one of the Web Store's Favorites of 2023 for one-click job bookmarking from more than 50 job boards (ToolRadar, 2026-06), which means you can save roles from Dice, Wellfound, Built In and LinkedIn into one list as you browse.

The official pricing page lists Teal+ at $13 every 7 days, $29 every 30 days, or $79 every 90 days, with a Free Forever plan offering unlimited resumes and unlimited job tracking (Teal, undated). Unlimited tracking on the free plan covers the core job most people need it for.

Use it to tailor concretely: match the job title on your resume to the posting's exact title where it's honest to do so, pull the stack and tooling keywords the description repeats, and make sure each bullet carries a number you can explain in an interview.

**The real limitation:** it surfaces no jobs. If your pipeline is empty, Teal does nothing for you until you've found roles on an actual board.

## Where to Access Each Tool

- **Dice** — the job seeker registration page for a free account, and the employer pricing page if you want to see current slot and plan costs.
- **Wellfound** — the job seeker sign-up page; check the official pricing page for the current tier list, since the employer side determines who posts there.
- **Built In** — the national homepage, then switch to your city's market page before setting up job alerts.
- **Hired** — the job seeker profile creation flow, not the employer or "for companies" section.
- **LinkedIn** — the Premium Career plan comparison page inside your account settings, where current rates and the free trial terms are shown for your region.
- **Teal** — the official pricing page for current Teal+ and Free Forever terms, and the Chrome Web Store extension listing to install the job bookmarking tool.

Prices and plan contents change, and some tiers differ by country. Check the platform's own pricing page rather than a third-party summary before you pay for anything.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I pay for LinkedIn Premium Career or Teal+ if I can only afford one?+**

They solve different problems. Premium Career gives you 5 InMail credits a month plus applicant insights (Salesbread, undated), which helps if your bottleneck is reaching people inside companies. Teal+ sits on top of a Free Forever plan that already includes unlimited resumes and unlimited job tracking (Teal, undated), so if your bottleneck is organization, you may not need to pay at all. Diagnose which problem you actually have over two weeks of searching, then pay for that one.

**Is it worth using Dice and Wellfound at the same time?+**

Yes, if your target range spans both company types — their listings barely overlap. Dice skews to enterprise, contract and agency-represented roles, while Wellfound's free employer tier (Recooty, undated) pulls in small startups that wouldn't pay for a slot elsewhere. If you already know you want one or the other, running both just adds noise.

**Do I need a paid Dice subscription to get noticed by recruiters?+**

Dice is free for tech professionals as job seekers (Dice, undated), and premium features like increased profile visibility start at a monthly price (Scale Jobs, undated). The visibility benefit is platform-reported, not independently verified, so run the free profile for a month with full stack keywords filled in and count the inbound messages. If you get none and your profile is genuinely complete, then test the paid tier for a single month.

**How many of these should I use at once?+**

Two boards and one tracker is a workable ceiling for most people. Pick boards that match your target company size — one broad or enterprise-leaning, one startup-leaning — and route everything into a single tracker so you can see what stage each application is at. More boards mostly means seeing the same aggregated listings repeatedly.

**Is Hired worth setting up if I'm already applying actively?+**

It costs you nothing as a job seeker (Underdog.io, undated) and runs passively once your profile is live, so it's low-downside. But because employers pay a placement fee based on a percentage of base salary (Flexiple, undated), the roles skew mid-level and above at funded companies, and acceptance into the marketplace isn't automatic. Set it up and forget it — don't let it replace active applications.
