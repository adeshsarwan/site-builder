A career change cover letter has one job: explain, in a page or less, why you are moving fields and why the skills you already have transfer to the role in front of you. It does the connecting work your resume can't, because your resume lists job titles from an industry the reader may not know.

This guide walks through what to collect first, the order to draft in, and two short template excerpts you can adapt. Everything here assumes you are applying to a posted opening in the US; norms differ by employer, by state and by country, so check the posting's own instructions before you follow any general rule.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Name the career change in the first two paragraphs and frame it as a deliberate move, not an apology for missing experience.
> - Biggest mistake: restating your resume. Use the letter to connect past results to the new role's requirements.
> - Quick win: lift three exact phrases from the job posting and answer each with a specific result you produced.

## What to Gather Before You Start

Open a blank document and paste all of this in before you write a sentence. Drafting is much faster when you are arranging material rather than hunting for it.

- **The full job posting, copied as plain text.** You will mine it for the exact job title, the listed requirements, and the employer's own wording.
- **The exact target job title and any requisition or job ID number.** Use the employer's wording, not your interpretation of it.
- **The company name, spelled and punctuated as the employer writes it**, plus one specific, verifiable detail about the organization — a product line, a service area, a recently announced program mentioned on its own site.
- **The hiring manager's or recruiter's name and title, if the posting or the company site lists it.** If it doesn't, note the team or department name instead.
- **Three to five achievements from your current or previous field, each with a number attached.** Volume, time saved, error rate, budget size, headcount trained, percentage change. Write them as rough notes now; you will shape them later.
- **A written list of your transferable skills, matched to the posting's requirements.** If you are not sure which of your skills carry over, the O*NET Career Changers Matrix lists, for each occupation code, up to 10 related occupations that use similar skills and experience, so a worker may transfer with minimal additional preparation (O*NET Resource Center (U.S. Department of Labor/Employment and Training Administration), undated). That gives you vocabulary for the new field.
- **Your education and training relevant to the new field, with completion dates** — degrees, certificates, licenses, courses in progress. A career change letter should show the value of your education and training alongside transferable skills (Coursera, undated).
- **The number of years you spent in your previous field**, expressed the way you want it read ("six years in", "a decade of").
- **Your finished resume.** You need it open so you can deliberately avoid repeating it.
- **Your contact block:** name, city and state, phone, professional email address, and the date you will send.

If any achievement on your list has no number, either find the number or replace the achievement. Never estimate a figure you cannot support, and never adjust a job title, date or credential to fit the new field better — rewording a responsibility for clarity is fine, inventing one is not.

## Step-by-Step Instructions

Draft in this order. The opening and the closing are the easiest to write once the middle exists, but writing the opening first gives the letter a direction, so start there and revise it last.

1. **Set the page up before you type.** Use a simple, professional font such as Arial or Helvetica at 10 to 12 points, left-aligned, single-spaced, with one-inch margins, aiming for one page (Indeed, undated). Cover letters should normally be only one page long (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Set this now so you can see the page filling up as you write.
2. **Write the heading and greeting.** A good cover letter includes a heading and greeting, a brief introduction, your highlights, and a closing (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Put your name and contact details at the top, then the date, then the employer's details, then the greeting. Example: `Dear [Hiring Manager Name], ` — or, if no name is published, `Dear [Department Name] Hiring Team,`.
3. **Write the opening paragraph: role, source, hook.** The first paragraph should state the job you are applying for, how you learned of the opening, and one line on what especially interests you about the job or employer (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Example sentence:

> I'm applying for the [Job Title] role ([Req #]) posted on your careers page, and I was drawn to it because [Company Name] handles [specific detail you verified] in-house rather than outsourcing it.

1. **Name the career change in one sentence, immediately after.** Don't make the reader work it out from your job titles. A career change letter should explain why you are changing careers and highlight transferable skills rather than dwelling on the experience you lack (Coursera, undated; Upwork, undated). Example sentence:

> After [number] years in [previous field], I'm moving into [new field] because the part of my work I consistently did best — [transferable skill] — is the core of this role rather than a side task.

1. **Write the qualifications paragraphs.** One paragraph is usually enough for the opening and the closing, while the qualifications step may run to two or more paragraphs, as long as the letter stays within a page (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Take two or three requirements from the posting and answer each with a result. If you use bullets, keep them in the action verb, task, measurable result shape:

- Rebuilt the intake process for [type of request], cutting average turnaround by [X%] across [number] monthly cases.

1. **Write the employer paragraph, without repeating your resume.** This paragraph explains your interest in the employer and should not repeat information already on your resume (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Tie your new training in here. Example sentence:

> I finished [Certificate or Course Name] in [Month Year] specifically to work on [problem the employer works on], and your [team or product] is where that applies most directly.

1. **Close with a plan of action.** Most cover letters explain why you're writing, refer to your qualifications, and close with a suggested plan of action such as a follow-up (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Example sentence:

> I'd welcome the chance to walk through how [transferable skill] transfers to [Job Title]; I'll follow up by [date] if I haven't heard back, and I'm reachable at [phone] or [email].

1. **Cut to length and read it aloud.** A good cover letter can be about 200 to 400 words in just three or four short paragraphs (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Delete any sentence that repeats a resume bullet word for word, and delete apologies for what you haven't done.
2. **Consider the T format if the posting is a strict checklist.** An alternative style lists the requirements from the job posting side-by-side with your qualifications (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). This can suit a career change because it forces a direct match for every requirement. Be aware that two-column layouts and tables are a documented parsing problem for some Applicant Tracking System (ATS) software, so if you submit into an online form, paste a plain single-column version into any text box and keep the T format for email or a PDF attachment.
3. **Do a final accuracy pass.** Check the company name, the job title, the hiring manager's name, your own dates and credentials, and every number you quoted. One wrong company name undoes the rest.

## Example Snippet

Both excerpts below are **templates to adapt, not copy**. The bracketed placeholders are there so the text can't be mistaken for a finished letter — replace every one, and replace the wording around them too, or the reader will recognise the pattern.

### Excerpt 1: opening and pivot (template to adapt, not copy)

> Template to adapt, not copy — career change opening
> 
> Dear [Hiring Manager Name],
> 
> I'm excited to apply for the [Job Title] position at [Company Name], which I found on [where you saw it]. After [number of years] in [current or previous field], I'm transitioning into [new field] to apply my experience in [transferable skill #1], [transferable skill #2], and [transferable skill #3].
> 
> The move isn't a detour. In my last role I spent most of my week on [task that overlaps with the new role], and your posting lists it as the first responsibility. What decided me on [Company Name] specifically was [one verified detail about the organization].

That opening structure follows a published career change template that states the role and company, the length and nature of prior experience, the new field, and three transferable skills (Monster, undated), combined with the advice to state the position, make a connection to why the company interests you, and lead with relevant skills rather than missing experience (Upwork, undated).

### Excerpt 2: qualifications paragraph with evidence (template to adapt, not copy)

> Template to adapt, not copy — qualifications
> 
> Your posting asks for [requirement quoted from the posting]. In [previous field], I [action verb] [specific task], which reduced [metric] by [X%] over [time period] across [number] [cases, accounts, sites]. I also [action verb] [second task], supporting [number] [users, clients, projects] at a time.
> 
> To close the gap between that work and [new field], I completed [Certificate or Program Name] in [Month Year] and have since [concrete practice project, volunteer work or freelance engagement]. I'd rather show you the output than describe it: [one line pointing to a portfolio, sample or reference].

Two cautions. Keep the numbers true — if you can't source a figure, describe the scope instead ("a team of [number]", "a [X]-state territory"). And don't let this paragraph become a paraphrase of your resume; the second paragraph of a cover letter should add your interest and reasoning rather than repeat what the resume already says (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

## Frequently asked questions

**My draft is 600 words. How much do I need to cut?+**

Aim for roughly 200 to 400 words in three or four short paragraphs, on one page (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Unless the job description sets a length, there is generally no required word count, but the letter should focus on the details most relevant to the job (Indeed, undated). Cut the sentences that repeat your resume first, then any background on why you left your old field beyond one clear sentence.

**Should I send a PDF or a Word file?+**

Follow the posting's instructions if it names a format. If it doesn't, PDF preserves your layout for a human reader, while .docx is sometimes requested by recruiters who need to edit or re-save it. If you are pasting into an application form rather than attaching a file, strip out tables, columns, and any text sitting in page headers or footers — those are documented parsing problems for Applicant Tracking System (ATS) software. Name the file with your own name and the role, not "cover letter final v3".

**Do I need an objective statement or a line about salary?+**

No objective statement — the whole letter is already your statement of intent, and repeating it wastes your opening. On pay, say nothing unless the posting asks you to include expectations or a history. If it does ask, answer in one line in the closing paragraph and check current rates for the role in your state before you name anything. Pay rules and disclosure requirements vary by state and by employer, so read the posting carefully.

**There's no name on the posting. Who do I address it to?+**

Check the company's site and the posting itself for a recruiter or team lead. If nothing is published, address the department or team — "Dear [Department Name] Hiring Team" — rather than "To Whom It May Concern," which reads as a mass mailing. Don't guess a name you can't verify.

**Is it acceptable to draft this with an AI tool?+**

It's fine to use AI programs such as ChatGPT or Microsoft Copilot to help write a cover letter, but the draft should then be edited to reflect your own voice, skills, experience, and interest (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). For a career change that editing pass matters more than usual: a generated draft will not know which of your results actually transfer, and it will happily invent a credential you don't hold. Check every fact in the output against your resume.

**Should I explain why I'm leaving my current field in detail?+**

One sentence, forward-looking. Explain what you are moving toward, not what went wrong. A career change letter should explain why you are changing careers, show the value of your education and training, and highlight transferable skills (Coursera, undated) — none of that requires criticising a previous employer or industry. If there's a gap in your dates, state it plainly in a clause and move on.
