Chronological, functional and combination resumes contain mostly the same information. What changes is the order it appears in, and therefore what a recruiter reads in the first few seconds. Chronological leads with your job history, functional leads with grouped skills, and combination puts a skills summary on top of a dated job list.

Most US office and trade roles still expect a chronological layout, so treat the other two as deliberate choices you can justify rather than defaults. This guide compares all three on the same four criteria, then gives you a plain rule for picking one.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Chronological organizes by dates, functional organizes by skill groups, combination puts a skills section above a dated history.
> - Dates are the thing recruiters check first — any format that hides them invites questions you then have to answer.
> - Default to chronological; use combination when your recent titles undersell you; use functional only when dates genuinely cannot carry the story.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

All three formats are compared below on the same four criteria: who they suit, how the sections are ordered, how long they usually run, and how they behave in an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) — the software many employers use to store and search resumes.

| Criterion | Chronological | Functional | Combination (hybrid) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Best for which job seeker | You're staying in the same field or a closely related one, your titles have moved forward or sideways in a way that makes sense, and your employment record has no long unexplained breaks | You're changing fields outright, returning after an extended break, or your work has been short-term, project-based or unpaid in a way that a dated list would fragment | You're pivoting into an adjacent field, your transferable skills matter more than your last job title, or you hold certifications and tools that your titles don't signal |
| Section order | Contact details, short summary, work history newest first with dates and bullets under each role, education, skills, certifications | Contact details, summary, grouped skill headings with achievement bullets under each, then a short list of employers with dates and no bullets, education | Contact details, summary, a skills or core-competencies block, then work history newest first with dates and bullets, education, certifications |
| Typical length | Commonly one page early in your career in most US private-sector roles, two pages once you have roughly a decade of relevant work; academic CVs and US federal applications run longer by their own conventions | Similar page counts, but the skills groupings expand easily, so it drifts past two pages faster than the others unless you cut | Similar page counts to chronological; the skills block costs you several lines, so you usually trim the oldest roles to stay within the same space |
| Compatibility with an ATS | Parses most predictably, because the software is looking for employer, title and date fields in the order it expects | Parses the content, but the dated history is thin, so searches and screens that filter on recency or tenure have less to match on | Parses predictably as long as the skills block is plain text; the dated history underneath gives the software the fields it looks for |

A few points apply equally to all three. Parsing problems are usually caused by formatting choices, not by the format itself: multi-column layouts, text boxes, graphics and charts, and contact details placed in a document's header or footer are the documented trouble spots, because that content can be read out of order or dropped. Any of the three layouts can be built in a single-column, plain-heading, standard-font document, and any of the three can be broken by decorative templates.

Bullets do the same job regardless of layout — action verb, the task, the result — and the result should be a number where you have one:

> Template bullet — adapt with your own numbers
> 
> Rebuilt the [weekly reporting process] for a team of [X], cutting turnaround from [X] days to [X] and removing [X%] of manual data entry.

One honesty rule cuts across all three: the format controls emphasis and order, not facts. Rewording a title for clarity — adding a plain-English equivalent in brackets after an internal title, for example — is fine. Changing dates, inventing a title, or implying a degree you didn't finish is not, and a functional layout does not make an omitted date disappear from a background check or a reference call.

## When to Choose Each

- **Choose chronological if** you're applying for your next role in the field you already work in and your title progression tells a clean story; you're in an industry where tenure and recency are screened on, such as nursing, skilled trades, accounting or public-sector work; or you're early in your career and your internships, part-time roles and first job are the main evidence you have. This is also the right default when a posting or recruiter asks for "work history" or "employment history" without saying more.
- **Choose functional if** you're moving into a different field where none of your past job titles resemble the target role and your evidence sits in projects, coursework or volunteering; you're returning after several years away — caregiving, illness, military transition, study — and a dated list would make the gap the first thing read; or your work has been a long run of short contracts, agency placements or freelance clients that would fill a page with dates and little else. Go in knowing the trade-off: you will be asked for dates at some point in the process, so keep a short dated employer list at the bottom and have a straight answer ready.
- **Choose combination if** you're pivoting into an adjacent field and need recruiters to see transferable skills before they see your current industry; your most recent title understates what you actually did, such as a generic "coordinator" title covering budget or vendor work; or you hold tools, licences or certifications — software, equipment, languages, a clearance — that a hiring team filters on and that your titles would never reveal. Keep the skills block to a handful of grouped lines, so the dated history still starts high on page one.

If you're genuinely torn, pick combination. It gives you the emphasis a functional resume offers while keeping the dated structure recruiters and parsing software expect.

## Frequently asked questions

**I've been sending a functional resume and hearing nothing back. Should I switch?+**

Try a combination version before you conclude the format was the problem. Keep your strongest skill groupings as a short block near the top, then rebuild a dated work history underneath with two or three result-focused bullets per role. That keeps the emphasis you wanted while giving a human reader — and an ATS field search — the dates and employers they look for. Change one variable at a time so you can tell what made the difference, and remember that silence usually has several causes, including volume of applicants and how closely your experience matches the posting.

**Can I use different formats for different applications?+**

Yes, and it's often sensible. You might send a chronological resume for roles inside your current field and a combination version for pivot applications. Keep one master document with everything in it, then cut down from it each time. The facts — titles, employers, dates, qualifications — must be identical across every version; only the order, emphasis and level of detail should change.

**Does a career gap mean I have to use a functional resume?+**

No. A short gap, or one with a straightforward explanation, usually sits fine in a chronological layout — you can list the period with a brief plain-language line such as a caregiving break or full-time study. Functional layouts are worth considering when the gap is long enough that a dated list would read as a blank, or when your relevant evidence sits outside paid employment. Either way, you'll be asked about it in a screening call, so decide what you want to say before you decide how to format it.

**How do I turn a chronological resume into a combination one?+**

Work from the posting. List the five or six capabilities it emphasizes, check which ones you can evidence, and group them into three or four labelled lines near the top. Then go through your existing bullets and move the strongest proof of those capabilities up into the most recent roles, trimming or deleting older detail to make space. Keep the whole thing single-column and in plain text, and avoid text boxes or sidebars for the new skills block.
