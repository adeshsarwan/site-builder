A resume is a short, targeted summary of the experience that matters for one job. A CV (curriculum vitae) is a full record of your academic and research work, and in the US it is mostly used for faculty, postdoc, fellowship and grant applications. Picking the wrong one usually means sending too much detail to an employer who wanted two pages, or too little to a search committee that wanted your whole publication list.

There is a third complication: in the UK, Ireland, much of Europe, Australia and many other countries, "CV" is simply the everyday word for the document a US employer would call a resume. The same request can mean two different things depending on where the job sits.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - A resume condenses your skills, qualifications and experience for one specific role; a CV lays out your full academic and research history (UC Davis Career Center, undated).
> - A CV has no fixed page limit and leads with publications and funding; a resume is tailored and trimmed for each application (CASRAI, undated).
> - Rule of thumb: send a CV for faculty, postdoc, research, fellowship and grant applications, and a resume for almost everything else (Indeed, undated) — unless the employer is outside the US, where "CV" usually just means resume.

Two things vary more than any guide can pin down. First, the employer: a university may ask for a resume for an administrative role and a CV for a faculty role in the same week. Second, the country: a request for a "CV" from a London employer and from a US search committee are asking for different documents. When the posting is ambiguous, write to the contact listed and ask which they want.

## When to Choose Each

- **Choose a resume if** you are applying to a company, non-profit or government agency; you are applying for a staff or administrative post at a college or university rather than a faculty post; or you are moving out of academia into industry and need to translate research work into skills an employer can use. For most jobs, including business roles and staff positions within academia, employers are likely looking for this shorter document (Indeed, undated). Tailor it for each posting and cut anything that does not support the role.
- **Choose a CV if** you are applying for a tenure-track or non-tenure-track faculty position; a postdoc or a research position at a national laboratory or research institution; or a fellowship, scholarship, award or research grant (Princeton University Center for Career Development, undated). Also choose it when a graduate program or a professional licensing body asks for your complete record. Keep publications and funding prominent (CASRAI, undated) and update it as a living document rather than rebuilding it each time.
- **Choose a "CV" in the non-US sense if** the employer is based outside the US and uses that word in the posting; you are applying through an international branch of a US organization that follows local hiring conventions; or a recruiter in the UK, Ireland, Europe or Australia asks for your CV. In practice, send a resume-style document of resume length, formatted to local norms — not your academic CV, unless the role is itself academic.

If you genuinely cannot tell which is wanted, default to the shorter document and offer the longer one: "I've attached a two-page summary and can send my full CV with publications if that's more useful."

## Frequently asked questions

**A job ad says "send your CV" but the role is a normal office job. What do I send?+**

Look at where the employer is. A US employer advertising a non-academic office role almost always means a resume — for most jobs, including business roles and staff positions within academia, employers are likely seeking the shorter resume-style document (Indeed, undated). An employer in the UK, Ireland, Europe or Australia using "CV" also means a short, targeted document, not an academic record. Send a tailored one- or two-page resume. If the posting asks for anything extra, such as references or a personal statement, add it as a separate section or file.

**How do I turn my academic CV into a resume?+**

Start from the job posting and work backwards. Keep the roles, skills and projects that support that job; cut the conference list, the full publication list and anything the employer has no use for. Then rewrite entries so each one shows what you did and what changed, rather than what your title was. > **Template — adapt with your own facts, never invent numbers** > Designed and taught [N] lab sections for [X] undergraduates per term, cutting assignment turnaround time by [X%] through a revised grading workflow. Keep the layout single-column and plain, since a reverse-chronological resume parses most reliably through an Applicant Tracking System (CareerOneStop, undated).

**Can I keep both documents going at once?+**

Yes, and if you are applying to both academic and non-academic roles you should. Treat the CV as your master record — it is a living document with no length limit (Princeton University Center for Career Development, undated) — and update it whenever something is published, funded or awarded. Then build each resume from it by selecting and rewriting, so you are never starting from a blank page. Everything in both documents must be accurate: condensing and rephrasing is fine, changing titles, dates or credentials is not.

**Is a skills-based layout a safe middle ground when I'm switching between the two?+**

Be careful. A functional resume organized by skill rather than chronology may highlight transferable skills well, but it is likely to be rejected by an Applicant Tracking System (CareerOneStop, undated), and ATS use has become common in US hiring (CareerOneStop, undated). A better compromise is a reverse-chronological resume with a short skills summary near the top. On the CV side, keep reverse chronological order within each section (Princeton University Center for Career Development, undated) so a reader can follow your record.
