Starting a job you can't walk into means nobody sees you struggling, and nobody sees you succeeding either. This roadmap gives you a checklist for the week before you start, hour-by-hour goals for day one and week one, measurable benchmarks a manager can verify at 30, 60 and 90 days, and word-for-word scripts for the conversations most new hires avoid.

It is built for both fully remote and hybrid roles. Where the two differ — office days, equipment, paperwork handoffs — the difference is called out. Details vary by employer and by state, so treat your offer letter and your HR portal as the final word on anything specific to you.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - In the week before day one, complete your tax withholding form and gather the ID documents your employer's I-9 instructions list, so paperwork doesn't eat your first morning.
> - By the end of week one, book recurring 1:1s with your manager and 20-minute intro calls with five teammates.
> - By day 30, ship one small deliverable end to end and write down the process you used, so your progress is visible without anyone watching you work.

## Before You Start: Pre-Day-One Checklist

Work through these in the week before your start date. Most take under 20 minutes. The goal is that nothing on day one is a logistics problem.

- Join a test video call with yourself on whatever platform the team uses, and check that your camera, microphone and headset all work without a prompt to install anything.
- Confirm what hardware the employer is shipping, when it arrives, and whether you are expected to use your own device until it does.
- Gather the identity and work-authorization documents listed in your employer's Form I-9 instructions and keep them together in one envelope or folder. Your employer must complete and sign Section 2 of Form I-9 within three business days of your first day of work for pay (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, undated), so delays on your side hold up theirs.
- Complete your Form W-4 if the employer's portal has it open before day one. All new employees first paid after 2019 are required to use the redesigned Form W-4, and an employer who doesn't receive one must withhold as if you were a single filer with no other adjustments (Internal Revenue Service, 14 September 2026).
- Submit your direct deposit details and note the benefits enrollment deadline on your calendar — enrollment windows are often short and employer-specific.
- Email your manager or HR contact to confirm your start time, the time zone it refers to, the agenda for day one, and a phone number to call if your login fails.
- If the role is hybrid, confirm which days you're expected on site, how you get a badge, and where you park or which transit stop you use. Do the commute once at the same hour you'd travel, if you can.
- Set up a work spot with a door or a wall behind you, a light source in front of your face rather than behind, and your laptop raised so the camera is at eye level.
- Set up a backup connection — usually your phone's hotspot — and test that your laptop actually connects to it.
- Write and say out loud a 60-second introduction: your name, what you were doing before, what you've been hired to do, one thing you're looking forward to learning.
- Write a list of 10 things you'd otherwise guess at: response-time expectations, whether cameras are on by default, who approves expenses, how urgent requests are flagged.
- Tell anyone you live with your working hours and when you'll be on camera, before the first week rather than during it.

## Day 1 to Week 1

Remote and hybrid starts fail quietly. Only about 1 in 10 employees strongly agree their organization does a good job of onboarding new hires (Gallup, January 2019), so assume some of the structure is yours to build.

**First morning, before your first meeting**

- Log in 20 minutes early and confirm email, chat, calendar and the video platform all open. Message your HR or IT contact immediately if any one of them doesn't.
- Set your profile photo, full name, pronouns if you use them, job title and time zone in the chat tool. In a distributed team, your time zone stops people scheduling 7 a.m. calls for you.
- Hand over your I-9 documents in whatever way HR has specified — in person, or on a video call with a representative.

**End of day 1**

- Confirm your payroll and tax forms are submitted and showing as received in the portal.
- Write down the names and roles of everyone you spoke to, plus one detail about what each person owns. Keep this in one document you'll add to all week.
- Send your manager three lines at the end of the day: what you set up, what you read, what you're blocked on.

**End of day 3**

- Have a recurring 1:1 on the calendar with your manager — weekly or fortnightly, with a start date, not a vague promise.
- Know the answer to three specific questions: how quickly people are expected to reply to chat messages, what counts as urgent, and which decisions need your manager's sign-off.
- Complete any required compliance or security training the employer has assigned, rather than letting it sit until week four.

**End of week 1**

- Hold 20-minute introduction calls with five people, at least two of them outside your immediate team. Ask each one: what do you work on, what do you need from someone in my role, and what do new people here usually get wrong?
- Name the three things your manager will judge your first 90 days on, in your manager's own words, written down.
- Finish one small, real task — a document edited, a ticket closed, a dataset checked — so you've been through the team's review and approval process once while the stakes are low.
- If the job is hybrid, go in on at least one office day and use it for the conversations that are awkward on video: shadowing, questions you'd feel silly typing, lunch with your team.

## 30/60/90 Day Benchmarks

Bring this to your first 1:1 and ask your manager to correct it. Written benchmarks are what replace being seen at a desk — and in March 2026, 22.6 percent of workers teleworked or worked at home for pay, a rate that stayed between 21.5 and 23.0 percent over the prior year (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 16 April 2026), so your manager is likely managing several people this way.

**Day 30**

- Ship one small deliverable end to end — written, reviewed, approved, released — without your manager chasing you for it.
- Document one process you had to learn the hard way, in a page your team can actually open, and post the link in the team channel.
- Hold a 30-minute feedback conversation with your manager where you ask what to keep doing and what to change, and write the answers down.

**Day 60**

- Own a recurring piece of work — a weekly report, a support rotation, a standing meeting you run — that was previously someone else's.
- Close out at least one task that required you to coordinate with a person outside your team, so your manager can see you navigating the organization rather than only your own queue.
- Raise a problem with a proposed fix attached, in writing, rather than only flagging it.

**Day 90**

- Deliver a project your manager assigned at the start of the quarter, on the date agreed, with any slippage flagged at least a week before the deadline.
- Give a short walkthrough — 10 minutes on a call or a written summary — of what you've shipped, what you've learned, and what you want to take on next.
- Agree written goals for the following quarter with your manager, including how each one will be measured.

Treat 90 days as the end of the beginning, not the finish line. Gallup found it takes 12 or more months for most people to get fully up to speed in a new job (Gallup, January 2019), so keep asking for feedback past the point where the company's formal onboarding stops.

## Scripts for Common Onboarding Conversations

Use these as written, or change the nouns. All of them work on a video call, and the last three work as chat messages too.

> Introducing yourself to the team on your first call
> 
> "Hi everyone, I'm Priya. I joined on Monday as a support analyst. Before this I spent four years doing customer support at a software company, mostly billing issues. For my first month I'm focused on learning your ticket system and shadowing calls. I'd love 20 minutes with each of you this week — I'll send times. If I ask you something obvious, that's why."

> Asking your manager for clearer priorities in week one
> 
> "I've got five things on my list and I'm not sure which matters most this week. Could we spend ten minutes ranking them? If I had to drop one, which one should it be?"

> Asking a busy teammate for help without apologizing three times
> 
> "I'm stuck on the refund report — I've tried pulling it from the dashboard and the numbers don't match the invoice totals. Do you have 15 minutes tomorrow to look at it with me? If not, is there someone else who'd know?"

> Requesting feedback at day 30
> 
> "I've been here a month. Can I ask two things? First, what's one thing I'm doing that you'd like me to keep doing. Second, what's one thing you'd change about how I'm working. I'd rather hear it now than at the six-month mark."

> Flagging that you're behind, before the deadline
> 
> "The migration won't be ready Friday. I hit a permissions issue that took two days to resolve. I can have it done Tuesday, or I can hand you the half that's finished on Friday and the rest Tuesday. Which is more useful to you?"

Compare that last script with the alternative: saying nothing on Friday, then explaining on Monday why it slipped. Same delay, completely different impression — one gave your manager a choice, the other gave them a surprise.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if I forget someone's name on a video call?+**

Say it out loud: "Sorry — remind me of your name, I've met about thirty people this week." Nobody in week one is penalized for this. Then add the person to the names-and-roles document you started on day one, with a note about what they own, and check it for 60 seconds before each meeting.

**How do I show I'm working when nobody can see me?+**

Show output, not activity. Send your manager a short end-of-day note in your first two weeks and a weekly three-line summary after that: what you finished, what's in progress, what's blocked. Post your questions and your finished work in shared team channels rather than only in direct messages, so the work is visible to more than one person.

**My manager keeps cancelling our 1:1. What do I do?+**

Don't let it lapse silently. After the second cancellation, send one message: "No problem on today — can we move it to Thursday? I have three decisions I need your call on." Naming the specific items makes the meeting worth rescheduling. If it's still not happening by week four, ask who else can approve your work in the meantime.

**How many questions is too many in the first week?+**

Batch them. Keep a running list and bring five or six to your 1:1 rather than sending fifteen separate messages across a day. For anything blocking you right now, ask immediately — spending three hours guessing costs the team more than a two-minute answer. Most teams expect heavy questioning for the first 30 days.

**When should I bring up pay or a promotion?+**

Not in the first 90 days unless your offer letter already set a review date. Pay is usually set by a band for your role and location, adjusted at a scheduled review or a promotion cycle, so find out from HR in month one when those cycles fall. For market rates, check your state labor department's wage data and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational wage pages, plus any pay range your state requires in job postings — rules on this vary by state.

**Should I go into the office more than required in a hybrid job?+**

In the first 30 days, yes if it's practical — go on the days your immediate team goes, not the quietest days. Use office days for shadowing, reviews and the questions that are awkward to type. After day 30, drop back to the required pattern and judge by whether your in-person days are producing conversations your video calls aren't.
