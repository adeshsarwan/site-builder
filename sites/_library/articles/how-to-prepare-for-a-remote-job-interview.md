A remote interview is still an interview: the hiring team is judging your judgment, your examples and your fit. What changes is that your internet connection, your lighting and the door behind you are now part of the first impression, and a frozen screen can cost you an answer you practiced for a week.

This guide gives you a countdown from one week out, a checklist of what to have within arm's reach, the question types that come up most often in remote hiring, and a step-by-step routine for the day itself.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Confirm the format a week out — live video and pre-recorded answers need different preparation.
> - Record a practice run and watch it back; it catches filler words and bad camera angles nothing else will.
> - Save the link, password and the interviewer's phone number where you can reach them without searching.
> - On the day, sign in early and look at the camera lens, not the face on your screen.

## Countdown to Interview Day

- **One week before.** Audit your interview space properly — camera framing, sound quality, background and lighting (wfh.team, undated). Then email the recruiter to confirm three things: the platform, the length, and whether it is a live call or a pre-recorded interview where you record answers for staff to review later, a format that has gained broader use (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor, undated).
- **Two to three days before.** Do several practice runs: have someone practice-interview you by phone or video, and record yourself so you can review the playback (CareerOneStop, undated; VidCruiter, undated). Write out six examples in the STAR structure — Situation, Task, Action, Result — and in the Action step describe what *you* did, using "I" rather than "we" (University of Cincinnati, Career & Co-op Support, undated).
- **The night before.** Test your lighting with the room as it will be at the interview hour, and keep the light source behind your computer so it falls on your face (Indeed, undated). Charge every device you might use, and save the meeting password somewhere you can reach in seconds (CareerOneStop, undated). Lay out the full outfit, head to toe, because you may need to stand (The Interview Guys, undated); skip patterned shirts, which distract more readily on video (CareerOneStop, undated).
- **The morning of.** Call a friend roughly 30 minutes beforehand to confirm you can see and hear each other without issues (Novoresume, 2026). Tell anyone else in your home exactly when and where you will be interviewing so they know not to walk in (Indeed, undated).

## What to Bring and Prepare

- **A laptop or desktop with working camera and microphone.** CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated) says most video interviews need a computer with audio and video capability, stable internet and a quiet space, though a phone or tablet may be sufficient.
- **A charged backup device on the same platform.** If your laptop fails, you can rejoin from your phone instead of losing the slot.
- **The interviewer's phone number, asked for at the start.** CareerOneStop (undated) advises getting it so you can continue the conversation if the video drops.
- **The meeting link and password in a file on your desktop.** Saving the password in an easy-to-reach spot minimizes technical glitches (CareerOneStop, undated).
- **A copy of your resume, printed or open in one tab.** CareerOneStop (undated) recommends keeping your resume or brief notes handy; you will be asked about dates and job titles.
- **One page of bullet notes, large type, beside the camera.** Placed there rather than below the screen, so glancing at them doesn't drop your eyes off the lens.
- **The job posting with three requirements highlighted.** You will want to tie at least one STAR example to each of them.
- **A written list of five or six questions for them.** Include how they handle work-from-home policies if that matters to you (CareerOneStop, undated).
- **A window seat or a ring light.** BetterUp (undated) suggests sitting in front of a window for soft light on your face, or using a ring light when that isn't possible.
- **Headphones with a built-in mic.** They stop audio echo and keep the interviewer's voice out of your own microphone.
- **A plain, unpatterned top and a full outfit including shoes.** You should look as professional as you would in person (CareerOneStop, undated), and you may stand to fetch something (The Interview Guys, undated).
- **A single browser window with only your portfolio open.** Close extraneous applications and tabs and mute notifications first (CareerOneStop, undated), which also keeps any screen share clean (Novoresume, 2026).

## Questions You're Likely to Face

These categories come up repeatedly in remote hiring for office and knowledge-work roles. Exact wording varies by employer and by the seniority of the role, and no single question is certain to appear — prepare the category, not the script.

| Question Category | Example Question | What It's Testing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behavioral — delivery | "Tell me about a project you shipped late. What happened?" | Whether you own outcomes; answer in STAR form with "I" in the Action step (University of Cincinnati, undated) |
| Behavioral — conflict | "Describe a disagreement with a colleague in another time zone." | How you resolve friction without a hallway conversation |
| Technical / role-specific | "Walk me through how you'd audit a reporting dashboard you inherited." | Depth of method, and whether you can explain it out loud |
| Remote work habits | "How do you structure a day when nobody sees you start?" | Self-management and whether you have actually worked this way |
| Communication | "When do you write an update instead of calling a meeting?" | Judgment about asynchronous work and written clarity |
| Culture fit | "What kind of manager gets the best work out of you?" | Fit with how this team actually operates, not a personality test |
| Motivation | "Why this role rather than an in-office one?" | Whether you want remote work specifically or any job at all |

For the technical row, expect a follow-up: interviewers often push a second time to see whether the answer was memorized. Have a second, smaller example ready for each of your strongest stories.

## Your Day-Of Routine

1. **Two hours before the call:** get up, eat something, and read your one-page notes once — not fifteen times. Re-reading past this point tends to make answers sound recited.
2. **90 minutes before:** dress completely, shoes included, in the unpatterned outfit you laid out (CareerOneStop, undated; The Interview Guys, undated).
3. **60 minutes before:** reset the room. Close extraneous applications and browser tabs, mute notifications on computer and phone (CareerOneStop, undated), and tell your household when and where you will be interviewing (Indeed, undated).
4. **30 minutes before:** call a friend to confirm audio and video work end to end (Novoresume, 2026). If you wear glasses, adjust the lighting to cut lens glare, and set the camera so you are centered and looking up very slightly (Indeed, undated).
5. **10 minutes before:** sign into the conferencing system as early as it allows, test the connection, check audio and video, and confirm your devices are charged (CareerOneStop, undated). Put your resume and notes within reach.
6. **First two minutes of the call:** ask for the interviewer's phone number in case the connection fails (CareerOneStop, undated). During the settling-in chat, smile and make eye contact through the camera (BetterUp, undated).
7. **Throughout the call:** look at the camera lens rather than the interviewer's image on screen, which is what preserves the appearance of eye contact (CareerOneStop, undated; VidCruiter, undated). If something breaks, mute your audio and video, fix it, and resume with a brief apology (CareerOneStop, undated).

> If your connection drops mid-answer
> 
> You: "Apologies — my video froze for a few seconds. May I pick back up at the part about the migration plan?" Interviewer: "We've only got eight minutes left, so give me the short version and we'll move on." You: "Understood. In short: I moved 40 reports onto the new system over six weeks at Harlow Analytics, and the piece I'd highlight is how I sequenced it so nothing went dark during month-end close."

1. **Final five minutes:** ask two or three of your prepared questions, then ask who else is involved in the decision and when they expect to move to the next stage.
2. **Within 30 minutes of hanging up:** write down the questions you were asked and the answers you fumbled. This is the raw material for the next round.
3. **Within 24 hours:** send a short thank-you email referencing one specific thing from the conversation, and attach anything you promised to send.

## Frequently asked questions

**What do I do if I'm running late or can't get into the meeting?+**

Call or email the interviewer immediately rather than spending five minutes troubleshooting in silence — this is why CareerOneStop (undated) advises getting a phone number up front, and it is worth asking the recruiter for one in advance too. Say when you will be ready, in minutes, and offer to continue by phone. Most schedulers can absorb a short delay; what reads badly is an unexplained empty screen.

**I blanked halfway through an answer. How do I recover?+**

Stop and say so plainly: "I've lost my thread — can I restart that example?" Then go back to the structure. Naming the Situation and Task out loud usually puts the Action and Result back in reach (University of Cincinnati, Career & Co-op Support, undated). A short pause on video feels longer to you than to the interviewer. Filling it with improvisation is more damaging than asking for ten seconds to think.

**How should I dress when the company is fully remote and never mentions a dress code?+**

Default to one step above what you think the team wears, in a solid color. CareerOneStop (undated) notes it is just as important to look professional on video as in person, and that patterned shirts distract more readily on camera. If you genuinely cannot read the culture, ask the recruiter directly when you confirm the logistics — that question is routine and nobody holds it against a candidate.

**They asked my salary expectations and I wasn't ready. What now?+**

Pay for remote roles is usually structured as a base salary, sometimes with a bonus or equity, and it varies sharply by seniority, industry and the location the employer anchors to — some pay by the company's headquarters market, others by yours. Say you'd like to understand the full scope first, and ask for their budgeted range. Before the next round, check current rates for your role level and metro on public salary data, including CareerOneStop's wage tools.
