Interviewers rarely ask "Why should we hire you?" to hear you praise yourself. They ask because they have two or three people who could all probably do the job, and they need a reason to pick one. A strong answer names the employer's problem, attaches your evidence to it, and stops.

Below: what the question is actually screening for, three worked answers for different situations, a template you can fill in with your own facts, and the specific phrases that cost candidates points.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Name the employer's problem first, then your evidence — not your qualities in the abstract.
> - Close with one quantified result; unverifiable self-praise reads as filler.
> - Never answer with what the job would do for you.

## Why Interviewers Ask This

By the time this question arrives, the interviewer usually already believes you could do the job. What they are screening for is risk and differentiation: of the people still in play, who is the safest bet to fix the problem that opened the role?

Published interview guidance describes the same underlying checks. Career Sidekick says employers use the question to gauge how confident you are in your own ability to perform the job, and to hear what sets you apart from other candidates (Career Sidekick, undated). Societe Generale's candidate guidance frames it as a test of whether you understand the employer's real needs, and of your motivation for the role (Societe Generale, undated). Naukri's guidance adds a communication test: can you state your value proposition clearly and succinctly (Naukri, undated)?

The red flags are equally specific. An answer built around what the job would give you reads as self-interest — a Seattle Central College career center handout advises treating the answer as a sales pitch summarizing your accomplishments, not a statement of what you want from the job (Seattle Central College Career Center, undated). A generic answer signals you didn't research the employer.

How much the answer counts varies by employer. Structured, scored interviews — common in US federal, state and local government hiring — rate it against fixed criteria. A small-company founder may treat the same question as a gut check.

## Strong Sample Answers

### Career changer (CAR structure: Challenge, Action, Result)

> Retail management into operations coordination
> 
> "You said late supplier deliveries keep blowing up the build schedule. At Marrow Lane Outfitters I ran stockrooms for six stores, and two vendors missed their windows most weeks. I rebuilt the reorder calendar, set a standing Monday call with both reps, and tracked every miss on a shared sheet the store managers could see. Late deliveries went from around twelve a month to three over a quarter. The product here is different, but chasing suppliers and protecting a schedule is the same work — I've just been doing it without the title."

### Entry-level (Robert Walters' formula)

This one follows the formula in Robert Walters' career guide — "I bring [strengths] that align with [specific job needs], and I'm confident I can [value you'll deliver]" — with the "new to this field, fast learner" framing their guide suggests for candidates without direct experience (Robert Walters, undated).

> First job after a two-year program
> 
> "I bring solid SQL and a habit of documenting what I build, which lines up with the reporting backlog you described, and I'm confident I can take the weekly dashboards off your analysts within a month. I'm new to insurance, but I'm a fast learner and very motivated — for my capstone I picked up Power BI in three weeks and built a claims dashboard for a fictional insurer, Tamarack Mutual. I'd rather be the person who clears the routine work so your senior people stop doing it."

### Senior hire (STAR, with a follow-up)

STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result — is a structured technique employers use to evaluate responses to behavioral and situational questions (Wikipedia, undated).

> Candidate for a support director role
> 
> "When I joined Halden Freight, support was running about 900 tickets a month with a four-day first response, and I had a year before I could ask for headcount. I split the queue by issue type, wrote playbooks for the top eight, and put two people on a triage rotation. First response dropped under eight hours in five months, and the headcount we added later came from a business case rather than a panic. You've described the same backlog as your top risk, so that's where I'd start."

> Interviewer pushback
> 
> "Halden was 200 people. We're 4,000. Why would that scale?"

> Your reply
> 
> "Unchanged, it wouldn't. The triage split scales; the playbooks would need an owner per region. So the first thing I'd ask for is thirty days to check whether your ticket mix looks like the one I just described — if it doesn't, the plan changes."

## Answer Framework You Can Adapt

Indeed's career guide recommends three steps: acknowledge the role's requirements, match your skills to the employer's needs, then give quantifiable achievements (Indeed, undated). Research the employer before you draft anything — CareerOneStop lists researching the employer and job as the first step toward a successful interview (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor, undated), and Novoresume's guidance makes the same point about tailoring your answer (Novoresume, undated).

> Core template
> 
> "From the posting and what you've described today, the priority is [employer's problem, in their words]. I bring [strength one] and [strength two], which line up with [specific requirement from the job ad]. At [company], I [action you personally took] and [quantified result — a number, percentage, or time saved]. I'm confident I can [specific value in your first 90 days]."

U.S. News & World Report lists presenting yourself as a problem solver as one way to answer this question (U.S. News & World Report, undated) — which is what the first and last lines of that template are doing.

> If the honest answer includes a gap
> 
> "I'll be straight with you: I haven't [missing requirement]. What I have done is [closest adjacent experience], and I got up to speed on [tool or process] in [timeframe] by [how you did it]. If that gap decides this, I'd rather know now. What you'd get from day one is [strength that is genuinely scarce]."

> If they push back
> 
> Interviewer: "That's a smaller budget than you'd be handling here."
> 
> You: "It is — about [figure] against [their figure]. The part that transfers is [specific skill, e.g. forecasting against an approval cycle]. The part I'd need help with is [honest answer], and I'd want [named person or process] involved for the first quarter."

Write your version out and say it aloud. CareerOneStop recommends writing responses that highlight past experiences, and practicing them with another person or in front of a mirror (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor, undated). Time it: if you're past 90 seconds and haven't named a result, cut.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Don't open with "I'm a hard worker, I'm passionate, and I'm a people person." Replace each adjective with one dated example that has a number in it.
- Don't say "because I really need this job" or "it would be a great opportunity for me" — the Seattle Central College handout's point is that this answer summarizes your accomplishments, not your wishes (Seattle Central College Career Center, undated).
- Don't claim "I'm the best candidate you'll interview." You haven't met the others. Say "here's what I'd bring to the problem you described" instead.
- Don't say "we" when the question is about your contribution. If the sentence is "we reduced churn," rewrite it as "I wrote the win-back sequence; churn fell from X to Y."
- Don't retell your resume in chronological order from your first job. Pick the one role that maps to this requirement and skip the rest.
- Don't name a result you can't source. If asked "where does that number come from?", you need an answer — a dashboard, a report, a manager who signed it off.
- Don't criticize other applicants or your current employer as your point of difference. Describe what you did, not who was worse.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if I'm asked this again in a final-round interview?+**

Don't repeat the first-round answer word for word — someone in the room has heard it. Use material from the process itself: "Since we spoke, two of your team mentioned the handover between sales and onboarding. That's the gap I'd work on first, and here's what I did about a similar one at [company]." New evidence, same structure.

**What if I'd be stepping down a level and they think I'll leave?+**

Say the quiet part first, because they're already thinking it. Name why you want a smaller scope — relocation, burnout from managing, wanting hands-on work again — then give a concrete commitment: what you'd want to be doing in two years, and whether that's a promotion track or not. Vagueness here reads as a candidate who'll bolt in six months.

**What if I honestly don't think I'm the strongest candidate?+**

You don't have to claim you are. Narrow the ground to where you genuinely win: "On [specific requirement], I'd be surprised if you found someone with more direct experience." Then name what you'd need support on. Interviewers who use scored criteria are rating each requirement separately anyway, so an honest strong answer on three of five can still beat a bluff on all five.
