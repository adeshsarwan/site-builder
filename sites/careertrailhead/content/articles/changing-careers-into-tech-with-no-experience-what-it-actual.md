Moving into a technology job from an unrelated field is a documented route, but it is a project with a timeline, a cost and a failure rate — not a decision you make in a weekend. Most career changers who land a first technical role spend somewhere between 9 and 24 months on it, working around a current job, and the hardest part is almost never learning to code. It is getting an employer to take a first look at someone with no paid technical history.

This roadmap sets out what the federal data actually says about which tech occupations are growing, a step-by-step plan built on publicly available resources, a side-by-side look at which of your existing skills carry over, and a checklist to see whether you can afford to start now.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Realistic timeline: 9-24 months from first study session to first paid technical role, assuming 8-15 hours of study a week alongside existing work.
> - Biggest obstacle: no verifiable paid technical history, which screens you out before skill is ever assessed — portfolio work, apprenticeships and internal moves exist to solve that specific problem.
> - First concrete action: open the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook and the O*NET OnLine profile for three target job titles and compare the stated entry-level education, projected growth and annual openings before you buy any course.

## Why People Make This Switch

Three signals in the federal data explain most of the movement into tech, and one of them is a warning rather than an invitation.

**Volume of openings in software roles.** Employment of software developers, quality assurance analysts and testers is projected to grow 10 percent from 2025 to 2035, much faster than the average for all occupations, with about 106,100 openings projected each year (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). That sits on a base of 1,905,400 jobs in 2025 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). A large occupation with steady replacement demand is a more forgiving target for a career changer than a small one, because hiring happens continuously rather than in rare bursts.

**Entry requirements that are not uniform.** The BLS lists a bachelor's degree in computer and information technology or a related field as the typical requirement for software developers, with some employers preferring a master's degree (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). But O*NET OnLine places software developers in Job Zone Four, where "most of these occupations require a four-year bachelor's degree, but some do not" (O*NET OnLine, U.S. Department of Labor, 2025). For web developers and digital designers, stated educational requirements range from a high school diploma to a bachelor's degree (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). That variation is the gap career changers work in — but note Job Zone Four also expects several years of work-related experience or training, so the degree is not the only hurdle.

**A caution about the traditional on-ramp.** Computer support roles have long been the common entry door. Employment of computer support specialists is now projected to *decline* 3 percent from 2025 to 2035, with about 48,700 openings a year, all expected to come from replacing workers who leave, as organizations implement automated tools such as chatbots for troubleshooting (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). The door is not closed — 903,100 people held these jobs in 2025 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025) — but plan support work as a stepping stone with a next step attached, not a destination.

## Step-by-Step Transition Plan

1. **Pick two target job titles and write down their entry requirements. (1-2 weeks.)** Use the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook and O*NET OnLine, both free. Compare projected growth and annual openings: software developer, QA analyst and tester roles show about 106,100 openings a year, web developer and digital designer roles about 13,600 on a base of 220,100 jobs, and computer support specialists about 48,700 openings all from replacement need (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). Smaller occupations are not worse, but they require a narrower, more geographically flexible search.
2. **Reality-test the work before you spend money. (2-4 weeks.)** Complete a free introductory course or tutorial series in the specific skill — writing code, configuring a machine, building a page — and keep a log of hours. If you cannot hold 6-8 hours a week for a month without external pressure, the longer plan will not survive contact with month five.
3. **Choose one structured credential or curriculum and finish it. (8-16 weeks for an entry-level IT certification; 6-12 months for a full development curriculum.)** For infrastructure and support tracks, CompTIA A+ is an internationally recognized entry-level IT qualification issued by CompTIA and accredited by ANSI since 2008 (Per Scholas, reporting on CompTIA A+). It requires passing two exams, Core 1 (220-1101) and Core 2 (220-1102), each a computer-based test with a 90-minute limit combining multiple-choice, drag-and-drop and performance-based questions (New Horizons, reporting on CompTIA A+). Exam voucher and training prices change and differ by country, so check the current price on CompTIA's own site before committing; if you have military or veteran status, note that CompTIA A+ is listed in the Army COOL database as approved for the DoD 8140/Cyberspace Workforce Program and eligible for GI Bill and promotion points (Office of the Secretary of Defense, Army COOL).
4. **Build three or four finished projects that someone else can use. (3-6 months, overlapping step 3.)** Finished and deployed beats ambitious and abandoned. Put the code or files in a public repository on a free platform such as GitHub, with a plain-English description of the problem each project solves.
5. **Search registered apprenticeships and earn-while-you-learn programs in parallel with your study. (2-6 weeks of searching, then program length as listed.)** CareerOneStop, sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor, lists apprenticeships in industries including information technology and describes them as combining a full-time job with training — "an affordable entry point to a high-paying job without the typical student debt associated with college" (CareerOneStop). CareerOneStop also states that 94 percent of apprentices who complete an apprenticeship stay employed in their job (CareerOneStop). Program length and eligibility are set by each sponsor and stated in the listing.
6. **Try the internal move before the external one. (3-6 months.)** If your current employer has any technical function, ask to take on a reporting, data, testing or systems task inside your existing role. An internal transfer converts "no experience" into "experience at a company that already trusts you," and it is usually the shortest path available.
7. **Apply at volume with tracking, and treat rejection as data. (3-9 months, often running past your study end date.)** Record every application, the job title, the stated education requirement and the outcome. If you get no responses after 40-60 applications, change one variable — target title, location radius, or the top third of your résumé — rather than applying harder.
8. **Keep the credential alive once you have it. (Ongoing, 3-year cycle.)** CompTIA A+ requires 20 Continuing Education Units over a three-year certification period, or re-passing the required exams, to stay current (Office of the Secretary of Defense, Army COOL).

## Readiness Checklist

Answer each one yes or no today. Four or more "no" answers in the money and time rows means your plan needs a different shape, not more willpower.

- I have at least three months of essential living costs saved in an account I can access without penalty.
- I know the dollar cost of my chosen study path, including exam vouchers, and I have that money set aside or scheduled.
- I have blocked 8-15 hours a week in a written calendar for the next 12 weeks, and I have told the people affected.
- I have completed at least 20 hours of free introductory material in the target skill and can describe what the work actually involves.
- I have read the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook entry and the O*NET OnLine profile for my two target job titles, including the stated entry-level education.
- I have checked the current wage data for those job titles on the BLS page for my state or metro area, not a salary figure quoted by a course provider.
- I have one finished project, deployed or demonstrable, that someone other than me can open.
- I have the names and contact details of at least three people currently working in the target role, and I have messaged at least one of them.
- I have searched the CareerOneStop apprenticeship listings for information technology openings within my commuting radius or open to remote applicants.
- I have asked my current manager, in writing, whether any technical or data task inside the business can be added to my role.
- I have set a specific review date 6 months from now and written down the one measurable result I expect by then.
- I have a stated fallback: the job, income or hours I return to if there is no offer by month 18.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will my pay go up or down when I switch?+**

Expect a dip before any rise. First technical roles are paid at entry level regardless of how senior you were in your previous field, and the BLS lists software developer roles as requiring no prior work experience in a related occupation at entry (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025) — which is helpful for getting in and unhelpful for your starting offer. Pay is usually a base salary, sometimes with a bonus or equity at larger employers, and it varies sharply by state, metro area, employer size and whether the role is support, web or software engineering. Check the wage tab of the relevant BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook page and the state-level data for your own location before you accept or reject anything; do not plan around figures quoted in course marketing.

**How long does this realistically take?+**

For a support or infrastructure route built around an entry-level certification such as CompTIA A+ — two exams, each with a 90-minute limit (New Horizons, reporting on CompTIA A+) — 8-16 weeks of study plus 3-6 months of applying is a common span, so roughly 6-12 months. For a software developer or web developer route, 9-24 months is the realistic band: 6-12 months of study and portfolio work, then 3-9 months of applications. An apprenticeship compresses the earning timeline because you are working while training (CareerOneStop), but places are limited and program length is set by the sponsor.

**What are the chances this does not work out?+**

Real, and worth planning for. The honest framing is that most people who drop out do so in months 3-6, before any portfolio exists — not because they failed an interview. Protect against that with the fallback item on the readiness checklist and a fixed 6-month review date. One data point on the apprenticeship route is encouraging: CareerOneStop reports that 94 percent of apprentices who complete an apprenticeship stay employed in their job (CareerOneStop) — note that this measures people who completed, not everyone who started.

**Do I need to go back to college for a degree?+**

It depends on the role. The BLS lists a bachelor's degree in computer and information technology or a related field as typical for software developers, with some employers preferring a master's (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025), and O*NET places the occupation in Job Zone Four, "Considerable Preparation Needed," with an SVP range of 7.0 to less than 8.0, while noting that most but not all occupations in that zone require a four-year degree (O*NET OnLine, U.S. Department of Labor, 2025). Requirements for web developers and digital designers range from a high school diploma to a bachelor's degree (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025), and support specialist candidates may qualify with a high school diploma plus relevant IT certifications (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). Read the posted requirements for the specific roles you want in your own area rather than generalising.

**Is help desk still a sensible first job in tech?+**

As a stepping stone with a planned next move, yes; as a ten-year plan, the data argues against it. Computer support specialist employment is projected to decline 3 percent from 2025 to 2035, with all of the roughly 48,700 annual openings coming from replacing workers who leave, partly because organizations are implementing automated tools such as chatbots for troubleshooting (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). Compare that with software developer, QA analyst and tester roles at 10 percent projected growth and about 106,100 openings a year, and web developer and digital designer roles at 5 percent growth and about 13,600 openings a year, driven by continued expansion of e-commerce and retail online offerings (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). If you start in support, pick a second destination on day one and keep studying for it.

**Can I do this while working full time?+**

Most career changers do, and it is the main reason the timeline stretches to 12-24 months rather than 6. Eight to 15 hours a week is a sustainable range for an adult with a job; 25-plus hours a week is sustainable for a few weeks and then stops. The alternative is an earn-while-you-learn route: CareerOneStop describes apprenticeships as combining a full-time job with training and as an affordable entry point without the typical student debt associated with college (CareerOneStop), which removes the income problem but requires you to win a place first.

**Does the certification expire?+**

Entry-level IT certifications generally need maintaining. CompTIA A+ requires 20 Continuing Education Units over a three-year certification period, or re-passing the required exams, to keep the credential current (Office of the Secretary of Defense, Army COOL). Build that into your plan and your budget rather than treating the credential as a one-time purchase, and confirm current renewal rules and fees with the issuing body, since they change.
