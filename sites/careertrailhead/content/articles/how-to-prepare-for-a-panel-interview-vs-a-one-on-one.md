A panel interview and a one-on-one ask for the same evidence, but they run on different mechanics: how fast the questions come, where you look, what you set up beforehand, and how many thank-you notes you owe afterwards. Most interviews move through four stages — introduction, the employer's questions, your questions, and a closing where you are usually told what happens next (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) — and a panel compresses those stages while adding people.

This guide compares the two formats dimension by dimension, gives you a separate prep checklist for each, and ends with a table you can work through the night before. Details vary by employer, by state agency hiring rules, and by country, so confirm the format and the roster when you accept the invitation.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - A panel runs the same four interview stages on one shared clock, so answers get cut shorter and topics hand off between interviewers; a one-on-one allows longer follow-ups on fewer topics.
> - Eye contact works differently: in a panel you answer the person who asked and then include the rest of the room or gallery; in a one-on-one you hold contact with a single interviewer.
> - Follow-up scales with headcount — a panel means collecting every name and title and writing distinct notes, while a one-on-one needs a single note referencing specifics from that conversation.

## How the Formats Differ

**Pacing**

- Panel: the four stages — introduction, employer questions, your questions, closing (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) — share one clock across several interviewers, so each person gets fewer minutes and topics often hand off mid-thread. Plan answers you can finish in about 90 seconds and expand if asked.
- One-on-one: a single interviewer controls the time, so follow-up questions stack on the same topic and a weak answer can be repaired later in the conversation.

**Body language cues**

- Panel: eye contact is divided. Begin an answer facing the person who asked, then sweep the rest of the group so no one is addressed only by your shoulder. In person, wait to sit until invited (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated), which matters more when several chairs are already taken.
- One-on-one: you hold contact with one person and can read their note-taking pauses. Employer-facing guidance tells interviewers to watch for signals such as a sudden lack of eye contact when a topic comes up (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) — in a one-on-one that is one observer, in a panel it is several.

**Tech setup (video versions)**

- Panel: you need gallery view so you can see everyone, and a name-matching list open off-camera. Multiple participants mean overlapping audio, so pause a beat before answering.
- One-on-one: a simpler single feed. Either way, the baseline is a laptop or desktop with working audio and video, stable internet and a quiet space, with phones or tablets sometimes sufficient (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Employers usually send a conference link, text a number to call, or schedule a time to contact you (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

**Your questions**

- Panel: two to three questions is the usual expectation (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated); direct each one to a named person so the manager, the peer and the HR representative each get something aimed at their angle.
- One-on-one: the same two to three, all to one person, which means they should cover different ground rather than variations on one theme. Employers can read no questions at all as disinterest (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

**Follow-up etiquette**

- Panel: collect every name, title and spelling during introductions, then write separate notes that reference what each person actually asked. If you only have the recruiter's address, send one note and ask that it be forwarded with your thanks named individually.
- One-on-one: one note, sent to that interviewer, referencing a specific exchange and the next step you were told about at the closing (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

## Format-Specific Prep Checklist

**Panel interview**

1. Email the scheduler 24-48 hours ahead and ask for each panellist's name, job title and role in the decision; write them in seating or gallery order on a card.
2. Record a practice run and watch it back for posture, eye contact, head nods and smiles (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated), checking specifically that you look up from the person who asked and include the rest.
3. Prepare one question per panellist — a scope question for the hiring manager, a day-to-day question for the peer, a process question for HR — so your two to three questions (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) are not aimed at one face.
4. For video panels, open the meeting in gallery view, mute notifications, and keep the name card taped beside the webcam rather than in another window.
5. Decide in advance how you will close: one sentence of thanks to the group and a request for each person's email address.

**One-on-one interview**

1. Plan your travel so you arrive 10 to 15 minutes early, go by yourself, bring a notebook and pens, leave coffee, water bottles and backpacks at home or in the car, and turn your phone off (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).
2. For video, test lighting and check the background for open closet doors, laundry piles or stacks of paper (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).
3. Practise answers with another person or in front of a mirror, using free online practice tools or a free mock interview at an American Job Center (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).
4. Prepare one deep example per core requirement in the job posting, because a single interviewer is likely to ask follow-ups on the same story rather than move on.
5. If you lack a private space or equipment, reserve one — a local American Job Center may be able to help, and a local library may have bookable space (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

## Quick Reference

| Prep task | Panel interview | One-on-one interview |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirm the format | Ask for the number of interviewers, their titles and whether questions rotate | Confirm who you are meeting and whether anyone else will join |
| Test audio and video | Join early, set gallery view, check you can hear quiet speakers and that overlapping talk is clear | Check mic level, lighting and background for distractions (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) |
| Timing on the day | Log in or arrive early enough to greet several people before the clock starts; in person, wait to be seated (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) | Arrive 10 to 15 minutes early, alone, with phone off (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) |
| Practice run | Record yourself and check you break eye contact deliberately to include a second and third listener (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) | Record or rehearse with a partner, focusing on handling a follow-up to the same story |
| Note-taking | Write names and roles first, then one keyword per question so you can attribute it later | Take sparse notes on next steps so you keep eye contact |
| Your questions | Two to three, each directed to a named panellist (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated) | Two to three covering different ground with one person |
| Follow-up | Separate notes referencing what each person asked, or one note to the recruiter to forward | One note referencing a specific exchange and the stated next step |

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I handle a panel interview held over video?+**

Join a few minutes early, switch to gallery view, and ask each person to say their name and role in the introductions so you can label them. Pause a full beat before answering to avoid talking over someone. Keep your name card at eye level beside the webcam, and confirm at the closing who to send follow-up notes to.

**The invitation says one-on-one, but two extra people are in the room. What now?+**

Treat it as a panel from that moment. Ask for names and titles before you sit, write them down in seating order, and start each answer with whoever asked before including the others. Redistribute your prepared questions so the new attendees each get one. Afterwards, ask the recruiter for their email addresses to follow up individually.

**I have back-to-back one-on-ones and then a group panel. How should I prepare differently?+**

Prepare depth for the one-on-ones and brevity for the panel. Expect repeated questions across sessions and answer them consistently rather than with new material. Keep a running list of names and what each person asked between sessions. Save one or two questions for the panel so you are not repeating what you already asked privately.
