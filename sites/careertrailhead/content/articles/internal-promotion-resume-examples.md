Applying for a job where you already work is a different writing problem from applying cold. The people reading you may know your work, but they still have to compare you on paper against external applicants, and the file usually travels through the same system and the same screens as everyone else's. The examples below show two ways to structure that document: one for a straight step up on your current team, one for a move into a different department where your titles don't match the posting.

Everything in quotes is a template with bracketed placeholders. Replace every bracket with your own facts — never invent a title, a date or a number you can't support.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Example 1 fits you if you're going up a level on the team you already work on and your recent work is the main evidence — it leads with a dated, chronological history under one employer heading.
> - Example 2 fits you if you're crossing into a different department and your job titles don't match the posting — it leads with a skills block before the work history.
> - The formatting choice that differs most is what sits directly under your name: a summary plus dated roles in Example 1, a 3–4 skill block in Example 2.

## Example Resumes & Cover Letters

Both resumes assume one employer with more than one role inside it. CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) suggests a reader scans a resume for roughly 15–20 seconds, so whatever matters most for the internal role belongs near the top, not on page two.

### Example 1 — Vertical promotion on your current team (3–6 years with the employer, one prior internal move)

> TEMPLATE — adapt every bracketed field before sending; this is not a ready-to-submit document
> 
> **[Your Full Name]** [City, State/Region] | [Phone] | [Work or personal email] | [Employee ID, if the internal form asks for it]
> 
> **Summary** [Current job title] with [X] years at [Current Employer], now responsible for [core area named in the posting]. Delivered [named project or process] across [X] teams. Trained [X] new team members since [Year].
> 
> **Experience** **[Current Employer] — [City, State/Region]** **[Current Title]**, [Month Year]–Present *(promoted from [Previous Title], [Month Year])* - Rebuilt [process or workflow] for [team/function], reducing [cycle time or error rate] by [X%] over [X] months. - Led [number] person working group on [initiative], delivering [outcome] [X] weeks ahead of the planned date. - Took on [responsibility named in the job posting] in [Month Year], covering [X] accounts/sites/cases per [week or month].
> 
> **[Previous Title]**, [Month Year]–[Month Year] - Handled [main task], raising [named metric] from [X] to [X] across [time period]. - Documented [process], cutting onboarding time for new starters by [X%].
> 
> **[Earlier Employer] — [City, State/Region]** **[Title]**, [Year]–[Year] - Managed [task], improving [metric] by [X%].
> 
> **Skills** [Skill from posting] · [System or tool used in the target role] · [Certification or licence, with issuing body and year]
> 
> **Education** [Qualification], [Institution], [Year]

This structure works here because your strongest argument is continuity: the same employer heading with two stacked titles shows the promotion visually, which CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) lists as something to note in the work experience section alongside employer, location, dates and accomplishments. The summary is four lines, inside the 2–4 sentence or 3–5 bullet range that same guidance suggests, so the reader hits your current responsibilities before the fold. Older roles are compressed to one bullet because the decision rests on recent work.

### Example 2 — Lateral or cross-department internal move, where your titles don't match the posting (10+ years, mixed roles)

> TEMPLATE — adapt every bracketed field before sending; this is not a ready-to-submit document
> 
> **[Your Full Name]** [City, State/Region] | [Phone] | [Email]
> 
> **Key Skills for [Exact Job Title from the Internal Posting]** - **[Skill 1 from the posting]:** [where you do it now], covering [X] [units] per [period]. - **[Skill 2 from the posting]:** [system or method], used daily since [Year]. - **[Skill 3 from the posting]:** [example], supporting [X] stakeholders across [X] departments. - **[Credential or licence]:** [issuing body], [Year].
> 
> **Work History** **[Current Employer] — [City, State/Region]** **[Current Title]**, [Month Year]–Present - Coordinated [cross-functional task touching the target department], cutting [handoff delay or rework] by [X%]. - Volunteered for [secondment, committee or cover arrangement] in [Year], producing [deliverable] used by [X] teams.
> 
> **[Earlier Internal Title]**, [Month Year]–[Month Year] - Ran [task], handling [X] [items] per [period] at [X%] accuracy.
> 
> **[Prior Employer] — [City, State/Region]** **[Title]**, [Year]–[Year] - Delivered [project], increasing [metric] by [X%].
> 
> **Education and Training** [Qualification], [Institution], [Year] · [Internal or external course relevant to the target function], [Year]

The skills block goes first because your chronology doesn't make the case on its own — CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) suggests opening with 3–4 major skills for the targeted job title when the work history isn't the strongest selling point, and notes that most employers still prefer a chronological format otherwise. The history stays underneath in reverse order, so nothing is hidden. That same guidance suggests covering relevant jobs from the previous 10–15 years, which is why the pre-[Year] roles are cut rather than listed.

### Example 3 — Short cover letter to send with either resume

> TEMPLATE — adapt every bracketed field before sending; this is not a ready-to-submit document
> 
> Dear [Hiring Manager's Name],
> 
> I'm applying for the [Exact Job Title], [requisition number], posted internally on [Date]. I've been with [Employer] since [Month Year], currently as [Current Title] in [Department].
> 
> The posting asks for [requirement 1] and [requirement 2]. In my current role I [specific example tied to requirement 1], which [result, e.g. reduced [X%] of [metric]]. I also [example tied to requirement 2], working alongside [target department] on [named project].
> 
> I've spoken with [manager's name] about applying, and I'm happy to discuss handover of [current responsibility]. I'm available for interview [general availability].
> 
> Regards, [Your Name] | [Phone] | [Email] | [Employee ID]

Keeping it to four short paragraphs leaves the detail in the resume and uses the letter for the two things an external applicant can't say: that you already know the department, and that you've handled the internal courtesy of telling your manager. Naming the requisition number matters because internal postings are often tracked by number inside an Applicant Tracking System (ATS), which CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) describes as software employers use to receive applications and track your status.

## How to Customize These Examples

Work through these in order before you reuse anything above.

- **Replace the header line of Example 2 — "Key Skills for [Exact Job Title from the Internal Posting]" — with the title exactly as written on the requisition,** not your shorthand for it. Internal job families often have formal titles that differ from what the team calls the role day to day.
- **Rewrite the three skill bullets in Example 2 using words pulled from the posting itself.** CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) suggests scanning 10–15 postings to find the requirements, skills and credentials that come up most, and notes that the specific words you use carry the keyword matching. If only one internal posting exists, scan external postings for the same job family.
- **Fix the stacked title lines in Example 1 — "[Current Title], [Month Year]–Present (promoted from [Previous Title], [Month Year])".** Use the titles and effective dates on your HR record, not approximations. An internal reader can check these, and you must never adjust a date or title to look better.
- **Replace every [X%] and [X] with a figure you can defend in the interview.** If you don't have a number, rewrite the bullet around a volume, a frequency or a scope — "[X] sites", "[X] reports per month" — rather than leaving a vague claim.
- **Trim the Summary in Example 1 to 2–4 sentences or 3–5 bullets** after you've added your own content, per the CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) range. Internal summaries tend to swell because you know too much context.
- **Rename the "Skills" heading at the bottom of Example 1** if the target department uses a different label — "Technical Skills", "Systems", "Licences" — so the section matches the vocabulary of the team reading it.
- **Cut the "[Earlier Employer]" block from Example 1 or Example 2 if it falls outside the previous 10–15 years,** which is the window that same guidance suggests for relevant work history.
- **Delete the "[Employee ID, if the internal form asks for it]" field** unless your internal application form actually requests it; otherwise it clutters the contact line.
- **In Example 3, change "[requisition number]" and "[Date]"** to the real reference, and remove the sentence about speaking with your manager if that conversation hasn't happened yet. Don't write it as fact in advance.
- **Have two people read the final version,** as CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) recommends, ideally one person inside the target department who can tell you whether your wording lands there.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I send a PDF, a Word file, or plain text?+**

Send whatever the internal application form specifies. If it gives you a choice, judge all three on the same two criteria — does the system accept it, and does the layout survive upload. PDF usually preserves the stacked title lines in Example 1; Word usually uploads cleanly and stays editable; plain text (ASCII) loses the bold and bullets but is sometimes required, as CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) notes, when a site or Applicant Tracking System can't read a Word-formatted file. Keep one copy of each version of your template so you're not reformatting under time pressure.

**How do I get Example 2 down to one page?+**

Decide first whether you need to. CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) says two pages are quite acceptable for experienced professionals and that a crowded one-page resume may get passed over. If you still want one page, cut the "[Prior Employer]" block, reduce each older internal role to a single bullet, and move the training line onto the same row as the qualification. Don't shrink margins or type size to the point where the skills block stops being scannable.

**Can I use years only instead of months in the date lines?+**

You can, and CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) notes that years-only is an option if a gap was under a year — but it also warns that some applicant tracking systems look for month or day dates. For an internal move your dates are already on file, so months are usually the safer choice in both templates. Whichever you pick, use the same format in every date line on the document.

**Should I add a quote from my current manager to the resume?+**

Only with permission. CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor, undated guidance) says a testimonial can work in a resume because it's a sales document, but that you should ask first. If you use one, put it as a single indented line under the Summary in Example 1 with the person's name and job title, and tell them you've done it — internally, they're likely to be asked about it.
