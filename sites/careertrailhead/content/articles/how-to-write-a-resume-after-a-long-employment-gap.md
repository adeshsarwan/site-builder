A long break in your work history changes a few decisions on your resume: which format to use, how precisely to date your jobs, whether the gap gets its own line, and which recent activity goes at the top. It does not change the basic job of the document, which is to show an employer what you can do for them now.

This guide takes you through it in drafting order — what to gather, what to write first, and a template excerpt you can adapt. It assumes you are writing truthfully about real dates and real roles, because everything that follows depends on that.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Keep reverse-chronological order where you can: CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor) says most employers prefer it, gap and all.
> - Don't hide dates you cannot hide — some Applicant Tracking System (ATS) software reads months and days (CareerOneStop).
> - Quick win: give the break its own dated entry with one factual label, then list any training, freelance or volunteer work beneath it.

## What to Gather Before You Start

Open a blank document and collect these first. Drafting goes faster when you are arranging facts rather than hunting for them.

- **The target job posting**, copied into a plain text file. You will mine it for the exact job title, the named tools, and the repeated phrases.
- **The target job title**, written out as the employer writes it. If the posting says "Accounts Payable Specialist," that is the title your summary should echo — not "finance professional."
- **An accurate date list for every paid role**: employer name, your job title, start month and year, end month and year. Check payslips, tax records, contracts, pension statements or your own online profile rather than relying on memory.
- **The start and end dates of the break itself**, to the month. ResumeLab treats a break shorter than nine months as not really an employment gap at all, so pin down the actual span before you decide how to handle it.
- **Three to five quantifiable achievements** from past roles — anything you counted, cut, sped up, sold, trained or fixed. A rough number is fine at this stage; you will tidy it later.
- **A written list of what you did during the gap**: caregiving, treatment and recovery, study, relocation, raising children, redundancy and job search, freelance jobs, volunteering, unpaid work for a family business.
- **Certificates, course completions and licences** with their dates, including anything finished during the break and anything that has since expired.
- **A tools and systems list** — software, machinery, platforms, languages — plus a note of which have changed version or been replaced since you last used them.
- **Your old resume and any performance reviews or reference letters** you kept. These are the fastest source of forgotten detail.
- **Current contact details**: phone, email you actually check, city and region, and your online professional profile link if you have one.
- **One or two plain sentences explaining the break**, drafted for your cover letter rather than your resume. CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor) says it is not necessary to explain a gap on the resume itself — the cover letter or the interview is the place for it.
- **Current contact details for two or three referees**, so you are not scrambling for them after the interview.

## Step-by-Step Instructions

Draft in this order. Each step produces something you can see on the page before you move on.

1. **Pick the format.** CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor) names three styles — chronological, functional and combination — and says most employers prefer a chronological resume listing jobs in reverse order, newest first. A functional resume groups skills and accomplishments into categories instead of under job entries; Indeed's career advice lists that as one way to reduce the visual weight of a gap, alongside dropping months or giving the gap its own entry. A combination keeps a reverse-chronological job list but leads with a skills block. Start with combination unless your break is very long and your skills are the stronger story.
2. **Write the header.** Name, city and region, phone, professional email, profile link. Put it in the body of the document, not in the page header — SSA's Choose Work guidance (2024) says text in document headers can fail to parse, and to bold section names instead. Example: `Dana Whitfield — Leeds, UK — 07xxx xxx xxx — dana.whitfield@email.com`.
3. **Write the summary last-but-place-it-first.** Three lines, present tense, aimed at the target title. Example: *"Accounts Payable Specialist with [X] years in high-volume invoice processing, returning to full-time work after a planned career break for caregiving. Recently completed [Certification Name], [Year]."*
4. **Build the experience section in reverse order.** One line per role: title, employer, location, dates. Then two to four bullets each, in the form action verb plus task plus measurable result — for example, *"Reconciled supplier accounts across [X] entities, cutting unmatched invoices by [X%] within two quarters."*
5. **Decide how precise your dates will be.** CareerOneStop says that if the gap was under a year you can list years only rather than months — but also warns that some applicant tracking systems look for month or even day of employment, so year-only dating is not foolproof. For a long break, date everything to the month and let the gap show.
6. **Give the break its own entry if it is long.** Indeed's advice includes listing a long gap as an entry in the experience section. Example: *"Career Break — Full-Time Caregiving, March [Year] – August [Year]. Maintained [Professional Body] membership; completed [Course Name], [Year]."* LinkedIn added a Career Break option to profiles in March 2022, with categories including caregiving and health and well-being, so the label will read as familiar.
7. **Add anything that kept your hands in.** Freelance pieces, volunteering, committee roles, a family business, a short contract. Format them exactly like paid jobs, with honest titles. Example: *"Volunteer Bookkeeper, [Organisation Name], [Year]–[Year]: processed [X] monthly transactions and produced quarterly reports for a committee of [X]."*
8. **Trim the far end.** CareerOneStop says a long work history does not need every job, and that you can leave off a role held before an early-career gap. Cut anything that no longer supports the target title.
9. **Write the skills section from the posting.** Use the employer's wording for tools you genuinely use: *"SAP, Excel (pivot tables, VLOOKUP), Xero, invoice matching, month-end close."* Add education and certifications underneath with their years.
10. **Do the formatting pass.** SSA's Choose Work guidance (2024) recommends body text in an ATS-friendly font such as Calibri, Arial, Helvetica, Georgia or Times New Roman at 10–12 points, and no pictures, tables, columns or text boxes, since those are the elements that commonly break parsing. Indeed suggests line spacing between single and 1.5. That same SSA post cites LinkedIn's figure that almost 99% of Fortune 500 companies use computerised applicant tracking systems, so the plain layout is worth the dull appearance.
11. **Check the dates against your records one last time.** Every month and year on the resume should match what an employer would see on a reference check or a tax record. Rewording a vague old job title for clarity is fine; changing a date is not.
12. **Draft the cover letter line about the break.** One factual sentence and a pivot forward — a report on LinkedIn's 2022 career-break survey (FDM Group) states 51% of employers would likely call back a candidate if they knew the context of the break, and 46% see people returning from breaks as an untapped talent pool.

## Example Snippet

### Resume excerpt — template to adapt, not copy

> Template to adapt, not copy — replace every bracketed placeholder with your own verified details.
> 
> **SUMMARY** [Target Job Title] with [X] years in [industry or function], returning to full-time work after a [length] career break for [caregiving / health / study / relocation]. Completed [Course or Certification Name] in [Year]; experienced with [Tool], [Tool] and [Process].
> 
> **EXPERIENCE** **Career Break — [Caregiving / Health and Well-Being / Full-Time Study]**, [Month Year] – [Month Year] - Completed [Course Name] through [Training Provider], [Year]. - Managed [X] hours per week of scheduling, budgeting and liaison with [X] external services.
> 
> **[Job Title]**, [Company Name], [City] — [Month Year] – [Month Year] - Processed [X] [invoices / orders / cases] per month, reducing average turnaround by [X%] over [X] months. - Trained [X] new team members on [System Name], cutting onboarding time from [X] weeks to [X].

### Cover letter paragraph — template to adapt, not copy

> Template to adapt, not copy — one factual sentence about the break, then forward to the role.
> 
> I took a [length] break from [Month Year] to [Month Year] to [reason, stated plainly and without apology]. During that time I kept my [Professional Body] membership current and completed [Course Name] in [Year]. I am now looking for a full-time [Target Job Title] role, and your posting's focus on [specific requirement from the posting] matches what I did at [Previous Company Name], where I [achievement with [X%] or [X] result].

Keep both excerpts short in your own version. The resume states the break as a fact; the letter supplies the context.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should my resume be now that I have added a career break entry?+**

Indeed's career advice says a resume should be one page in most cases, with some exceptions where two pages are appropriate — typically long technical, academic or senior histories. A break entry is only two or three lines, so it rarely pushes you over. If you are over length, cut the oldest roles before you cut recent achievements.

**Should I send a PDF or a Word file?+**

Follow the application instructions first. Where they are silent, the two sources disagree on the same criterion of machine readability: SSA's Choose Work guidance (2024) says to save as PDF or DOCX and avoid GIF, PNG, JPG or TXT, while Betterteam advises using .doc or .docx unless the posting specifically asks for a PDF, on the grounds that PDFs are not compatible with every applicant tracking system. DOCX satisfies both pieces of advice, so it is the safer default.

**Do I need an objective statement to explain that I am returning to work?+**

No. A short summary aimed at the target job title does the same work and says more about what you offer. If you want one line about returning to full-time work, fold it into the summary rather than adding a separate objective block.

**What if the break has not ended — I have no current job to list?+**

That is the normal situation when you are applying. Date the break entry from its start month to "Present" and list what you are doing now: study, volunteering, a course in progress, job-search-related training. Do not invent a current role or stretch an old end date to cover the period.

**Should my online profile match the dates on my resume?+**

Yes — mismatched dates are the detail that gets noticed. LinkedIn added a Career Break option to the Experience section in March 2022, with categories such as caregiving and health and well-being, so you can record the period there in the same way you have on the resume.

**How do I handle references after several years out?+**

Contact former managers and colleagues before you submit anything, confirm they are willing, and check their current job title and contact details. Do not list references on the resume itself unless the posting asks for them; keep a separate one-page list ready to send.
