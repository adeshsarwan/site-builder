Startup interviews tend to be shorter, less scripted and more founder-led than processes at large employers. You may meet the CEO in round one, be asked to solve a live problem with incomplete information, and hear a decision within days. That rewards candidates who arrive with specific knowledge of the product, clear stories about shipping work with few resources, and sharp questions about runway, roadmap and role scope.

This guide gives you a countdown plan, a bring-and-prepare checklist, the question types that come up most often in early-stage hiring, and an hour-by-hour routine for the day itself. Formats vary by company, stage and country, so confirm the details with your recruiter or hiring contact rather than assuming.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Research first: learn the product well enough to describe who it is for, what it costs to adopt, and one thing you would change.
> - Practice second: rehearse five STAR stories out loud, each with a number you can defend and a mistake you can name.
> - Lock logistics early: confirm format, time zone, interviewer names and whether a take-home or live exercise is part of the loop.
> - On the day, build in buffer time, a named breathing technique before you start, and a follow-up note within 24 hours.

## Countdown to Interview Day

**1 week before**

- Use the product. Sign up for the free tier, run through onboarding, and write down three things that confused you and one feature you would prioritise next. Founders notice candidates who have actually logged in.
- Email your hiring contact to confirm the interview format in writing: who you are meeting, their role, the length of each stage, and whether there is a live exercise, take-home task or portfolio walkthrough.

**2-3 days before**

- Write five STAR stories (Situation, Task, Action, Result) that map to the job ad's top responsibilities. Each needs one defensible number and one thing you would do differently. Say them out loud and time them — aim for 90 seconds to two minutes.
- Run one mock interview with a friend who will interrupt you. Early-stage interviewers often push back mid-answer to see how you handle challenge, so rehearse that rather than a clean monologue:

> Practice exchange: scope and evidence
> 
> **Interviewer:** You said you grew activation at Harrowgate Labs. What did you actually own?
> 
> **You:** I owned the onboarding flow end to end. We were losing people at the integration step, so I cut it from six screens to three and added a skip option. Drop-off at that step went from 38% to 22% over two months.
> 
> **Interviewer:** How do you know that was your change and not seasonality?
> 
> **You:** Fair challenge — we didn't run a clean A/B test because traffic was too low. I compared the same cohort window against the previous quarter and checked the step-level funnel, which only moved where we made changes. With more traffic I'd have held a control group.

**The night before**

- Test the actual tool: join the video link, check camera, mic and screen share; or walk the route to the office at roughly the same time of day you'll travel. If it's an in-person loop, confirm the building entrance and whether you need photo ID at reception.
- Lay out clothes, charge your laptop and phone, and reread the job ad alongside your five stories so the links are fresh. Stop preparing at a set time and get to bed — cramming new material after that point tends to crowd out what you already know.

**The morning of**

- Spend ten minutes re-reading your own notes only: the five stories, your three questions, and the names and roles of your interviewers.
- Before you start, do four rounds of box breathing (inhale four counts, hold four, exhale four, hold four) and read one answer aloud to warm up your voice. Speaking out loud once before the call stops the first answer sounding flat.

## What to Bring and Prepare

| Item | Why it matters here |
| --- | --- |
| Six printed copies of your CV (in-person) or a PDF named `Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf` | Startup panels change at short notice; someone who was added that morning won't have read it. |
| A one-page summary of your five STAR stories, in large text | Glanceable on a desk or second screen when a question catches you cold. |
| Written answers to "why this company" and "why now" | Early-stage founders ask motivation questions in nearly every first-round conversation; vague answers read as mass applying. |
| Three questions about the business: funding stage, current headcount plan, and what success looks like in the role at 90 days | Shows you are assessing risk like someone who intends to stay. |
| Notes from using the product, including one improvement idea | Converts generic interest into evidence you spent time on it. |
| A portfolio, GitHub link or work sample, with one piece you can narrate for five minutes | Small teams hire on demonstrated output more than credentials. |
| Your availability for the next two weeks, plus notice period in writing | Startups often move to the next stage the same day and will ask on the spot. |
| Interviewer names, job titles and the exact meeting link or address | Prevents the five-minute scramble that eats your composure. |
| Photo ID and any building access instructions | Many co-working and shared offices will not let you past reception without it. |
| A notebook and two pens, or a blank doc for a live exercise | Whiteboard and take-home style tasks appear frequently in startup loops across engineering, design and growth roles. |
| Water, a charged phone and a charger or power bank | Back-to-back startup loops can run two to three hours with no scheduled break. |
| A note of your salary research range for this role level, in this city and industry | So you can answer a compensation question with a bounded range rather than a number you regret; check current local market data on national job boards or a government labour statistics site before you go. |

## Questions You're Likely to Face

These categories show up repeatedly in early-stage hiring. Wording varies by interviewer and country, and nothing here is guaranteed to be asked — treat it as coverage, not a script.

| Question Category | Example Question | What It's Testing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behavioural — ownership | "Tell me about something you shipped where nobody told you what to do." | Whether you can operate without a defined brief or manager oversight. |
| Behavioural — failure | "Describe a project that missed its goal. What did you change afterwards?" | Honesty, and whether you extract a transferable lesson rather than blame. |
| Technical / role-specific | "Walk me through how you'd diagnose a sudden 40% drop in weekly active users at a 20-person SaaS company." | Structured thinking out loud under incomplete information. |
| Technical / role-specific | "Show me a piece of your work and explain the trade-offs you made." | Depth of actual contribution, and whether you can defend decisions. |
| Prioritisation under constraint | "You have two engineers and three must-do projects this quarter. What ships?" | Comfort cutting scope, and whether you ask about business impact first. |
| Culture fit — pace and ambiguity | "What do you do when priorities change mid-sprint for the third time?" | Tolerance for instability, and whether you push back constructively. |
| Culture fit — motivation | "Why a seven-person company instead of a large employer?" | Whether you understand the trade-offs you're signing up for. |
| Closing / logistics | "What are you looking for in terms of compensation and equity?" | Whether your expectations fit the stage; answer with a researched range tied to your level, city and sector, and ask how they structure cash versus equity before committing to a number. |

## Your Day-Of Routine

1. **Wake at least three hours before the interview start time.** That gives your voice time to settle and leaves room for a transport problem without panic.
2. **Eat within 45 minutes of waking and finish caffeine at least 90 minutes before you start.** A late coffee tends to arrive as jitter exactly when you need steady hands.
3. **Spend 20 minutes, no more, on review.** Reread only your one-page story sheet, your three questions and the interviewer names. Do not open new research — it rarely lands and it crowds out what you rehearsed.
4. **Get dressed and ready 60 minutes before start time**, then leave your materials in one place by the door or beside your desk so nothing is hunted for later.
5. **For in-person: aim to be in the neighbourhood 30 minutes early and at reception 10 minutes early.** Use the spare 20 minutes in a nearby café, not the lobby, where waiting that long looks awkward. For remote: join the call two to three minutes before the hour.
6. **In the five minutes before you start, do four rounds of box breathing and say one sentence out loud** — the opening of your "why this company" answer works well as a vocal warm-up.
7. **During the interview, write down the exact wording of any question you fumble**, plus each interviewer's name and one thing they said about the roadmap or team plan. You will need both afterwards.
8. **Within the last five minutes, ask your three questions and ask directly about next steps and timing**: who decides, what the following stage involves, and when you should expect to hear.
9. **Within 30 minutes of finishing, spend 10 minutes writing notes while it is fresh** — questions asked, answers you'd improve, and anything you promised to send.
10. **Send a follow-up email within 24 hours**: three or four sentences, one specific thing from the conversation, and any promised attachment. If you fumbled a question, add two tightened sentences on it rather than relitigating the whole answer.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if I'm running late?+**

Message your hiring contact as soon as you know, not when you arrive. Give a realistic new arrival time and a one-line reason without a long apology, then stop explaining. For remote interviews, email and use any chat channel you have, since the interviewer may be sitting on the call. Small teams usually run tight calendars, so offer to shorten your end or rebook rather than assuming the full slot still exists.

**I blanked on a question. How do I recover?+**

Say so plainly and buy structured time: "Let me think about that for a few seconds." Silence of five to ten seconds reads as considered, not lost. If nothing comes, ask a clarifying question — it often unlocks a usable example. If you still have nothing, say you would rather give a real example than a made-up one, and offer to follow up by email. Then actually send it within 24 hours.

**How should I dress when the dress code isn't stated?+**

Ask your hiring contact directly; it is a normal question and the answer removes the guesswork. If you cannot ask, look at team photos on the company's site or social accounts and go one step smarter than what you see. For most startups that means clean, well-fitting casual rather than a suit. Dress codes vary widely by country, sector and whether the role is customer-facing, so verify locally rather than copying advice written for another market.

**The interviewer seemed distracted or cut the interview short. Does that mean it went badly?+**

Not necessarily. At small companies the interviewer is often a founder juggling a customer incident, and short slots are common. Judge the signal by content instead: did they describe the role's first 90 days, mention next steps, or ask about your availability? If the end felt abrupt, send your follow-up note the same day and include the one point you did not get to make.

**Can I ask about runway and funding without sounding negative?+**

Yes, if you frame it around planning rather than doubt. Something like: "How are you thinking about hiring and roadmap over the next 12 months?" usually gets you the substance. A founder who reacts badly to a straightforward question about stage and plans has told you something useful. Ask near the end, after you have shown interest in the work itself, and listen for specifics rather than reassurance.
