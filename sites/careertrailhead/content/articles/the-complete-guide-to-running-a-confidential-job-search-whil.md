Looking for work while you still have a job is the strongest position to search from, and the most fragile. One public resume, one recruiter InMail seen by the wrong colleague, or one 2pm "dentist appointment" too many, and you lose the leverage that made your search safe in the first place.

This guide walks through how to configure the platforms you'll actually use, build a parallel identity for your search, handle references and interview scheduling, and keep records somewhere your employer can't read. It names the specific settings to change — not just the principle behind them.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Set job-board resume visibility before uploading anything: Indeed's default is "Employers can't find you" (Indeed support, undated), and CareerBuilder offers a confidential resume that hides contact details.
> - Use LinkedIn's Open to Work "Recruiters only" option, which shows no green profile banner — but know LinkedIn says it cannot guarantee recruiters at your own company never see it (LinkedIn Help, as cited by Contentin).
> - Run the entire search from a personal email address, personal phone and personal device, with a tracker that lives outside any employer-owned account.

## Why This Strategy Matters

The risk in an employed job search is rarely that someone finds out you're looking. It's that they find out at the worst possible moment — before you have an offer, before you've banked a bonus, before a project you were counting on for your resume is finished. Once your employer knows, you no longer control the timeline. You become the person who is leaving, and decisions about raises, promotions and new work start getting made around you.

The second problem is that job platforms are built for discovery, not discretion, and their defaults differ from each other. Indeed says its default profile setting is "Employers can't find you," meaning the profile is shown only to employers you apply to or send a resume to, and is not publicly searchable (Indeed support, undated). CareerBuilder takes a different approach: its "Hide contact details" option keeps your resume publicly searchable but hides your contact information, letting employers reach you through a confidential CareerBuilder email address that you can simply ignore (CareerBuilder, undated). Monster's "Privacy Plus" lets you block a set number of companies from finding your public resume, something Monster itself describes as useful if you don't want your current employer to see it (Monster, undated). None of that happens automatically. You have to go and set it.

Be sceptical of visibility statistics while you do. One career-advice site claims profiles with Open to Work enabled receive up to 40% more recruiter InMail messages (CareerBldr, undated) — treat that as an unverified vendor-side claim rather than a benchmark, and the same goes for any response-rate or time-to-hire number you see quoted without a named study behind it. No setting, tool or tactic guarantees an interview.

Confidentiality is a process, not a secret. The readers who get caught usually aren't careless about privacy settings; they're careless about the boring operational edges — the work laptop, the reference who mentions it over coffee, the recurring calendar hole at 11am every Thursday.

## Step-by-Step Process

1. **Search for yourself the way a colleague would.** Open a private browsing window, log out of everything, and run your own name against your employer and the word resume — for example, `"Priya Raman" resume "[Current Employer]"` and then `"Priya Raman" CV filetype:pdf`. Write down every old job-board profile, personal site or PDF that surfaces, because those are the things you need to take down or lock before you start applying.
2. **Set resume visibility on every board before you upload a file.** Decide per platform: leave Indeed on "Employers can't find you" so your profile only reaches employers you actually apply to (Indeed support, undated); use CareerBuilder's "Hide contact details" confidential resume if you want to stay searchable without exposing your phone number (CareerBuilder, undated); use Monster's "Privacy Plus" to block named companies from finding your public resume (Monster, undated). Note one trap: Monster says that if you apply using a posted resume, information marked confidential — contact details, current company, references — is included in that application anyway, so the employer can reach you.
3. **Configure LinkedIn's Open to Work as "Recruiters only."** Click the "Open to" button below your profile photo and headline, choose "Finding a new job," fill in target job titles, locations, job types and start date, then set visibility to "Recruiters only" rather than "All LinkedIn Members" (as documented by MagicPost and JobQuest, undated). That option shows no green profile banner and shares your status privately with people using LinkedIn Recruiter.
4. **Create a separate search identity.** Register a personal email in the format `firstname.lastname.career@[your provider]`, add your personal mobile number to it, and use that pair on every application. Standardise your subject lines so nothing routes to a work inbox by accident — for example: `Application — [Job Title], Req [Number] — [Your Name]`.
5. **Build two versions of your resume.** The applied version names your employer and carries metrics; the version you post publicly on any board describes it generically — "regional logistics operator, 400+ staff (name on request)" — and uses the posting's exact job title in your headline plus three keywords lifted from the requirements section, such as "demand planning," "S&OP" and "SQL." Keep both files in personal cloud storage, never on an employer-owned drive.
6. **Recruit two references who don't report to your current employer.** Ask a former manager and a former peer, in writing, with an explicit confidentiality request. Personalise this before sending — do not paste it as written:

> Reference request email
> 
> Subject: Quick confidential favour — reference for [Role Type] roles
> 
> Hi [Name], I'm running a quiet search for [role type] roles and would value your name as a reference for the [specific project you worked on together] work. My current employer doesn't know I'm looking, so I'd be grateful if you kept this between us. Happy to send the job description and a short reminder of what I did on [project] so you're not caught cold. — [Your Name]

1. **Pre-block your interview availability in your work calendar.** Put a recurring private hold from 8:00–9:30am two mornings a week, labelled consistently ("personal appointment") so it reads as routine rather than as a pattern of sudden absences. Then ask employers for those slots first: "I have availability Tuesday or Thursday before 9:30am, or any time after 5:30pm."
2. **Read your own contract and handbook before you talk to a competitor.** Look specifically for notice period, non-solicitation, moonlighting and non-compete clauses, and note which US state or country's law the contract names. The FTC's April 2024 final noncompete rule is not in effect — a district court order on 20 August 2024 stopped the FTC from enforcing it, and the FTC voted to formally vacate the rule on 5 September 2025, returning the question largely to state law and case-by-case enforcement (FTC, 2024–2025; AVMA, September 2025). This is not legal advice; if a clause looks like it bites, pay a qualified employment lawyer in your jurisdiction for an hour.
3. **Set up a tracker outside every employer system.** A personal spreadsheet with columns: Company | Role | Source | Date applied | Contact | Last touch | Next action | Date of next action. Fill the "next action" column with something specific like "follow up with [hiring manager] on 14th if no reply," because an empty next-action cell is how applications die quietly.
4. **Lock in a weekly 60-minute cadence and one outreach message.** For example, Sunday 7–8pm: three tailored applications, two outreach notes, tracker updated. Personalise the note below for each recipient — the bracketed detail is the whole point of it:

> Outreach to someone doing the job you want
> 
> Subject: Question about [specific team] at [Company]
> 
> Hi [Name], I work in [your function] and I'm exploring [role type] moves quietly — I'd appreciate your discretion. I noticed [specific thing: a product launch, a team restructure, a talk they gave]. Would you be open to 15 minutes to tell me what the [team] actually measures success on? Happy to work around your calendar. — [Your Name]

## Before You Start Checklist

- Old job-board and personal-site profiles found in step 1 either deleted or set to a private visibility option.
- Personal email and personal mobile added to every platform profile, with all work contact details removed.
- Applications, drafts and your tracker stored on a personal device and personal cloud account — nothing on an employer laptop, VPN, work phone or work Google/Microsoft account.
- Only reputable, free-to-use job banks on your list; CareerOneStop (sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor) names CareerBuilder, Indeed and ZipRecruiter as examples and notes its own Job Finder combines these with state job banks (CareerOneStop, undated).
- A note to yourself of the scam red flag CareerOneStop names: you should never have to pay to search job openings, and posting your resume should be free (CareerOneStop, undated).
- Public-facing resume version scrubbed of your employer's name, client names and anything confidential.
- Two references confirmed in writing, neither of them anyone who reports to or socialises with your current manager.
- One-line answer rehearsed for "are you looking?", plus a decision made in advance about what you will say if an offer requires a reference from your current employer.
- Contract clauses on notice period, non-solicitation and non-compete read and summarised in your own words, with a note of any clause you want a lawyer to look at.
- Interview-window holds already in your work calendar for the next three weeks, so you never have to invent a reason at short notice.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if a recruiter at my own company sees my "Recruiters only" Open to Work signal?+**

LinkedIn says it takes steps to avoid showing the "Recruiters only" signal to recruiters at your own company, but states it cannot guarantee they will never see it (LinkedIn Help, as cited by Contentin, undated). Treat it as low risk rather than no risk. If your employer has a large in-house talent team, or if you work somewhere that uses agency recruiters heavily, you may prefer to leave the feature off entirely and rely on direct applications and outreach instead.

**My employer asks everyone to list them as a reference. What do I do?+**

Most employers will accept former managers, peers and clients at offer stage and reserve a current-employer check for after you accept, or they will run an employment verification rather than a reference conversation. Say so plainly and early: that your search is confidential and your current employer can be contacted once an offer is signed. If a hiring team refuses that entirely, you are being asked to take all the risk before you have anything in writing, and it is reasonable to decline.

**Should I post my resume publicly at all, or only apply directly?+**

Public posting generates inbound contact you would not otherwise get, which matters if you are in a niche role. The middle path is a searchable but shielded profile — CareerBuilder's "Hide contact details" option keeps your resume findable while routing employers through a confidential CareerBuilder email address you choose whether to answer (CareerBuilder, undated). If you are in a small industry where your resume would be identifiable even without your name on it, apply directly only.

**What if a posting turns out to be from my own employer or a company that shares investors with it?+**

Blind postings and group-owned sister companies are a real hazard, so check the description for internal clues — a named applicant tracking system, familiar job-title conventions, a location that matches an office you know. If you only discover it after applying, withdraw in writing with a neutral line and keep the message short. Do not explain yourself at length; brevity reads as professionalism rather than panic.

**How do I explain the gaps in my day when interviews stack up?+**

Use half-days of annual leave rather than scattered hours, because one booked absence raises fewer questions than three mysterious ones. If your employer requires a reason for leave, give the same mundane answer consistently and do not embellish it. When an onsite is unavoidable, ask the employer whether a first-stage video call can replace it — most will accommodate a candidate who is currently employed, since they deal with it constantly.

**Is it worth telling my manager once I'm close to an offer, so they can counter?+**

Some managers respond to that information by trying to keep you, and some respond by starting to plan without you — you are the only person who can judge which yours is. If you do want a counter-offer conversation, have the written offer in hand first, and be clear with yourself about what would actually make you stay beyond money. Any pay figures you discuss should come from current published salary data for your role and region rather than from guesswork, so check a reputable source before the conversation.
