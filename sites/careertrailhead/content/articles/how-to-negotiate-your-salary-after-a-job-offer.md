Negotiating after an offer is mostly preparation plus two or three sentences said calmly. You need a defensible number range, one or two reasons the employer should move, and a clear sense of which parts of the package are flexible at that company — base pay is often capped by a band, while start date, sign-on money and remote days frequently are not.

This playbook covers how to read your leverage, which tactics suit which circumstances, scripts you can adapt, and what to confirm in writing before you accept. What is negotiable varies a lot by employer, sector and state: public-sector and union-covered roles often use fixed pay scales, while private employers in competitive markets have more room.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Base pay is usually the hardest lever when a role sits inside a published band; ask the recruiter directly what the band is for your level and location before you name a number.
> - Sign-on bonus and start date are commonly the most flexible items for mid-level private-sector roles — get the sign-on amount, payment date and any repayment clause in writing.
> - Equity needs checking, not just counting: most grants use a four-year schedule with a one-year cliff where 25% vests at year one and the rest monthly (Carta, undated), and leaving before the cliff forfeits the whole grant (Incentrium, undated).

## Know Your Leverage

Leverage is a set of facts about the employer's position, not a feeling about your own. Work through three of them before you respond to an offer.

**Market data.** Get a range for your occupation in your metro area, not a national average. The Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics program, which the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics describes as "the only comprehensive source of regularly produced occupational employment and wage rate information for the U.S. economy" (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, undated), publishes annual estimates for over 800 occupations at national, state and metro level (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, undated). CareerOneStop's Salary Finder, run by the U.S. Department of Labor, presents the same source data as low, median and high wages for more than 900 occupations in any U.S. location (CareerOneStop, undated), drawing on the May 2025 estimates (CareerOneStop, undated). O*NET OnLine shows national and state wage data in its Wages & Employment Trends summary (O*NET OnLine, undated). These are occupational averages, so treat them as a floor check rather than a target for specialised or senior roles.

**A competing offer.** A written offer from another employer is the strongest single lever, because it makes your walk-away concrete. A vague "I'm interviewing elsewhere" does far less, and inventing one is a bad bet in small industries.

**Time-to-fill pressure.** Replacing you as a candidate costs the employer weeks. SHRM benchmarking has put average time-to-fill at roughly 42 days in past survey cycles (SHRM, undated), with 2026 data showing a median of 39 calendar days for non-executive roles and 45 days for executive roles (SHRM, 2026); other SHRM breakdowns show shorter medians at entry level than at executive level (SHRM, 2025-2026). The longer a role has been open, the more a hiring manager will pay to stop searching.

**Scarcity.** If the job description names a licence, clearance, language or stack that few local candidates hold, say so explicitly when you counter.

## Negotiation Tactics and When to Use Them

| Tactic | Best Used When | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask for the pay band and level before naming a number | The recruiter asks for your expectations on the first call, or you are in a state or city with pay-range disclosure rules | The recruiter may hold the question open and re-ask it at the offer stage, so you still need a range ready within a day or two |
| Counter on base with a researched range | Your target sits above the offer but inside the band, and you have metro-level wage data to cite | Adds roughly three to five business days while the recruiter re-approves the figure with finance or HR compensation |
| Trade base for a sign-on bonus | The recruiter says base is capped at the top of the band for your level | Sign-on money is usually one-time and often carries a 12-month repayment clause if you leave, so your year-two pay stays at the lower base |
| Present a written competing offer | You genuinely hold one and would accept it | The employer may decline to match and close the file, leaving you with only the other offer; some firms also mark matched hires as flight risks |
| Negotiate non-cash items (remote days, start date, title, training budget) | Pay is fixed by a public scale, union agreement or civil-service grade | Verbal promises on remote days are frequently not in the contract and evaporate when a manager changes; you must get them written in |
| Ask for a 6-month compensation review instead of more now | The employer is at its ceiling but the hiring manager is clearly keen | Reviews are rarely binding; without a written target figure and date, the conversation often restarts from zero |
| Go silent for 24 hours to "create pressure" | Almost never — only if you need the time to compare a second written offer | Recruiters working to a deadline may move to a backup candidate; unexplained silence after a verbal offer reads as a decline at some firms |

## Sample Negotiation Conversations

Names and figures below are invented. Adapt the structure, not the wording.

### Dialogue 1: countering on base pay

> Recruiter (Dana, Northfield Analytics)
> 
> We'd like to offer you the senior data analyst role. Base is at the number we discussed, plus the standard annual bonus. Can you confirm today?

> Candidate
> 
> Thank you — I want to accept this. One thing before I do: the base is below the midpoint for senior analysts in this metro area in the Department of Labor's wage data, and I'd be taking on the vendor reporting that wasn't in the original description. Could you move the base up by six percent?

> Recruiter
> 
> That's above the band for this level. I can't do six.

> Candidate
> 
> Understood. What is the top of the band for this level? If base is capped there, I'd look at a sign-on that covers the bonus I'm forfeiting by leaving in March.

> Recruiter
> 
> Let me take the sign-on idea to the hiring manager and come back Thursday.

### Dialogue 2: negotiating remote days

> Recruiter (Marcus, Calderwood Health)
> 
> Everything's approved — the salary is final at the figure in the letter. We're four days on-site, one flexible.

> Candidate
> 
> The pay works for me. The commute is the issue: it's ninety minutes each way. Would two fixed remote days, Tuesday and Thursday, be possible after my first month?

> Recruiter
> 
> The team policy is one. I can't rewrite that for a new hire.

> Candidate
> 
> Would your manager consider two days as a reviewed arrangement — one day from the start, a second from month three if my reporting deadlines are met? If that's agreed, I'd want it noted in the offer letter rather than kept as a verbal understanding.

> Recruiter
> 
> A reviewed arrangement I can ask about. I'll confirm in writing either way by Friday.

## Before You Say Yes

Ask for every item below in the written offer or an email from the recruiter. A verbal "yes, of course" from someone who later leaves the company is worth very little.

1. **Base salary, with the pay period and annualised figure.** Candidates routinely confuse an hourly rate, a monthly figure and an annual salary, especially when the role is non-exempt and overtime rules apply.
2. **Bonus: target percentage, what triggers it, and whether it is discretionary.** "Up to 15%" often means company performance gates individual performance, and a first-year bonus is frequently prorated from your start date.
3. **Sign-on bonus: amount, payment date and clawback period.** Many sign-ons are repayable in full if you leave within 12 months, and some are paid after a probation period rather than with the first paycheck.
4. **Equity: number of units, grant date, strike price if applicable, and the vesting schedule.** The common structure is four years with a one-year cliff — 25% at year one, then roughly 1/48 monthly (Carta, undated) — and leaving before the cliff forfeits the entire grant (Incentrium, undated).
5. **Benefits start date and employee cost.** Coverage sometimes begins on the first of the month after hire, not day one, and CareerOneStop notes benefits "can make up almost a third of your total pay package" (CareerOneStop, undated), so compare premiums and retirement match, not just salary.
6. **Start date and any pre-employment conditions.** Offers are commonly contingent on a background check, drug screen, references or licence verification; know which apply before you resign from your current job.
7. **Work location and remote arrangement in writing.** Hybrid schedules agreed verbally with a recruiter are often absent from the contract and change when the manager or policy changes.
8. **Title, level and who you report to.** Internal level codes drive future raise bands and eligibility, and a title that sounds senior at one employer can sit a level lower in the pay structure.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I negotiate on a verbal offer or wait for the letter?+**

You can open the conversation on a verbal offer, but do not resign anything until terms are in writing. A practical approach: thank them, ask for the full package in writing, then counter by email so the numbers are documented. If a recruiter pushes for an answer on the call, say you'll respond within 24 hours of seeing the written terms.

**Can an employer withdraw an offer because I negotiated?+**

It happens rarely, but it is possible. In most U.S. jurisdictions employment is at-will, which makes a rescinded offer hard to enforce (National Association of Colleges and Employers, undated). A claim may exist if the offer specified a term or just-cause termination (NACE, undated), or where you relocated or declined other offers (firsthr.app, undated). Federal law bars rescission based on protected characteristics (Indeed, undated). Consult an employment attorney in your state.

**Is it acceptable to counter twice?+**

Once is normal; twice is possible only if you are countering on a different item. Coming back a second time on base pay after they moved reads as bad faith. Coming back once on base, then accepting their number while asking about a start date or sign-on, is routine. Bundle all your asks into one message rather than drip-feeding them over several days.

**What if the salary is fixed by a government or union scale?+**

Public-sector, school district and union-covered roles usually place you on a grade and step, so base is rarely movable. Focus instead on starting step placement — many systems allow credit for prior experience or education if you ask before signing — plus start date, shift assignment, relocation support and tuition or certification funding. Ask HR which of these the collective agreement or personnel rules permit.
