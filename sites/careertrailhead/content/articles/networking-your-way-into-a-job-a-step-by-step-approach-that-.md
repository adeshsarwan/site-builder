Most job searches stall at the same place: you send applications into a portal and hear nothing back. Networking fixes that by getting a human being to look at your name before, or instead of, the pile. This guide gives you a concrete sequence — build a contact list, request short informational conversations, ask for introductions, and follow up on a set cadence — with message examples you can adapt.

It is work, not magic. No tactic here guarantees an interview, and what each employer does with a referral varies widely. But a structured approach gives you something a volume-apply strategy cannot: people who know what you're looking for and remember you when something opens.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Ask for a 20-minute informational conversation, not a job — CareerOneStop's sample line is "Do you have time in the next two weeks for a 20-minute informational interview?" (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated)
> - End every conversation by asking for two names you could speak to next, and send a thank-you within 24 hours
> - Follow up once after about seven business days, then every four to six weeks with a one-line update

## Why This Strategy Matters

Online applications are easy to send and easy to ignore. Every posting on a large board collects hundreds of them, and the people reading those applications are usually working through a queue with no context about who you are. A referral or an introduction changes the context — someone inside the organization has already attached a name and a short endorsement to your file.

The hiring data that exists backs this up as a channel worth your time. Employee referrals delivered more than 30 percent of all hires overall in 2016 and 45 percent of internal hires, according to data reported by SHRM (SHRM, undated). The same report notes that internal sources produced 52 percent of hires in 2016, compared with 48 percent from external sources (SHRM, undated). LinkedIn, writing about its own internal referral tool, reported hiring referred candidates about two weeks faster than before, and an 11.4 percent increase in people hired via referral in the three months after launch compared with the three months before (LinkedIn (company blog), undated). Treat all of these as snapshots of particular organizations in particular years, not as rates you should expect in your own search.

CareerOneStop, run by the U.S. Department of Labor, describes networking as the most valuable way to invest your time in a job search: in a networking conversation you describe the work you're looking for and ask about jobs now or in the future (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). That's the whole mechanism. You are not asking for a favor; you are making sure that when a role opens, someone already knows what you do.

How much of this is available to you varies. Formal referral programs and referral bonuses are set employer by employer — a large company may have a structured portal, a 12-person firm may have nothing but the owner's inbox. Alumni offices and career services exist at most US colleges but differ enormously in what they'll do for graduates. And if you're job searching outside the US, or on a visa, the norms around cold outreach and the rules around who can work where are different again; check with the relevant national employment service and, for anything immigration-related, a qualified adviser rather than a contact's recollection.

## Step-by-Step Process

1. **Write a one-sentence target statement.** You cannot ask for help until you can say what you want in a form someone can repeat to a colleague. Draft it, then say it out loud.

> Target statement — personalize every bracket before using it
> 
> "I'm looking for [mid-level operations analyst] roles at [regional hospital systems] in [the Denver metro area], where I can use [SQL and process documentation]."

1. **List 30 people you already know.** Work through the categories CareerOneStop names: friends, family, neighbors, former teachers, professors and classmates, people in community or religious groups, current and past co-workers and supervisors, and social media contacts (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Put them in a spreadsheet with columns for Name, How I know them, Industry, Last contact, Next action. Include people who are not in your field — they know people who are.

1. **Build a target employer list of 15 to 20 organizations.** Then follow each one's social media pages so postings reach you directly, which CareerOneStop recommends as an online networking action (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). On LinkedIn, press **Follow** on the company page and check its **Jobs** tab; on a company page you can also open the **People** tab and sort by what employees studied or what they do.

1. **Find second-degree connections at those employers.** In LinkedIn search, run the role title, switch to the **People** tab, then use **All filters** to set **Connections: 2nd**, **Current company: [target employer]** and **Locations: [your metro]**. Example search string: `"supply chain planner" hospital`. Note down who could introduce you.

1. **Request informational interviews — not jobs.** An informational interview is an informal conversation with someone in a career or company of interest, used to learn about the work environment, get advice on breaking into a field, and meet people who may offer future leads (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). CareerOneStop's recommended approach is to make contact by phone or email, introduce yourself, your situation and the purpose, suggest dates and times, and close with a thank-you (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

> Email — rewrite this in your own words before sending
> 
> Subject: 20 minutes about [field] — [mutual contact] suggested I write
> 
> Hi [Name] — I'm [your name], currently [your situation in six words]. I'm exploring [target role] and [specific thing about their path or employer] is exactly the route I'm trying to understand. Do you have time in the next two weeks for a 20-minute informational interview? I'm free [two specific windows], and happy to work around you. Thank you either way.

The 20-minute line is CareerOneStop's own sample wording (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

1. **Prepare five questions you cannot answer from the website.** Generic questions get generic answers. Ask things like: "What does the first 90 days actually look like in that team?" or "What do people underestimate about moving from [your current field] into this one?" Keep to the 20 minutes you asked for and stop at 20 unless they continue.

1. **Ask for two names at the end of every conversation.** CareerOneStop suggests asking contacts whether they know anyone they'd be willing to connect you with (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Use a line like: "Is there one or two other people you'd suggest I speak to?" If they offer, ask whether they'd prefer to make the introduction or for you to write and mention their name.

1. **Send a thank-you within 24 hours, with one specific detail.** Example subject line: `Thank you — and the [vendor-management] point stuck with me`. Two short paragraphs: what you took from it, and what you did as a result. This is the message that makes you memorable, not the request.

1. **Convert a warm contact into a referral only when a specific role is posted.** Send the posting link, your resume, and three lines they can paste into a referral form. Example: `Role: [title, req number]. Why me: [two relevant achievements with numbers]. Happy for you to pass or skip — no awkwardness either way.` Whether an internal referral form exists at all depends on the employer.

1. **Set a follow-up cadence and one event a month.** Follow up once after about seven business days if there's no reply, then move the contact to a four-to-six-week check-in with a genuine update ("finished the [certification]", "started as [role]"). Alongside that, book one career fair, conference, chamber of commerce event, or alumni/career services appointment each month — all channels CareerOneStop recommends (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).

## Before You Start Checklist

Work through these before you send the first message. Each one takes under half an hour.

- A target job title you can state in one breath, matching the wording employers actually use in postings — not your internal company title.
- A resume whose top third mirrors the language of your three most-wanted postings: job title alignment, three or four repeated skill keywords, and at least two achievements with a number attached.
- A LinkedIn headline that names the role you want rather than only the role you have, plus an About section that ends with how to reach you.
- A spreadsheet or tracker set up with columns for Name, Employer, Source of introduction, Date contacted, Date followed up, Next action date.
- A list of 15 to 20 target employers, each one followed on at least one social platform so their postings appear in your feed.
- Two or three sample pieces of your own work — an article, project write-up or portfolio page — posted or linked on your profile, which CareerOneStop lists as an online networking action (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated).
- Two calendar blocks a week, 45 minutes each, reserved for outreach and follow-up only.
- A realistic pay range for your target role in your location, checked against the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics data, which BLS also provides guidance on using in salary negotiations (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, undated).
- A professional email address and a signature block with your phone number, city and profile link.
- One sentence ready for when someone asks why you're leaving your current job — short, neutral, no complaints.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if I don't know anyone at the company?+**

Start one ring out. Look for people who used to work there, people who worked with your target team at a supplier or client, or alumni of your school in the same city. A past employee will often speak more freely about the culture than a current one, and current staff frequently stay in touch with the colleagues who left.

**Doesn't this feel like using people?+**

It does if your first message asks for a job. The informational interview format exists precisely to avoid that — it's an informal conversation to learn about the work and get advice, not a request for employment (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor), undated). Most people enjoy talking about their own path for 20 minutes, and you make it mutual by being easy to help: specific ask, short meeting, prompt thank-you.

**How many people should I contact, and what reply rate is normal?+**

Treat outreach as a steady weekly habit rather than a blitz — five to eight new contacts a week is sustainable for most people alongside applications. Reply rates vary enormously by field, seniority and whether there's a shared connection, and nobody can promise you a figure; plan for silence from the majority and judge the method over a couple of months, not a week.

**I'm employed and don't want my manager to know. Can I still do this?+**

Yes, with care. Keep outreach on your personal email and phone, and turn off LinkedIn's "Share profile changes" setting before you edit your profile so updates don't broadcast to your network. LinkedIn's "Open to work" feature also has a recruiters-only visibility option, though no platform setting is airtight — assume anything you post could be seen.

**Does a referral mean I skip the application?+**

Almost never. In most organizations a referral means your application gets looked at by a person sooner, not that the process is waived — you'll still submit through the portal and interview. How much weight a referral carries, and whether a referral bonus exists at all, is set employer by employer, so ask your contact how their process actually works before you assume.

**What do I do when a contact goes quiet after being helpful?+**

Assume workload, not rejection. Send one short follow-up after about a week referencing the specific thing you discussed, then let it rest and return in four to six weeks with an update rather than a repeat ask. Contacts who've gone cold often resurface months later when a role opens on their team — which is the whole reason to keep the tracker current.
