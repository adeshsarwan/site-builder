Most US hiring processes now mix formats: a phone screen with a recruiter, one or two video rounds with the hiring manager and a team, and sometimes a final in-person visit. The questions often repeat across rounds, but the mechanics do not — pacing, what the interviewer can see, what can break, and how you follow up all change.

This guide compares the three formats across the things that actually trip people up, then gives you a prep checklist for each and a single table you can work through the night before.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - On the phone your voice carries everything; on video the camera crops you to the shoulders up, so eye level and framing matter more than posture.
> - Notes are invisible on a call, must sit beside the webcam on video, and go in a notebook in person — CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor-sponsored), undated, advises bringing a notebook and pens.
> - Follow-up differs by format: ask the caller to spell their name and give an email, note what was screen-shared on video, and write separately to each person you met onsite.

## How the Formats Differ

**Pacing and turn-taking**

- Phone: with no visual cue for when someone has finished, pauses feel longer than they are. Leave about a one-second beat after the interviewer stops before you answer, and expect to be asked more follow-ups because they cannot read your face.
- Video: platform latency adds a fraction of a second, so talking over each other is common. Wait until their audio has fully stopped, and raise a hand or unmute-and-say-their-name rather than cutting in on a panel.
- In person: turn-taking is natural, but there is more small talk at the start and end. CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor-sponsored), undated, advises showing confidence while letting the interviewer start the dialogue.

**Body language cues**

- Phone: the interviewer has only tone, pace and volume. Sitting upright or standing changes how your voice projects, and a smile is audible in vowel sounds.
- Video: you are cropped to roughly the shoulders up. Put the camera at eye level, fill the middle of the frame with your head and upper chest, and look at the lens for the first and last sentence of each answer.
- In person: full posture, handshake norms (which vary by region and industry), and seating order in a panel room are all visible. Take the seat you are offered; if none is indicated, sit opposite the person who greeted you.

**Tech and setup**

- Phone: a wired headset or the handset itself beats speakerphone. Check signal where you will actually sit, and silence other devices.
- Video: test the link, camera and mic in the same browser or app you will use, and keep a backup — the dial-in number or the recruiter's mobile — on paper next to you.
- In person: the setup is travel. CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor-sponsored), undated, suggests planning to arrive 10 to 15 minutes early, turning your phone off beforehand, going by yourself, and leaving coffee, water bottles and backpacks at home or in the car.

**Notes and materials**

- Phone: notes can be spread out freely. Keep the job posting, your question list and three or four examples in front of you.
- Video: anything you read should sit immediately beside the webcam, on one page, in large type — eyes travelling across a second monitor is visible.
- In person: a notebook and pens, plus printed copies of your résumé for a panel, are the practical limit. CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor-sponsored), undated, recommends bringing a notebook and pens.

**Follow-up etiquette**

- Phone: you may never see the caller's name written down. Ask them to spell it and ask for the best email address before you hang up.
- Video: thank-you notes can reference the deck or document that was screen-shared, which shows you were paying attention to content rather than the call.
- In person: collect names and roles as you go, and send a separate short note to each person who interviewed you rather than one message to the recruiter.

## Format-Specific Prep Checklist

**Phone screen**

- Call a friend from the exact chair and room you will use, and ask them to confirm there is no echo, no background hum and no dropout.
- Charge the phone to full and switch call waiting or notifications off so a second call cannot interrupt.
- Write the interviewer's name, the job title and the posting's three main requirements on one sheet and tape it where you can see it without rustling paper.
- Put a glass of water within reach and keep food and gum out of the room.

**Video interview**

- Raise the webcam to eye level with a stack of books or a stand, so the lens is level with your eyebrows rather than looking up at you.
- Join the test meeting 15 minutes before and grant camera and microphone permissions in the same browser profile or app version you will use live.
- Sit facing a window or lamp, with nothing bright directly behind you, and check what is in frame over your shoulder.
- Save the dial-in number and the recruiter's phone number on paper in case the video fails.
- Close other applications and pause cloud backups or large downloads that compete for bandwidth.

**In-person interview**

- Map the route, parking or transit stop and the building entrance, and plan to arrive 10 to 15 minutes early (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor-sponsored), undated).
- Turn your phone off, not to silent, before you reach reception, and go by yourself (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor-sponsored), undated).
- Leave coffee, water bottles and backpacks at home or in the car, and carry a notebook, pens and printed résumé copies instead (CareerOneStop (U.S. Department of Labor-sponsored), undated).
- Write down each interviewer's name and role in your notebook as you are introduced, for your follow-up notes.
- Email or call the recruiter the day before to confirm the start time, the building address and who to ask for at the desk.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I handle a panel interview held over video?+**

Ask the recruiter in advance for the attendee list with roles, and write the names in the order they appear on your screen. Direct each answer to the person who asked by name, since eye contact cannot signal it. Switch to gallery view so you can see reactions, and pause longer than usual before answering to avoid talking over someone on a lag.

**The process starts with a phone screen and ends onsite. How much should my answers change?+**

Keep the content consistent — interviewers compare notes — but adjust the depth. Phone screens usually test fit and basic requirements, so answer in 60 to 90 seconds. Onsite rounds allow whiteboards, documents and follow-ups, so bring longer examples and the detail behind your numbers. Reuse your strongest examples rather than inventing new ones for each round.

**What if some interviewers are in the room and others join by video?+**

Ask beforehand where the camera and room microphone sit, and whether remote participants can hear people at the far end of the table. Address the screen as deliberately as the room: name remote participants when answering them, and repeat questions asked in the room so the remote side has context. Follow up with everyone, in-room and remote, individually.
