Moving into a management job means two onboarding jobs at once: learning a new organisation, and learning how to get work done through other people. This roadmap gives you a pre-start checklist, concrete goals for your first day and first week, measurable benchmarks at 30, 60 and 90 days, and scripts you can say out loud in the conversations that come up most.

If no one has trained you for this, you are in ordinary company — Gartner research reported by Fast Company (2024-04-10) found a large share of new people managers get no formal training before stepping into the role, and a Chartered Management Institute study cited by Reed found most people entering management have had no proper training. A written plan is the cheapest substitute.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - In week 1, hold a separate 30-minute one-on-one with every direct report before you hold any group discussion about how the team works.
> - By day 30, write down the three outcomes your own manager will judge you on and email them back for confirmation.
> - By day 90, ship one small team deliverable and document one process the team was carrying in people's heads.

## Before You Start: Pre-Day-One Checklist

Work through these in the week before you start. Most take under 15 minutes each.

- Email your manager or HR contact to confirm your start time, the address or video link, who meets you, and what you need to bring.
- Gather your identity and work-authorisation documents. In the US, you must complete Section 1 of Form I-9 no later than your first day of employment (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, 2025-01-20), and your employer must complete and sign Section 2 within three business days of your hire date (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services). Outside the US, ask HR which equivalent paperwork applies.
- Finish whatever the employer's portal lets you do early: tax forms, direct deposit, emergency contacts, benefits enrolment deadlines. An effective new-hire checklist covers paperwork, IT setup, policies, benefits, role training and manager check-ins (AIHR), so ask for theirs and find the gaps.
- Ask IT which accounts will be live on day one — email, payroll, VPN, the chat tool, the project tracker — and specifically whether you will have manager-level access to approve time off and expenses, which is usually a separate request.
- Request the team's org chart with names, job titles, start dates and time zones, and read it twice.
- Ask for the last two quarters of team goals or metrics and a list of projects currently in flight.
- Ask your manager one question by email: "What would make my first 90 days obviously successful to you?" Keep the reply.
- Do a dry run of the commute at the hour you'll actually travel, or, if remote, test camera, headset, screen sharing and a backup connection on the exact platform the company uses.
- Sort the home logistics that will otherwise eat week one: childcare, pet cover, parking permit, building access card, dress code.
- Draft the five questions you will ask each direct report in their first one-on-one, so you ask everyone the same ones.
- Write down three things you will not change in your first 30 days — a recurring meeting, a tool, a reporting format — so you have an answer ready when someone asks if you're about to reorganise everything.
- Put two 30-minute blocks a day in your calendar for the first week, labelled "write up notes", before anyone else fills the space.

## Day 1 to Week 1

**First morning**

- Complete Section 1 of Form I-9 or your country's equivalent before you do anything else; in the US the deadline is your first day of employment (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, 2025-01-20).
- Log into email, chat and the project tracker, and raise an IT ticket immediately for anything that fails rather than working around it all week.
- Learn and correctly use the names of at least five people, including the person who handles payroll and the person who handles building or systems access.

**End of day 1**

- Have a 15-minute conversation with your own manager covering: how often you'll meet, how they prefer to be contacted for urgent things, and what is already on fire.
- Send each direct report a short message introducing yourself and booking their first one-on-one for this week.
- Write a one-page note of everything you didn't understand — acronyms, product names, team nicknames. This list is your week-one curriculum.

**End of day 3**

- Confirm your employer has completed Section 2 of your I-9; in the US they must do so within three business days of your hire date (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services).
- Hold at least half of your first one-on-ones. Leaders interviewed for a book on senior leadership recommend making your first real conversation with each direct report one-on-one when you're discussing how the department works, and keeping any group gathering purely social (SHRM, 2022-10-12).
- Attend the team's existing meetings as an observer and change none of them yet.

**End of week 1**

- Every direct report has had a private 30-minute conversation with you in which you asked the same five questions and wrote down the answers.
- You can name each person's current projects and the one thing each says is blocking them.
- You have set a weekly recurring one-on-one slot with each report, with a shared agenda document. Harvard Business Review (April 2024) advises new managers to schedule the meeting weekly, create an agenda, and send the key discussion points to the report in advance.
- You have emailed your manager a short summary: what you heard, what you plan to look at next, and one thing you need from them.

A useful contrast. Bad week one: you spend four days reading documents, then announce a new status-report template on Friday. Good week one: you spend four days asking people what they're working on and what gets in the way, then on Friday you remove one recurring meeting that three people told you was useless.

## 30/60/90 Day Benchmarks

A 30-60-90 day plan is simply a written outline of what you intend to accomplish in each of those stretches (Indeed Career Guide, 2026-06-16), used in a management move to get clear on expectations, spot problems early and set goals (LeadDev, 2025-11-05). Write yours in the first week and send it to your manager. Keep each item something a third party could check.

**Day 30 — understand the work and the people**

- Every direct report has had at least four weekly one-on-ones with you, with written agendas. Gallup research cited by Baylor University Human Resources (2023) found employees who have regular one-on-ones with their manager are more likely to be engaged.
- You have held introductory one-on-ones with your key stakeholders outside the team — LeadDev's example goal for a new manager is scheduling those initial stakeholder conversations inside the first 30 days (LeadDev, 2025-11-05).
- You have written down, and had your manager confirm in writing, the three outcomes you'll be measured on.

**Day 60 — take something on**

- You own and have started one small, visible piece of work. Indeed's template suggests month two is when a new manager leads a small project, after month one spent building relationships and learning processes (Indeed Career Guide, 2026-06-16).
- Recurring meetings with outside stakeholders are in the calendar, not just one-off introductions (LeadDev, 2025-11-05).
- You have given each direct report one piece of specific feedback — naming the behaviour, the situation and the effect — and asked each for one piece in return.

**Day 90 — deliver and document**

- The project you picked up at day 60 has shipped, or has a dated plan and a named owner for each remaining step.
- One process the team was carrying in people's heads is written down: who does what, in what order, and what triggers it.
- You have a documented plan for the next two quarters, including any hiring or skills gaps, discussed with your manager. Indeed's template puts team feedback and longer-term planning in month three (Indeed Career Guide, 2026-06-16).

Two cautions. Onboarding doesn't stop at 90 days — the guidance summarised by Vensure is that SHRM recommends onboarding run across the whole first year, with checkpoints at week one, month one, 60 days and 90 days — and an excerpt on new leaders published by SHRM (2022-10-12) notes that although books focus on the first 90 to 100 days, many of the results that matter take longer than that to show up.

## Scripts for Common Onboarding Conversations

Say these more or less as written. Short sentences are fine; people remember what you asked, not how polished it sounded.

### Introducing yourself to the team (first group gathering, day 1 or 2)

> Keep it short and say what happens next
> 
> "I'm Dana, I'm your new manager, and I've done this kind of work for about six years. I'm not planning to change anything this month. I've booked 30 minutes with each of you this week to hear what you're working on and what's annoying you. After those, I'll tell you what I've heard and what I think we should do first. Ask me anything now or in your one-on-one."

### Asking your own manager for clearer priorities (week 1 or 2)

> Name the choice you're trying to make
> 
> "I've got three things people have told me are urgent: the vendor renewal, the hiring backlog, and the reporting mess. I can do one of them properly this month. Which one matters most to you, and what does done look like? If I'm wrong about the list, tell me what's missing."

### First one-on-one with a direct report (week 1)

> Four questions, then listen
> 
> "Four things I want to ask, and I'm asking everyone the same ones. What's on your mind right now? What's on your plate this month? What do you need from me? And what should I know about how this team works that nobody writes down? I'll take notes and send you what I heard."

Those four openers follow the agenda Drexel University Human Resources publishes for one-on-ones with a new employee: what's on your mind, what's on your plate, what do you need from me, and feedback in both directions.

### Asking for feedback at day 30

> Make it easy to be honest
> 
> "It's been a month. What's one thing I've done that you'd like me to keep doing, and one thing you'd change? You can be blunt — I'd rather hear it now than at my review. If nothing stands out yet, tell me what you'd want to see from me by day 90."

### When you don't know the answer (any week)

> Don't guess in front of your team
> 
> "I don't know yet. I'll find out who owns that and come back to you by Thursday."

Then actually come back on Thursday, even if the answer is "still looking".

## Frequently asked questions

**What if I forget someone's name in week one?+**

Say so immediately and ask again: "Sorry, remind me of your name — I've met a lot of people this week." Nobody holds that against a manager in their first fortnight, but they do notice if you avoid them for a month instead. Keep a private list of names, roles and one personal detail, and review it for two minutes before each meeting in your first two weeks.

**Nobody has given me any management training. Is that normal?+**

It is common. Gartner research reported by Fast Company (2024-04-10) found a large share of new people managers get no formal training before taking the role, research cited by Pryor Learning found new managers wait years on average for their first formal training, and a January 2023 Gartner survey found managers with two years' experience or less struggle to support their team. Ask HR in week one what training exists and when you can join it, and in the meantime book a monthly 30-minute conversation with an experienced manager in another team.

**Should I change anything in my first 30 days?+**

Change only things that are cheap to reverse and that your team has already asked for — cancelling a meeting everyone calls useless, or fixing a broken handover. Save structural changes, new tools and new metrics for after day 60, once you can explain the problem in the team's own words. A manager who redesigns the workflow in week two usually has to undo it in week eight.

**What if I'm now managing people who used to be my peers?+**

Have the awkward conversation in a private one-on-one in week one rather than letting it sit: "Our working relationship changes a bit now — tell me what you want to be different, and what you want kept the same." Be consistent about the things that are now your job, such as approving leave and giving performance feedback. Expect the first month to feel strange and the strangeness to fade once people see you apply the same rules to everyone.

**When can I ask about pay or a promotion?+**

Not in the first 90 days unless you were promoted internally without a pay conversation, in which case raise it in week one — ask HR when the change takes effect and get it in writing. Otherwise, find out in month one when the company's review and compensation cycle runs, then plan your case for the cycle before that date. Pay structures and timing vary a lot by employer, state and country; check your offer letter, your employer's policy, and current public salary data for your role and metro area before you name a number.

**What do I do if my manager is too busy to onboard me?+**

Send them a one-page 30-60-90 plan in week one and ask for a yes or no on each item — it is far easier to react to a draft than to produce guidance from scratch. Book a recurring 30-minute slot rather than relying on ad hoc time. If they cancel twice in a row, ask for a single written answer to one question: what does success look like at day 90?
