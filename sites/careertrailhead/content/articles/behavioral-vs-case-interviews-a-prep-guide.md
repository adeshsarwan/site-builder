Behavioral interviews ask you to describe how you handled real past situations so the interviewer can judge your behavior and personality (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor, undated). Case interviews hand you a business problem and watch you structure it, question it, and do arithmetic on the spot — BCG calls this a case study or problem-solving interview (BCG, undated), and McKinsey formally names its version the Problem-Solving Interview (McKinsey & Company, undated).

The two formats differ in pacing, in what silence signals, in what sits on your desk, and in what a sensible follow-up note says. This guide compares them dimension by dimension, gives you a separate prep checklist for each, and ends with a task-by-task reference table. Expect specifics to vary by employer, country office and role level — consulting, finance and tech all run these formats slightly differently.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Behavioral interviews reward prepared, self-contained stories; the STAR structure — Situation, Task, Action, Result — is the standard framework (MIT CAPD, undated).
> - Case interviews reward audible reasoning: BCG's published 'Don't' list for case interviews names Stay Silent, Rush Your Response and Panic Under Pressure (BCG, undated).
> - Your desk differs: a case needs pen and paper in reach (Bain & Company, undated); a behavioral interview needs a one-page story grid you can glance at, not read from.

## How the Formats Differ

**Pacing and who drives the clock**

- Behavioral: the interviewer asks, you answer in a contained block, then they probe. CareerOneStop notes behavioral questions are more probing and more specific than traditional interview questions (CareerOneStop, U.S. Department of Labor, undated), so budget for two or three follow-ups on a single story.
- Case: one prompt opens a back-and-forth that runs most of the slot. BCG describes candidates structuring an approach, asking thoughtful questions, analyzing data and performing quick calculations (BCG, undated) — you set the pace between data drops.

**Body language and what silence signals**

- Behavioral: steady eye contact and settled posture support a story you already know. Bain's in-person tips cover eye contact, posture and minimizing fidgeting (Bain & Company, undated).
- Case: the same posture rules apply, but silence is read as being stuck rather than being thoughtful. Announce a pause before you take one.

**Desk, materials and tech setup**

- Behavioral: a clear surface, your resume, and a short prompt card. If it is a video round, CareerOneStop advises checking computer, camera and microphone and practicing on the employer's platform, such as Zoom or Teams (CareerOneStop, undated).
- Case: Bain advises bringing pen and paper to in-person case interviews (Bain & Company, undated) and, for virtual cases, a quiet well-lit space with a neutral or blurred background, tested internet, camera and microphone, notifications off, and verbally signaling when you are taking notes (Bain & Company, undated).

**What the interviewer is recording**

- Behavioral: evidence of past behavior. McKinsey recommends preparing two personal examples demonstrating impact in each of four designated theme areas for its Personal Experience Interview (McKinsey & Company, undated) — a useful shape for any employer's version.
- Case: how you got there. BCG's 'Do' list names Listen Actively, Think Structurally, Communicate Clearly, Clarify Assumptions and Show Your Thinking (BCG, undated).

**Follow-up etiquette**

- Behavioral: CareerOneStop says a thank-you note should include appreciation, continued interest, a brief restatement of your qualifications, and the date and time you will follow up (CareerOneStop, undated), sent soon after — the same day is best, and email is best if a decision will be made quickly (same source).
- Case: same four elements, but reference the problem rather than relitigating your answer. Sending corrected math after the fact rarely changes a score and can read as second-guessing; if you genuinely misread an exhibit, one sentence is the limit.

## Format-Specific Prep Checklist

**Behavioral interview**

1. Build a story grid: four themes down the side, two examples each across — the shape McKinsey recommends for its Personal Experience Interview (McKinsey & Company, undated). Eight stories, one line each.
2. Write each one in STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result (MIT CAPD, undated) — and end every Result with a number you can defend, e.g. "onboarding time at Harlow Freight dropped from 11 days to 6."
3. Record a mock interview with a friend, as CareerOneStop's virtual-interview guidance suggests, watching for natural gestures and visible engagement (CareerOneStop, undated). Cut any answer running past 2 minutes.
4. If it is a panel — two or more interviewers, possibly HR, the hiring manager, the supervisor and a team lead (CareerOneStop, undated) — ask the recruiter for names, titles and seating or screen order, and write them on a card beside your keyboard.

**Case interview**

1. In person: two working pens, a blank unlined pad in landscape, business professional dress, and arrival early enough to settle (Bain & Company, undated).
2. Virtual: neutral or blurred background, light in front of you, camera at eye level, notifications off, and a tested mic and connection (Bain & Company, undated).
3. Drill arithmetic on paper, not mentally: ten percentage-change sums, five break-evens and five market-size estimates, timed at 60 seconds each.
4. Rehearse clarifying out loud — restate the question, state two assumptions, ask one scoping question — since BCG lists Clarify Assumptions and Show Your Thinking among its 'Do's (BCG, undated).
5. Practice signaling your pauses, because on video the interviewer cannot see your pad:

> Signaling a pause on a virtual case
> 
> **You:** "I'd like 60 seconds to structure this. I'll be writing, not reading." **Interviewer:** "Fine — but while you write, tell me what you think the client's actual decision is." **You:** "Whether to keep the Dunmore plant open past next year. I'll come back with the two or three figures that settle it."

| Test audio and video | Check computer, camera and mic and practice on the employer's platform, e.g. Zoom or Teams (CareerOneStop, undated) | Same checks, plus screen-share of a PDF exhibit and confirm your mic picks you up while you look down at paper (Bain & Company, undated) |
| Set the room | Quiet space, notifications off, face lit from the front | Add a neutral or blurred background and clear desk space wide enough for a landscape pad (Bain & Company, undated) |
| Items within reach | Resume, job ad, story grid card, water | Two pens, blank unlined paper, calculator only if the interviewer permits it (Bain & Company, undated) |
| Content prep | Two examples per theme across four themes, each written in STAR (McKinsey & Company, undated; MIT CAPD, undated) | Three reusable structures plus timed arithmetic drills; practice asking one scoping question before structuring (BCG, undated) |
| Mock run | Friend asks five probing follow-ups; record and cut answers over 2 minutes (CareerOneStop, undated) | Partner reads a prompt, withholds data until you ask, and interrupts once to test that you don't rush or go silent (BCG, undated) |

## Frequently asked questions

**What changes when a case interview is also a panel, held over video?+**

Ask the recruiter in advance who will be on the call and who runs the case, since a panel may include HR, the hiring manager, the supervisor and a team lead (CareerOneStop, undated). Address your structure to the case-giver, but look at the camera when summarizing. Say names aloud before answering, because gallery view scrambles seating order across devices.

**How do I switch gears when both formats run back to back in one day?+**

Treat them as separate rounds with a reset in between. Clear your case paper off the desk before a behavioral slot so you are not glancing at calculations, and put your story grid away before a case. Two minutes between rounds is usually enough to swap materials, re-read the interviewer's name, and drink water.

**Should I send one thank-you note or one per interviewer?+**

Send one per interviewer where you have their email, each referencing something specific from that conversation. Keep the four standard elements: appreciation, continued interest, a brief restatement of your qualifications, and the date and time you will follow up (CareerOneStop, undated). Send soon after — the same day is best, and email is best if a decision will be made quickly.
