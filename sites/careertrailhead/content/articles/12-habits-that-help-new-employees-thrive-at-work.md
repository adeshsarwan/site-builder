Starting a new job is less about talent than about the handful of routines you set up in your first few weeks. Gallup reported in 2021 that only 12% of U.S. employees say their company does a good job of onboarding, and only 29% of new hires feel fully prepared and supported to excel after it — which means most people have to build their own structure rather than wait for one to arrive.

The habits below are specific: a phrase to say, a slot in the calendar, a note to keep. They work whether your employer runs a formal 30- to 90-day programme or hands you a laptop and wishes you luck. What varies by employer, country and team is the formality — scripts that sound normal in one office read as pushy in another, so adjust the wording and keep the mechanism.

> Key takeaways
> 
> - Meet your onboarding buddy repeatedly, not once: SHRM reported in 2022 that 86 percent of new hires who met a buddy four to eight times in their first 90 days said it made them more productive.
> - Keep one running question list and bring it to your weekly 1:1 instead of pinging colleagues all day — you stop interrupting people and you get fuller answers in one sitting.
> - Send a short recap after every briefing ("here's what I understood — correct me if I've got it wrong") so wrong assumptions surface in week one, not at the deadline.

## The Habits

1. **Book a recurring chat with your onboarding buddy in week one.** SHRM reported in 2022 that 56 percent of new hires who met their buddy at least once in their first 90 days said it helped them become productive quickly, rising to 73 percent for two to three meetings, 86 percent for four to eight, and 97 percent for more than eight — frequency is the whole point. On day two, message whoever was assigned to you (or a colleague who has been there a year or more, which NYU Wasserman's programme lead Mendez suggests as the minimum for a useful buddy) and say: "Could we put 20 minutes in the diary every Tuesday for my first three months? I'd rather save my questions for you than interrupt people all day."

1. **Keep one running question list instead of asking as you go.** Batching turns ten small interruptions into one conversation where the person can give you context, not just an answer. Open a single note on your first morning titled "Questions — [team name]", add to it all week, and bring the list to your 1:1; the one exception is anything that blocks you for more than 30 minutes, which you ask immediately.

1. **Send a recap after every briefing for your first month.** Writing back what you heard exposes the thing you misunderstood while it still costs nothing to fix. Within an hour of any handover meeting, send four or five lines: "Thanks — here's what I took away: 1) the draft goes to Priya first, 2) deadline is the 14th, 3) I own the data pull but not the slides. Correct me if I've got any of that wrong."

1. **Write your own version of the job description in week one.** Gallup has found that only 43% of employees strongly agree they have a clear job description, and new starters are the most exposed to that fog. Draft a single page listing what you think you own, what you contribute to, and what you don't touch, then open your second-week 1:1 with: "I've written down how I understand my remit — can you tell me what's wrong on this page?"

1. **Block two protected work windows a week from day one.** Being instantly reachable all day means you never produce anything long enough to be judged on. Put two 90-minute blocks in your calendar labelled with the actual task, and set a status message that tells people how to break through: "Heads down on the Q3 report until 11 — call me if it's urgent."

1. **Ask for the unwritten version of any documented process.** Written procedures describe the official route; colleagues know which steps the team actually skips and which ones trigger a rejection. When someone sends you a wiki page, reply: "Got it, thanks. Is there anything on here that's out of date, or a step people usually do differently?"

1. **Run two 20-minute intro conversations a week for your first six weeks.** Knowing who owns what saves days of routing work to the wrong person, and the people you've met are the ones who answer you fastest later. Send the same three-line invite each time: "I'm new on the X team and I'll be sending you Y. Could I get 20 minutes to ask what you need from me and what usually goes wrong?"

1. **Flag slippage at the halfway point, never at the deadline.** A manager can reassign, trim scope or buy you two days when there's time left; at 5pm on the due date they can only absorb the damage. Put a reminder at the midpoint of every task in your first 90 days, and if you're behind, say: "I'm about half done and I don't think I'll finish by Thursday. I can either cut the competitor section or get it to you Monday — which do you prefer?"

1. **Keep a personal fixes file from your first login.** The password resets, VPN quirks, file paths and approval chains you solve once will all come back, and writing them down turns you into the person who can onboard the next hire. Use whatever note app you already have, one line per fix, dated — and at day 60, offer the cleaned-up version to your manager as a starter guide.

1. **Ask for feedback at day 30, not at your probation review.** Talya Bauer, author of a SHRM Foundation guideline on onboarding, has said that research and conventional wisdom both suggest employees get about 90 days to prove themselves in a new job, and SHRM advises leaders to check in with new employees in 30-day increments — if yours doesn't, start it yourself. Ask one narrow question rather than "how am I doing?": "In the first month, what's one thing I should do differently in the next month?"

## Habits That Backfire vs. Habits That Work

Most new-starter mistakes are good instincts pointed the wrong way. The behaviours in the left column look identical to the ones in the right from a distance — the difference is in the mechanism.

| Both look like | What backfires | What works instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asking lots of questions | You message a senior colleague eight times on day three — "where's the brand folder?", "who approves this?", "what's the file naming?" Each one takes them out of their own work, and by the fourth they're answering in one word. | You collect the same eight questions in your running list and bring them to the Tuesday buddy slot. You get the folder, the approver, the naming rule *and* the reason behind the naming rule in twelve minutes. |
| Being keen | In week two you volunteer for a cross-team project, a conference write-up and the social committee. In week five you deliver your first real task late, and that's the only thing anyone remembers. | You deliver your assigned work on time through weeks one to four, then at your day-30 check-in say: "My core tasks are landing on schedule now — I'd like to take the conference write-up if it's still open." |
| Showing initiative | You rebuild the team's tracking spreadsheet on day four because the old one is clunky. It turns out finance pulls a report off it on the 1st, and your version breaks it. | You note the clunky bits as you use them for three weeks, then bring one slide to your 1:1: "This takes me about 40 minutes a week. Here's a version that takes ten — who would I need to check with before changing it?" |
| Being responsive | You reply to every message within two minutes, all day, every day. Six weeks in you have a reputation for being helpful and nothing finished with your name on it. | You keep two 90-minute blocks and answer everything else in batches at 11am and 4pm, with a status line saying when you're back. People learn your rhythm within a fortnight. |
| Taking feedback well | Your manager says your updates are too long. You say "sorry, I'll fix that" — and three weeks later you send another 600-word update, because you never agreed what shorter means. | You say: "So five bullets rather than paragraphs, and only decisions and blockers? I'll do that for the next three and we can check at the end of the month whether it's working." Then you diarise the check. |
| Admitting a problem | You realise on Monday you can't hit Thursday's deadline, hope it resolves itself, and tell your manager Thursday afternoon. The client call is Friday and nothing can be moved. | You flag it Monday with two options — cut a section or move to Monday — and the manager picks one. Half the stress, and you've shown you track your own work. |
| Learning the culture | You accept every invitation and spend four weeks in meetings where you have no role, then discover you've missed the one recurring review where your work gets approved. | In week one you ask your buddy: "Which of these meetings do I actually need, and which one would hurt most if I missed it?" You attend those and decline the rest with a line asking for the notes. |

The pattern across all seven pairs: the version that works has a time attached to it — a weekly slot, a halfway-point check, a day-30 question — and the version that backfires happens whenever the impulse strikes.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if my employer doesn't assign me an onboarding buddy?+**

Appoint one informally. SHRM's 2022 figures showed the benefit rises sharply with contact — 56 percent of new hires who met a buddy once in 90 days said it helped them become productive, against 86 percent of those who met four to eight times — so the recurring slot matters more than the official title. Pick a colleague who has been there at least a year, ideally two, and ask in week one for a standing 20 minutes: "I'd rather save my questions up for you than interrupt the whole team." That is habit one, run by you instead of by HR.

**How do I ask for feedback without sounding anxious about my probation?+**

Use the narrow day-30 question from habit ten rather than "how am I doing?" Asking "what's one thing I should do differently in the next month?" gives your manager something small and specific to answer, and it reads as planning rather than reassurance-seeking. SHRM advises managers to check in with new employees in 30-day increments anyway, so you are usually just starting a conversation they already intended to have — and Talya Bauer's point that employees get roughly 90 days to prove themselves is a good reason not to wait until month three to find out what's missing.

**I'm remote. Do these habits still work?+**

They matter more, because nobody can see you struggling. The three that carry the most weight remotely are the recap message after every briefing (habit three), since you lose the corridor correction; the twice-weekly 20-minute intro calls (habit seven), since you won't meet anyone by accident; and the status line on your protected work blocks (habit five), which replaces the visible signal of someone sitting with headphones on. Keep your question list in whatever note tool you already use and paste it straight into the 1:1 invite so your buddy can prepare.
